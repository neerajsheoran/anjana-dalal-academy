## Objects and the Materials They Are Made Of

Pick up any object near you — a steel lunch box, a wooden pencil, a cotton handkerchief, a plastic water bottle. Each of these is an **object** — something we use for a purpose. But what gives each object its shape, strength, and behaviour? The answer lies in the **material** it is made from.

An **object** is a thing we can see, touch, and use. A **material** is the substance used to make that object.

The same material can be turned into many different objects. A single block of wood can become a cricket stump, a rolling pin (belan), or a door frame. At the same time, a single object — like a chair — can be made from different materials: wood, plastic, steel, or even bamboo. The material chosen depends on what properties are needed.

> **Try this:** Walk through your home and list five objects. For each one, write down what material it is made of. You will notice that most objects around us are made of just a handful of common materials — metal, wood, plastic, glass, cloth, rubber, and clay.

## Properties of Materials

Why do we pick one material over another? Because every material has a unique set of **properties** — measurable characteristics that tell us how it behaves. Understanding these properties helps us decide which material fits a particular job.

### Hardness and Softness

A material is **hard** if it resists being scratched or dented. The granite slab (pathar) used for a kitchen countertop is extremely hard — a steel knife cannot scratch it. Diamond, the hardest natural substance, is used to cut glass and polish other stones.

A **soft** material changes shape easily under pressure. The cotton filling inside a pillow compresses when you rest your head on it. Wax is soft enough to be moulded into decorative diyas for Diwali.

### Roughness and Smoothness

Run your fingers across a jute bag (bori) — you will feel a coarse, uneven surface. That is **roughness**. Now touch a stainless-steel plate — the surface feels even and flat. That is **smoothness**.

Rough surfaces provide better grip. Rubber chappals have a rough sole so your feet do not slip on wet floors. Smooth surfaces are easier to clean, which is why hospital walls are finished with glossy tiles.

### Lustre

**Lustre** is the natural shine a material shows when light falls on it. Freshly polished brass utensils — the lota or the thali in a pooja room — gleam brightly under light. Most metals (gold, silver, copper, aluminium) are **lustrous**.

Materials like chalk, cardboard, and unglazed clay pots appear dull. They are **non-lustrous**. Interestingly, metals lose their lustre over time if they corrode. An iron railing left in the rain develops a reddish-brown coat of rust and looks dull.

### Transparency, Translucency, and Opacity

Hold a clear glass tumbler up to a light bulb — you can see the bulb clearly through it. Glass is **transparent**; it lets light pass straight through.

Now hold a sheet of oiled paper (or butter paper) in front of the same bulb. You see a blurred glow but cannot make out the shape of the bulb. Oiled paper is **translucent**; it allows some light through but scatters it.

Finally, hold a steel thali in front of the bulb. No light gets through at all. Steel is **opaque**; it blocks light completely.

| Category | What it does to light | Everyday examples |
|---|---|---|
| Transparent | Lets all light through clearly | Window glass, clean water, cling film |
| Translucent | Lets some light through, blurs it | Frosted bathroom glass, thin cotton dupatta, greased paper |
| Opaque | Blocks all light | Wooden door, brick wall, aluminium foil |

### Solubility

Drop a spoonful of sugar into a glass of warm chai — it disappears as it **dissolves**. Sugar is **soluble** in water. Salt, baking soda, and lemon juice are also soluble.

Now drop a spoonful of turmeric (haldi) powder into water and stir. The water turns yellow, but fine particles settle at the bottom after some time. Turmeric is largely **insoluble**. Sand, iron filings, and marble chips also do not dissolve in water.

> **Kitchen experiment:** Take four glasses of water. Add a spoonful of salt to the first, sugar to the second, sand to the third, and mustard oil to the fourth. Stir each glass for one minute. Salt and sugar dissolve. Sand sinks. Oil floats as a separate layer. Each material behaves differently because of its solubility.

### Sinking and Floating

Some materials **sink** when placed in water while others **float**. A steel spoon sinks because steel is denser than water. A dry wooden block floats because wood is less dense than water. A cork bottle-stopper bobs on the surface, and so does a plastic ball.

This property matters in real life. Boats are designed with hollow shapes so that even though steel is heavy, the overall structure is light enough to float. Life jackets are filled with foam — a material that floats — to keep a person above water.

### Conductivity

**Thermal conductivity** describes how well a material transfers heat. Hold a steel spoon in a cup of hot dal — within seconds the handle feels warm. Steel conducts heat quickly. That is why cooking vessels are made from metals like steel, aluminium, and copper.

But the handles on those vessels are often wrapped in wood or plastic, which are **poor conductors** (also called **insulators**). They prevent heat from reaching your hand.

**Electrical conductivity** works the same way. Copper wires carry electricity from the power station to your home because copper is an excellent electrical conductor. The plastic coating around the wire is an insulator — it stops the current from reaching you.

## Grouping Materials by Their Origin

Materials can be sorted into two broad families based on where they come from.

### Natural Materials

These are found directly in nature — in plants, animals, or the earth. Humans harvest or extract them but do not create them from scratch.

- **From plants:** Cotton (for clothes and bandages), jute (for sacks and bags), wood (for furniture and fuel), rubber (from the latex of rubber trees), bamboo (for baskets and scaffolding)
- **From animals:** Silk (spun by silkworms), wool (sheared from sheep), leather (processed from animal hide), beeswax (from honeybee combs)
- **From the earth:** Iron ore, bauxite (source of aluminium), limestone, marble, clay, sand, coal, petroleum

### Synthetic (Human-Made) Materials

These are manufactured in factories, often by combining or chemically transforming natural raw materials.

- **Plastics** (polyethylene, PVC, polystyrene) — made from petroleum
- **Nylon and polyester** — synthetic fibres used in clothing, ropes, and bags
- **Cement and concrete** — made by processing limestone and clay
- **Glass** — made by heating sand (silica) with soda ash and limestone
- **Steel** — made by adding carbon to iron in a blast furnace

> Neither category is "better" than the other. Natural materials are renewable in many cases but may be limited. Synthetic materials can be designed for exact purposes but may be harder to dispose of.

## Choosing the Right Material for the Right Job

Every object around us exists because someone asked: "Which material has the properties this job demands?" Here are some everyday decisions explained through material properties.

| Object | Material chosen | Key properties that matter |
|---|---|---|
| School-bag body | Nylon or polyester fabric | Lightweight, water-resistant, strong, flexible |
| School-bag buckle | Moulded plastic or metal | Hard, rigid, durable |
| Window pane | Glass | Transparent (lets sunlight in), hard, smooth |
| Raincoat | PVC-coated fabric | Waterproof, flexible, lightweight |
| Electric plug pins | Brass or copper | Excellent electrical conductors |
| Electric plug body | Hard plastic (Bakelite) | Electrical insulator, heat-resistant |
| House walls | Brick, cement, concrete | Hard, opaque, strong, weather-resistant |
| House roof tiles | Clay or concrete | Waterproof, opaque, heat-resistant |
| Cooking tawa | Cast iron | Conducts heat evenly, extremely hard, durable |
| Tawa handle | Wood or Bakelite | Insulator (stays cool), easy to grip |
| Bicycle tyre | Rubber | Flexible, waterproof, provides grip on roads |
| Jewellery | Gold, silver | Lustrous, does not corrode, can be shaped into thin designs |

Notice how a single object — like an electric plug — uses two different materials because different parts need different properties. The pins must conduct electricity; the body must block it.

## Recycling and Caring for Our Materials

Every material we use comes from the earth in some form. Mining metals, cutting trees, and drilling for petroleum all affect the environment. **Recycling** — collecting used materials and reprocessing them into new products — reduces this impact.

**Materials that are commonly recycled:**
- **Paper and cardboard** — old newspapers and packaging boxes are turned into fresh paper, saving trees.
- **Glass** — broken glass bottles are melted and reshaped into new bottles or jars.
- **Metals** — aluminium cans, iron scrap, and copper wire are melted down and recast. Recycling aluminium uses roughly 95 percent less energy than producing it from raw ore.
- **Certain plastics** — PET bottles (the ones stamped with a "1" symbol) can be recycled into polyester fibre for clothing or carpets.

**Materials that are difficult to recycle:**
- Multi-layer chip packets (a sandwich of plastic and aluminium foil)
- Contaminated medical waste
- Thermoset plastics (like Bakelite) that cannot be melted and reshaped

The most effective approach follows three steps, in order of priority: **Reduce** (use less material in the first place), **Reuse** (use an item again instead of discarding it), and **Recycle** (send it for reprocessing). Carrying a cloth bag to the market, refilling a steel water bottle instead of buying a new plastic one, and donating old clothes instead of throwing them away are small actions that make a large difference over time.

## Key Terms at a Glance

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Object | A thing made for a particular use |
| Material | The substance from which an object is made |
| Hardness | A material's ability to resist scratching or denting |
| Lustre | The natural shine of a material's surface |
| Transparent | Allows light to pass through clearly |
| Translucent | Allows some light through but scatters it |
| Opaque | Does not let any light pass through |
| Soluble | Dissolves in a given liquid (usually water) |
| Insoluble | Does not dissolve in a given liquid |
| Conductor | A material that allows heat or electricity to pass through it easily |
| Insulator | A material that resists the flow of heat or electricity |
| Natural material | A material obtained directly from nature |
| Synthetic material | A material manufactured by humans |
| Recycling | Reprocessing used materials to make new products |
