## Measurement and Standard Units

**Child:** Maa, we are getting a new bookshelf for my room. How will the carpenter know what size to make it?

**Mother:** He will come to our house, take a measuring tape, and measure the wall where the shelf will go — the height, the width, and the depth. Then he will note those numbers down in centimetres or metres and build the shelf to match.

**Child:** But Maa, in olden times there were no measuring tapes, right? How did people measure things?

**Mother:** They used parts of their own body. Stretch your hand wide — the distance from the tip of your thumb to the tip of your little finger is called a handspan. People also used the cubit, which is the length from your elbow to the tip of your middle finger. And the foot — literally the length of a person's foot.

**Child:** That sounds easy enough. Why did people stop doing that?

**Mother:** Try this. Measure the width of your study table using your handspan, and I will do the same with mine.

**Child:** I get about nine handspans. You get... only seven? But the table is the same!

**Mother:** Exactly. My hand is bigger, so I get fewer spans. If I told a furniture maker "the table is seven handspans wide" and he used his own hand to build it, the new table could be completely different from ours. That is why people created **standard units** — units that are the same for everyone, everywhere.

**Child:** And the standard unit for length is the metre?

**Mother:** Yes. The metre is part of the **SI system** — the International System of Units. One metre is exactly the same whether you are in Lucknow, London, or Lagos. From the metre, we get smaller units — centimetres and millimetres — and a larger unit — the kilometre. Our bookshelf might be 180 cm tall. The distance from our home to the market is about 2 km. The thickness of your identity card is roughly 1 mm. Each unit fits a different scale of measurement.

## Measuring Length Correctly

**Child:** Maa, today in class I measured my pencil with a ruler and got 14 cm, but my friend measured the same pencil and said it was 14.3 cm. How can the same pencil give two different readings?

**Mother:** Let me ask you something. When you placed the pencil on the ruler, did you line one end up with the zero mark, or with the edge of the ruler?

**Child:** I think I used the edge of the ruler...

**Mother:** That could be the problem. On many rulers, there is a tiny gap between the physical edge and the zero line. If you start from the edge, your reading will be slightly off. This is sometimes called a **zero error**. Always start from the zero mark.

**Child:** Okay. But even when I use zero, sometimes I am not sure of the exact marking at the other end.

**Mother:** That is probably a **parallax error**. Were you looking at the ruler from directly above the pencil tip, or were you leaning to one side?

**Child:** I was leaning a bit because my friend was sitting close to me.

**Mother:** When your eyes are not directly above the point you are reading, the marking appears to shift. It is like how the needle on a car's speedometer looks like it points to a different number if you view it from the passenger seat instead of the driver's seat. To get the correct reading, your line of sight must be straight down — perpendicular to the ruler.

**Child:** What about measuring something round, like a cricket ball or a bangle? A ruler cannot bend around it.

**Mother:** Good thinking. For curved surfaces, you wrap a **thread** snugly along the curve, mark the point where the thread meets the starting point, then lay the thread straight against a ruler. Or you can use a **measuring tape**, which is flexible enough to bend around objects. When we bought curtains last month, the tailor used a measuring tape to go around the curtain rod — that gave the circumference directly.

## Types of Motion

**Child:** Maa, we went to the playground today, and I noticed something. The merry-go-round goes in circles, the swing goes back and forth, and when I ran across the field, I went in a straight line. Are those all different kinds of motion?

**Mother:** They are! Scientists sort motion into types based on the path the moving object follows. Let me explain each one.

**Child:** Start with the straight-line one — that is the simplest.

**Mother:** When an object moves along a straight path, it is called **rectilinear motion**. Think about a train on a perfectly straight stretch of railway track, or a ball rolling down a smooth, straight corridor. The path has no curves or turns.

**Child:** And the merry-go-round?

**Mother:** That is **circular motion**. The seats trace a circle around the central pole. At home, look at the ceiling fan — each blade tip moves in a circle around the centre. The second hand of the clock on your wall also traces a circle.

**Child:** The swing must be different because it does not go all the way around.

**Mother:** Correct. The swing moves forward and backward through the same arc, passing through a central resting point each time. That is **oscillatory motion** — or to-and-fro motion. The pendulum inside a wall clock does the same thing, ticking left and right over and over.

**Child:** Is oscillatory motion the same as periodic motion? Our teacher used both words.

**Mother:** They are related but not identical. **Periodic motion** is any motion that repeats after a fixed interval of time. Oscillatory motion is always periodic because the back-and-forth cycle repeats at regular intervals. But periodic motion is a bigger category. The fan's circular motion is also periodic — each full rotation takes the same amount of time — yet it is not oscillatory because the blades do not reverse direction.

**Child:** What about a spinning top? My friend brought a lattoo to school.

**Mother:** A spinning top demonstrates **rotational motion**. The top spins around an axis that runs through its own body. A potter's wheel works the same way — it rotates around a vertical axis.

**Child:** And what kind of motion does a butterfly have? It flies in such a zigzag way!

**Mother:** That is called **random motion** — movement that has no predictable pattern. A butterfly darts left, right, up, down, with no fixed path or rhythm. Dust particles floating in a sunbeam also drift randomly.

**Child:** Can an object have two types of motion at the same time?

**Mother:** Absolutely. When we drove on the highway last weekend, the tyres of our car were rotating around their axles — that is rotational motion — while the car itself moved forward in a straight line — rectilinear motion. Many everyday objects combine different types of motion.

## Speed — Connecting Distance and Time

**Child:** Maa, during our road trip to Nani's house, the highway sign said "Jaipur 240 km." We reached in about 4 hours. Was that fast or slow?

**Mother:** Well, think about it this way. You covered 240 kilometres in 4 hours. That means in every hour you covered roughly the same fraction of that distance. The idea of how much distance you cover in a given amount of time is called **speed**.

**Child:** So if we covered more distance in the same time, our speed would be higher?

**Mother:** Exactly. A bullock cart might cover only 5 kilometres in one hour — low speed. Our car covered much more — higher speed. An aeroplane covers hundreds of kilometres in an hour — very high speed. Speed connects two things: **distance** and **time**.

**Child:** On the highway, the car seemed to move at the same pace for a long time. But in the city, we kept stopping and starting.

**Mother:** That is a great observation. On the highway, when the car covers roughly equal distances in equal chunks of time, that is called **uniform motion**. In city traffic — with red lights, pedestrians, and turns — the car covers very different distances in each chunk of time. That is **non-uniform motion**.

**Child:** So almost nothing in real life moves with perfectly uniform motion?

**Mother:** Very few things do, but the concept is still useful. It gives us a simple starting point for understanding how objects move. As you learn more science, you will build on these ideas and study motion in much greater detail.
