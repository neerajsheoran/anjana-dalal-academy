## Measurement and Standard Units

When you visit a cloth shop, the shopkeeper pulls fabric along a wooden metre rod to measure exactly how much you need. But what if there were no metre rod? Centuries ago, people relied on body parts to estimate lengths. A **handspan** (the distance from the tip of your thumb to the tip of your little finger when your hand is fully stretched) was used for short lengths. A **cubit** (elbow to fingertip) helped measure longer objects. A **foot** (the length of a person's foot) served as another rough unit.

![Non-standard units of measurement — handspan, cubit, foot](non-standard-units.png)

The trouble with these **non-standard units** is obvious: your handspan is not the same as your teacher's. If a tailor in Jaipur measures cloth using his forearm and sends the measurement to a tailor in Kolkata, the second tailor's forearm will likely be a different length, and the garment will turn out the wrong size.

![The International System of Units — a universal standard](si-units.png)

To solve this problem, scientists around the world agreed on a single, universal system called the **International System of Units**, abbreviated as **SI** (from the French *Systeme International*). In this system, the **metre (m)** is the standard unit for measuring length. Because everyone — in India, in Brazil, in Japan — uses the same definition of a metre, measurements can be shared and compared without any confusion.

### Smaller and Larger Units of Length

Not everything is conveniently measured in metres. The thickness of a greeting card is far less than a metre, while the distance from Delhi to Mumbai is far more. That is why we use related units:

| Unit | Symbol | How it relates to the metre |
|------|--------|-----------------------------|
| Millimetre | mm | 1 m = 1000 mm |
| Centimetre | cm | 1 m = 100 cm |
| Metre | m | Base unit |
| Kilometre | km | 1 km = 1000 m |

- The **millimetre** is perfect for tiny measurements — the width of a pencil lead or the thickness of a plastic ruler.
- The **centimetre** works well for everyday objects — the length of your eraser or the width of your palm.
- The **metre** suits larger objects — the height of a door, the width of a classroom.
- The **kilometre** is used for long distances — highway signs reading "Agra 180 km" or "Chennai 350 km."

> **Quick conversion chain:** 1 km = 1000 m, 1 m = 100 cm, 1 cm = 10 mm. Every step is a factor of 10, making conversions straightforward.

## Measuring Length Correctly

The most common tool for measuring length in school is a **ruler** (also called a scale), marked in centimetres and millimetres. For longer measurements — say, the dimensions of a room or the height of a plant in the garden — a **measuring tape** is more practical because it is flexible and can wrap around curved surfaces.

### Steps for Accurate Measurement with a Ruler

![Steps for accurate measurement with a ruler](measuring-with-ruler.png)

1. Place the object flat alongside the ruler on an even surface.
2. Align one end of the object precisely with the **zero mark** on the ruler — not with the edge of the ruler, which sometimes starts before zero.
3. Look at the other end of the object and note which marking it lines up with.
4. Keep your eye **directly above** the point you are reading.

### Two Common Mistakes

- **Zero error:** Some students begin measuring from the edge of the ruler or from the "1 cm" mark instead of "0." This adds or subtracts length that does not belong to the object.
- **Parallax error:** If you tilt your head and look at the scale from an angle, the reading shifts. The correct reading is obtained only when your line of sight is **perpendicular** (at right angles) to the ruler at the point of measurement.

### Measuring Curved or Irregular Lengths

A rigid ruler cannot follow a curve. To measure a curved line — such as the boundary of a leaf or the seam of a cricket ball — place a **thread** carefully along the curve, mark where the curve ends on the thread, then straighten the thread against a ruler to read the length. A **measuring tape** can also bend around objects like a tree trunk to give the circumference directly.

## Types of Motion

When an object shifts its position over time, we say it is in **motion**. A parked auto-rickshaw on a street is **at rest** — its position does not change. The moment the driver starts it and moves forward, it is in motion.

Objects move in different patterns. Scientists classify these patterns into distinct **types of motion**.

### Rectilinear (Straight-Line) Motion

![Rectilinear motion — movement along a straight line](rectilinear-motion.png)

An object travels along a straight path without turning. Think of a train running on a straight stretch of track between two stations, or a bowling ball rolling down the lane toward the pins.

### Circular Motion

![Circular motion — object moving in a circle around a centre](circular-motion.png)

An object traces a circle around a fixed centre. The blades of a ceiling fan in your living room sweep around the central hub continuously. The tip of the seconds needle of a clock moves in a circle. The wheels of a moving bicycle also rotate in a circular path.

### Periodic and Oscillatory Motion

![Periodic and oscillatory motion — pendulum swinging back and forth](periodic-oscillatory-motion.png)

Any motion that **repeats itself after equal intervals of time** is called **periodic motion**. The Earth completing one orbit around the Sun every 365 days is periodic. The ringing of your school bell at fixed times during the day is periodic.

A special kind of periodic motion is **oscillatory (to-and-fro) motion**: the object swings back and forth about a central resting position. A child pumping a swing in the park moves forward and backward through the same arc repeatedly. The pendulum inside a grandfather clock swings left and right in a steady rhythm.

> All oscillatory motion is periodic, but not all periodic motion is oscillatory. The fan's circular motion is periodic (each full rotation takes the same time) yet not oscillatory.

### Rotational Motion

![Rotational motion — spinning around an axis](rotational-motion.png)

When an object spins around an axis that passes through it, the motion is **rotational**. A potter's wheel spinning on its own axis, or a top (lattoo) twirling on the ground — these are rotational motions. Notice how the axis itself may stay in one place while the body rotates around it.

### Random Motion

![Random motion — unpredictable movement with no fixed path](random-motion.png)

Sometimes an object moves without any predictable pattern. A butterfly fluttering through a garden changes direction constantly, with no fixed path or rhythm. Dust particles dancing in a beam of sunlight also exhibit **random motion**.

> **Combination of motions:** Many real-life objects show more than one type of motion at the same time. A bicycle wheel, for example, rotates around its axle (rotational) while the bicycle itself moves forward in a straight line (rectilinear).

## Speed — Connecting Distance and Time

![Speed — connecting distance and time](speed-distance-time.png)

Imagine two friends cycling from home to school. One reaches in 10 minutes, the other in 15 minutes, even though they travel the same road. The first friend was moving **faster** — covering more distance in less time. This idea of "how quickly something covers distance" is called **speed**.

At this level, the key idea is simple: **speed links distance with time**. A greater distance covered in the same amount of time means a higher speed. The same distance covered in more time means a lower speed.

- A bullock cart ambling through a village covers a short distance every minute — low speed.
- An express bus on a national highway covers a large distance every minute — high speed.
- A jet aircraft covers an enormous distance every minute — very high speed.

### Uniform and Non-Uniform Motion

![Uniform and non-uniform motion comparison](uniform-nonuniform-motion.png)

- **Uniform motion:** An object covers **equal distances in equal intervals** of time. A metro train gliding between two stations at a steady pace is close to uniform motion.
- **Non-uniform motion:** An object covers **unequal distances in equal intervals** of time. A scooter weaving through busy market lanes — stopping, starting, slowing, speeding — shows non-uniform motion.

## Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Non-standard unit | A measurement unit (handspan, cubit, foot) that varies from person to person |
| SI unit | A universally agreed-upon standard unit of measurement |
| Metre (m) | The SI unit of length |
| Parallax error | Incorrect reading caused by looking at a scale from an angle |
| Zero error | Mistake arising from not aligning the object with the true zero mark |
| Motion | A change in the position of an object over time |
| Rest | The state of an object whose position does not change |
| Rectilinear motion | Movement along a straight-line path |
| Circular motion | Movement along a circular path around a centre |
| Oscillatory motion | To-and-fro movement about a fixed central position |
| Periodic motion | Motion that repeats at regular time intervals |
| Rotational motion | Spinning of an object around an axis passing through it |
| Random motion | Unpredictable movement with no fixed path or pattern |
| Speed | How quickly an object covers distance over time |
| Uniform motion | Equal distances covered in equal time intervals |
| Non-uniform motion | Unequal distances covered in equal time intervals |
