## Playing with Fridge Magnets

**Child:** Maa, look! I took all the magnets off the fridge and stuck them together. Some of them snap together really fast, but when I flip one around, they keep sliding away from each other. Why is that happening?

**Mother:** You have just discovered one of the most important rules about magnets. Every magnet has two ends called poles — a North Pole and a South Pole. When you bring a North Pole near a South Pole, they attract each other and stick together. But when two North Poles or two South Poles face each other, they push apart. We say "like poles repel, unlike poles attract."

**Child:** Maa, but how do I know which end is north and which is south? These fridge magnets do not have any labels on them.

**Mother:** Good question. Here is a simple test. Tie a thread around the middle of a bar magnet and hang it so it can spin freely. After a few moments, it will stop and settle in a fixed direction. The end pointing toward geographic north is the North Pole. You can then use that known magnet to test your fridge magnets — whichever end of the fridge magnet gets attracted to your bar magnet's north pole must be the fridge magnet's south pole.

**Child:** That is like a detective trick for magnets!

**Mother:** Exactly. And here is something else to try. Take one of those fridge magnets and hold it near different things on the kitchen counter — a steel spoon, a wooden spatula, a glass bowl, a plastic lid. Notice which ones it sticks to and which ones it ignores.

**Child:** The spoon sticks! But the glass, the wood, and the plastic do not do anything. Why not?

**Mother:** Because only certain materials respond to magnets. Iron, steel, cobalt, and nickel are what we call magnetic materials. Everything else — wood, plastic, glass, rubber, copper, aluminium — these are non-magnetic materials. A magnet has no effect on them at all.

**Child:** Wait, Maa — copper and aluminium are metals, right? So not all metals are magnetic?

**Mother:** That is one of the biggest misconceptions people have. Being a metal does not automatically make something magnetic. Gold, silver, copper, and aluminium are all metals, but a magnet will not attract any of them.

## The Compass on a Family Trip

**Child:** Maa, remember when we went trekking near Manali last summer and my phone lost signal? You took out that small round thing with a needle inside and used it to find the trail. What was that?

**Mother:** That was a compass. It is one of the oldest navigation tools in the world. Inside the compass there is a thin, magnetised needle balanced on a tiny point so it can rotate freely. Because of the Earth's magnetic field, the needle always settles so that one end points toward geographic north.

**Child:** But Maa, why does it point north? Is there a giant magnet buried at the North Pole?

**Mother:** In a way, yes! The Earth itself behaves like a massive magnet. Deep inside, the planet has a core made of molten iron and nickel, and the movement of this liquid metal generates a magnetic field. The geographic North Pole of the Earth is actually near the Earth's magnetic south pole. Since unlike poles attract, the north-seeking end of the compass needle is pulled toward geographic north.

**Child:** That is confusing — the geographic north is a magnetic south?

**Mother:** It is a historical naming quirk, but the logic is simple. Just remember: the compass needle's north pole is attracted toward geographic north because that is where the Earth's magnetic south pole is located. Unlike poles attract — that is the same rule you discovered with the fridge magnets.

**Child:** Maa, did people in India use compasses long ago too?

**Mother:** Ancient Indian, Chinese, and Arab sailors all used magnetic rocks and needles to navigate across oceans. Before satellites and GPS existed, a compass was the most reliable direction-finding tool. Even today, hikers and mountaineers carry one as a backup in case electronic devices fail.

## Finding a Lost Needle

**Child:** Maa, I was helping Nani with sewing yesterday and I dropped a needle on the carpet. We searched for ten minutes and could not find it. Nani was worried someone would step on it.

**Mother:** Next time that happens, grab a magnet. Move it slowly just above the carpet, close to the surface. The steel needle will jump up and stick to the magnet right away.

**Child:** Because steel is a magnetic material!

**Mother:** Exactly. The magnet's force can reach through the thin carpet fibres because the carpet is made of non-magnetic cloth. The magnet ignores the cloth and attracts only the steel needle.

**Child:** Maa, can I make my own magnet? I want to magnetise a nail and use it to pick up things.

**Mother:** Sure! Take an iron nail and a bar magnet. Place the bar magnet's north pole at one end of the nail and slide it all the way to the other end — always in the same direction. Lift the magnet, bring it back to where you started, and repeat. Do this about 40 times. Then test the nail by bringing it close to some iron filings or pins.

**Child:** Will the pins stick to the nail?

**Mother:** If you stroked it enough times, yes. What you are doing is aligning the tiny magnetic regions inside the iron — these are called domains. When enough of them line up in the same direction, the whole nail behaves like a magnet.

**Child:** Can I also make a magnet with electricity? I read about electromagnets.

**Mother:** Yes. Wind some insulated copper wire around an iron nail — maybe 50 turns — and connect the wire ends to a battery. The nail will become a magnet as long as the current flows. Disconnect the battery and the magnetism disappears. That is the beauty of an electromagnet — you can switch it on and off.

**Child:** Where are electromagnets used in real life?

**Mother:** In many places. Giant electromagnets on cranes lift heavy iron and steel at scrapyards. MRI machines in hospitals use incredibly powerful electromagnets to take detailed pictures of the inside of your body. Electric bells, speakers, and motors all use electromagnets too.

## Magnets All Around Us

**Child:** Maa, I never realised magnets were in so many things! Where else are they hiding?

**Mother:** Open the fridge door and let it close on its own. Feel how it pulls shut at the end? There is a magnetic strip hidden inside the rubber seal all around the door. That strip keeps the door tightly closed so cold air does not escape.

**Child:** What about the earphones I use for music?

**Mother:** Inside each earphone there is a tiny permanent magnet and a coil of wire. When music signals flow through the coil as electric current, the coil interacts with the magnet and vibrates a thin disc. That vibration is what you hear as sound.

**Child:** And maglev trains — those float using magnets, right?

**Mother:** Exactly. Maglev stands for "magnetic levitation." Powerful magnets in the track and the train push against each other so the train actually hovers above the rails. With no contact, there is almost no friction, so these trains can reach very high speeds.

**Child:** Maa, one last thing — how should I take care of magnets so they stay strong?

**Mother:** Good question. Never drop a magnet on a hard floor or hit it with a hammer — that shakes up the aligned domains inside and weakens the magnet. Never heat a magnet either, because heat also scrambles the domains. And always keep magnets away from your phone, laptop, or memory cards — a strong magnetic field can damage the data stored on electronic devices.

**Child:** So basically, do not drop them, do not heat them, and keep them away from electronics?

**Mother:** Perfect summary. If you are storing bar magnets, place them in pairs with opposite poles facing each other and put a small piece of iron across the ends. This forms a closed loop for the magnetic field and helps the magnets stay strong for a long time.

**Child:** Thanks, Maa! I am going to go magnetise a nail right now.

**Mother:** Have fun — and count your strokes. Science rewards patience!
