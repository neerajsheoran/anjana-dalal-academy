## Playing with Fridge Magnets

**Meera:** Yaar, look what I figured out yesterday! I took all the colourful magnets off our fridge and tried sticking them together. Some snap together really fast, but when I flip one around, they slide away from each other. Why?

**Arjun:** Oh nice, you found one of the basic magnet rules. Every magnet has two ends called poles — a North Pole and a South Pole. When North meets South, they attract and stick. When two North ends or two South ends face each other, they push apart.

**Meera:** That sounds like a rule.

**Arjun:** It is THE rule. "Like poles repel, unlike poles attract." Teacher said it three times in class so we would not forget.

**Meera:** But how do I even know which end is north and which is south? The fridge magnets do not have any labels!

**Arjun:** There is a cool detective trick for that. Take a bar magnet, tie a thread around the middle, and let it hang freely. After a few minutes it will stop spinning and settle in one direction. The end pointing towards north — like the geographical north of the Earth — is the North Pole.

**Meera:** That is like the magnet is finding its own compass direction!

**Arjun:** Yes. Then once you know that bar magnet, you can use it to test your fridge magnets. Whichever end gets attracted to the bar's North Pole must be the fridge magnet's South Pole.

**Meera:** Detective work for magnets, yaar. I love it.

**Arjun:** Try one more thing. Take any fridge magnet and hold it near different things on the kitchen counter — a steel spoon, a wooden spatula, a glass bowl, a plastic dabba lid.

**Meera:** Let me guess in my head. The spoon will stick? The others maybe not?

**Arjun:** Try and see.

**Meera:** Spoon sticks! Glass, wood, plastic — nothing happens. Why?

**Arjun:** Because only certain materials react to magnets. Iron, steel, cobalt, and nickel are magnetic materials. Wood, plastic, glass, rubber, copper, aluminium — all non-magnetic.

**Meera:** Wait wait. Copper and aluminium are metals, na? So not all metals are magnetic?

**Arjun:** That is the biggest misunderstanding ever. Being a metal does not mean being magnetic. Gold, silver, copper, aluminium are all metals, but a magnet will not touch them.

## The Compass on a Family Trip

**Meera:** Arjun, last summer my family went trekking near Manali and my mobile lost signal. My dadu took out this small round thing with a needle inside and used it to find the trail. What was that?

**Arjun:** A compass! One of the oldest navigation tools ever. Inside there is a thin magnetised needle balanced on a tiny point so it can spin freely. The Earth's own magnetic field makes the needle settle so one end always points north.

**Meera:** But why does it point north? Is there a giant magnet buried at the North Pole or something?

**Arjun:** Kind of, actually! The whole Earth behaves like a huge magnet. Deep inside, the planet has a core of molten iron and nickel, and the movement of all that liquid metal makes a magnetic field. The geographic North Pole is actually near the Earth's magnetic SOUTH pole.

**Meera:** Wait, that is so confusing. North is south?

**Arjun:** I know, it is a naming mess from long ago. Just remember the logic — the needle's North end gets attracted toward the geographic North because the magnetic south is there. Unlike poles attract. Same rule you discovered with the fridge magnets.

**Meera:** Oh okay, it links back to the same rule. So in a way I already knew it!

**Arjun:** Yes! And by the way, ancient Indian, Chinese, and Arab sailors all used magnetic stones and needles to find their way across oceans, long before GPS was even imagined.

**Meera:** Even today my dadu carries one when he treks. Says you should never trust just your mobile in the mountains.

**Arjun:** Smart man. Phones die, batteries finish, signal vanishes. A compass needs no charging. Ever.

## Finding a Lost Needle

**Meera:** Arjun, I was helping Naani with her sewing yesterday and dropped a needle on the carpet. We searched for ten minutes and could not find it. Naani was worried someone would step on it barefoot.

**Arjun:** Next time grab a magnet! Move it slowly just above the carpet, close to the surface. The steel needle will jump up and stick.

**Meera:** Because steel is a magnetic material!

**Arjun:** Exactly. The magnet's force reaches right through the thin carpet fibres because the carpet is non-magnetic. The magnet ignores the cloth and grabs only the steel needle.

**Meera:** Oho, that is brilliant. I want to try it next time.

**Arjun:** Hey, here is something even cooler. Can I make my OWN magnet? Like, turn a regular nail into a magnet?

**Meera:** You can actually. Take an iron nail and a bar magnet. Put the bar magnet's north pole at one end of the nail and slide it all the way to the other end — always in the SAME direction. Lift the magnet, bring it back to where you started, repeat. Do this around 40 times.

**Arjun:** What happens after 40 strokes?

**Meera:** The nail itself becomes a magnet. Bring it near iron filings or pins and they will stick to it.

**Arjun:** How? Why does just stroking it work?

**Meera:** Inside the iron there are tiny magnetic regions called domains, all pointing in random directions. When you stroke with the magnet, you slowly line them all up in the same direction. Once enough of them are aligned, the whole nail behaves like a magnet.

**Arjun:** What about an electromagnet? I read about those.

**Meera:** Yes! Wind insulated copper wire around an iron nail — maybe 50 turns — and connect the wire ends to a battery. The nail becomes a magnet ONLY while the current flows. Cut the battery off and the magnetism vanishes.

**Arjun:** Whoa, like an on-off magnet.

**Meera:** That is what makes electromagnets so useful. Giant ones on cranes at scrapyards pick up heavy iron and steel. MRI machines in hospitals use super powerful electromagnets to take pictures of inside your body. Electric bells, speakers, motors — all use electromagnets.

## Magnets All Around Us

**Meera:** Yaar, I never realised magnets are in SO many things! Where else are they hiding?

**Arjun:** Try this — open your fridge door and let it close slowly. Feel how it pulls itself shut at the end? There is a magnetic strip hidden inside the rubber gasket. That is why no cold air escapes.

**Meera:** I never noticed that pull, but now you said it I can feel it in my head!

**Arjun:** And the earphones you use for music?

**Meera:** Tell me there are magnets in them too!

**Arjun:** Tiny permanent magnet plus a coil of wire inside each earpiece. When the music signal flows through the coil as electric current, the coil pushes and pulls against the magnet, vibrating a thin disc. That vibration becomes the sound you hear.

**Meera:** Music is literally a magnet shaking really fast?

**Arjun:** Basically yes! And maglev trains — those float using magnets, right?

**Meera:** Maglev is short for "magnetic levitation." Powerful magnets in the track and the train push against each other so the train hovers above the rails. No contact, no friction, super high speeds.

**Arjun:** That is from a future movie, yaar.

**Meera:** And we have not even talked about doorbells, fans, motors, microphones — they all use magnets too.

**Arjun:** Okay one last thing — how do we take care of magnets so they stay strong?

**Meera:** Never drop them on a hard floor or hit them with a hammer — that shakes up the aligned domains inside and weakens the magnet. Never heat them either. And keep them AWAY from your phone, laptop, or memory cards. A strong magnetic field can damage the data stored on them.

**Arjun:** So basically — do not drop, do not heat, keep away from electronics.

**Meera:** Perfect summary. And if you store bar magnets, place them in pairs with opposite poles facing each other, and put a small piece of iron across the ends. This forms a closed loop for the magnetic field and helps them stay strong for a long time.

**Arjun:** Yaar, I am going to go magnetise a nail right now.

**Meera:** Have fun! Count your strokes — patience matters with magnets.
