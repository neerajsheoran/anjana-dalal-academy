## The Story Behind Magnets

![Magnetite (lodestone) attracting small iron pieces](story-behind-magnets.png)

Thousands of years ago, people in ancient Greece noticed something strange about certain rocks near a place called **Magnesia**. These rocks could pull small pieces of iron toward themselves without anyone touching them. The Greeks named these rocks **magnetite**, and the special pulling ability came to be known as magnetism. Another name for these naturally magnetic rocks is **lodestone**.

A popular legend says that a shepherd named Magnes discovered this when the iron nails in his shoes got stuck to a rock on a hillside. Whether or not the story is true, it tells us that humans have been fascinated by magnets for a very long time.

Today, magnets are everywhere around us. The seal on your refrigerator door, the clasp of a magnetic pencil box, the tiny speaker inside your earphones — all of these rely on magnetism.

## What Magnets Can Do — Properties of Magnets

A **magnet** is any object that produces a magnetic force. Here are the key properties that every magnet shows:

### 1. Magnets attract certain materials

![Magnets attract iron, steel, cobalt, and nickel](magnets-attract-materials.png)

Bring a magnet close to an iron safety pin, a steel spoon, or a nickel coin — the magnet pulls them. These metals (iron, steel, cobalt, and nickel) respond to magnetic force.

### 2. Every magnet has two poles

![Every magnet has a North Pole and a South Pole](magnet-two-poles.png)

The two ends of a magnet are called **poles**. One end is the **North Pole (N)** and the other is the **South Pole (S)**. The magnetic force is strongest at the poles and weakest in the middle.

### 3. Like poles repel, unlike poles attract

![Like poles repel, unlike poles attract](poles-repel-attract.png)

This is the golden rule of magnetism:

| Combination | Result |
|-------------|--------|
| North + North | Repel (push apart) |
| South + South | Repel (push apart) |
| North + South | Attract (pull together) |

> **Try this at home:** Take two small magnets from your fridge. Bring them together one way and feel them snap together. Now flip one magnet around and try again — they will resist and push each other away. You have just demonstrated the rule of poles!

### 4. A freely hanging magnet points north-south

![A freely hanging magnet always points north-south](magnet-north-south.png)

If you tie a bar magnet to a thread and let it hang without touching anything, it will slowly spin and settle so that one end points toward geographic north and the other toward south. This is the principle behind every compass.

### Shapes of magnets

![Different shapes of magnets — bar, horseshoe, disc, ring, and cylindrical](shapes-of-magnets.png)

Magnets are manufactured in many shapes depending on their use:

- **Bar magnet** — rectangular block, commonly used in school experiments
- **Horseshoe magnet** — U-shaped, gives a strong pull between its two arms
- **Disc magnet** — flat and round, found inside speakers and headphones
- **Ring magnet** — doughnut-shaped, used in science projects
- **Cylindrical magnet** — rod-shaped, found inside motors

## Magnetic and Non-Magnetic Materials

Not everything sticks to a magnet. Materials that are pulled toward a magnet are called **magnetic materials**. Those that are not affected at all are called **non-magnetic materials**.

| Magnetic materials | Non-magnetic materials |
|--------------------|------------------------|
| Iron nail | Wooden ruler |
| Steel key | Plastic pen cap |
| Cobalt strip | Glass marble |
| Nickel coin | Rubber band |
| | Copper wire |
| | Aluminium foil |
| | Cotton cloth |

> **Common mistake:** Many students think all metals are magnetic. That is not true. Copper, aluminium, gold, and silver are metals, but a magnet has no effect on them.

### A useful trick — finding a lost needle

Have you ever dropped a sewing needle on the floor and struggled to find it? Run a magnet slowly over the area. The steel needle will jump up and cling to the magnet. This works because steel is a magnetic material.

## Finding the Poles and Using a Compass

### How to find the poles of a magnet

If someone gives you a magnet without any markings, how would you figure out which end is north and which is south?

1. Tie the magnet with a thread at its centre so it hangs level and can swing freely.
2. Wait for it to stop spinning. One end will point toward geographic north — that end is the **North Pole**.
3. The opposite end is the **South Pole**.

### The magnetic compass

![The magnetic compass](magnetic-compass.png)

A **compass** is a small device with a lightweight magnetic needle balanced on a tiny pivot. Because the needle can rotate freely, it always settles along the north-south direction.

Travellers, hikers, and sailors have used compasses for centuries to find their way. Even today, when you open a maps application on a phone, the digital compass inside the phone uses a magnetic sensor to show which direction you are facing.

### Why does a compass work? — Earth as a giant magnet

![Earth acts as a giant magnet with a magnetic field](earth-giant-magnet.png)

The Earth itself behaves like an enormous magnet. Deep below the surface, the planet's molten iron core generates a magnetic field that stretches far into space.

The geographic North Pole of the Earth is close to the Earth's magnetic south pole. That is why the north-seeking end of a compass needle (which is really a tiny magnet) is attracted toward geographic north — because unlike poles attract.

> **On a family trip:** If you are ever trekking in the hills and lose mobile signal, a simple compass can guide you. Place it flat on your palm, wait for the needle to settle, and the marked end will point north. From there you can work out east, west, and south.

## Making Your Own Magnet

You can turn an ordinary iron or steel object into a magnet. This process is called **magnetisation**.

### Method 1 — Single-direction stroking

![Making a magnet by single-direction stroking](single-direction-stroking.png)

1. Hold a bar magnet in one hand and an iron nail in the other.
2. Place one pole of the magnet at one end of the nail.
3. Slide the magnet along the nail in **one direction only** — from head to tip.
4. Lift the magnet away, bring it back to the starting end, and stroke again.
5. Repeat at least 30–40 times.
6. Test the nail by bringing it near some iron filings or small pins. If they stick, your nail has become a magnet!

The stroking aligns the tiny magnetic regions (called **domains**) inside the iron, turning the whole nail into a magnet.

### Method 2 — Using electric current (electromagnet)

![Making an electromagnet using electric current](electromagnet.png)

1. Wind a length of insulated copper wire (about 50 turns) around an iron nail.
2. Connect the two ends of the wire to a battery.
3. The nail now acts as a magnet — it can pick up pins and paper clips.
4. Disconnect the battery and the magnetism disappears.

This is called an **electromagnet**. Unlike a permanent magnet, an electromagnet can be switched on and off, which makes it extremely useful in machines.

## Where Magnets Are Used Every Day

Magnets play a role in a surprising number of devices and technologies:

| Application | How magnetism helps |
|-------------|---------------------|
| Refrigerator door seal | A flexible magnetic strip inside the rubber gasket holds the door shut, keeping cold air in |
| Speakers and earphones | A permanent magnet and a coil work together to vibrate a cone, producing sound |
| Electric motors and fans | Magnets and electric current create the spinning motion that drives the motor |
| MRI scanners in hospitals | Extremely powerful magnets create detailed images of organs and tissues inside the body |
| Maglev trains | Strong magnets lift the train above the track, removing friction so it can travel at very high speeds |
| Magnetic compass | A magnetised needle shows direction for travellers |
| Cranes at scrapyards | Large electromagnets pick up and move heavy loads of iron and steel |
| Magnetic stickers and boards | Small magnets hold papers, notes, and decorations on metal surfaces |

## Taking Care of Your Magnets

Magnets can lose their strength if they are not handled properly. Here are some guidelines:

- **Store bar magnets in pairs** with a piece of wood between them, and place small iron pieces (called keepers) across the ends to complete the magnetic circuit.
- **Do not drop or hammer** a magnet — the shock can jumble the internal magnetic domains and weaken the magnet.
- **Do not heat** a magnet — high temperatures destroy the alignment of domains.
- **Keep magnets away from electronic devices** such as phones, laptops, and memory cards. The magnetic field can damage stored data or interfere with sensors.
- **Do not bring magnets near a mechanical watch** — the magnetic field can affect the metal parts inside and make the watch run incorrectly.

## Key Terms at a Glance

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Magnet | An object that attracts iron, nickel, cobalt, and steel |
| Magnetite / Lodestone | A naturally occurring magnetic rock |
| Poles | The two ends of a magnet where the force is strongest (North and South) |
| Magnetic material | A substance that is attracted by a magnet (e.g., iron, steel) |
| Non-magnetic material | A substance that is not attracted by a magnet (e.g., wood, plastic) |
| Compass | A device with a free-spinning magnetic needle used to find direction |
| Magnetisation | The process of turning a non-magnetic iron object into a magnet |
| Electromagnet | A temporary magnet created by passing electric current through a wire coil around an iron core |
| Magnetic field | The invisible region around a magnet where its force can be felt |
| Demagnetisation | Loss of magnetism due to heating, dropping, or hammering |
