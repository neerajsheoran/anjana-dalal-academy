## What Sets the Living Apart from the Non-Living?

Picture your neighbourhood on a busy morning. A sparrow hops along a wall, pecking at seeds. The tulsi plant on your balcony stretches its leaves toward the sunlight. Meanwhile, the clay pot beside it has not changed in years — it neither grows nor moves.

What is the secret difference? How can we confidently say the sparrow and the tulsi are **alive**, while the pot is not? Biologists have identified a set of traits that every living thing possesses — the **characteristics of life**. When an entity displays all of these traits, we call it a **living organism**.

> **Central thought:** A living organism takes in nourishment, harnesses energy, grows, senses its surroundings, removes waste, moves in some fashion, and brings forth new life.

---

## The Seven Hallmarks of Life

Every organism on our planet — from the microscopic bacteria in curd to the majestic Asian elephant — shares these seven traits:

1. Movement
2. Nutrition (obtaining food or energy)
3. Respiration (releasing energy from food)
4. Growth
5. Excretion (removing waste)
6. Sensitivity (responding to the environment)
7. Reproduction (producing young ones)

Let us look at each one closely.

---

## 1 — Movement

**Every living thing moves**, though the style of movement varies enormously.

### How Animals Move

Animals shift their entire body from one spot to another — this is called **locomotion**. A parrot flies, a fish glides through water, a monkey swings between branches, a snake weaves across the ground.

*Why do animals need to move?*
- To search for food and water
- To flee from danger
- To seek a mate
- To find a suitable home

### How Plants Move

Plants are anchored by roots, so they cannot walk. Yet they are far from motionless. Plants exhibit **growth-based movements** and **rapid responses**.

*Everyday observations:*
- **Sunflower heads** track the Sun from east to west during the day — a phenomenon called **phototropism**.
- **Chhui-mui (Mimosa pudica / Touch-me-not / लाजवंती)** — brush its leaflets lightly and they snap shut within seconds. This is a **thigmonastic response** (response to touch).
- Roots push downward, drawn by gravity, while shoots climb upward, drawn by light.
- Pea plant tendrils coil tightly around a nearby stick or wire for support.

> *Try this at home:* Place a potted bean seedling near a window. After a few days, notice how the stem bends toward the window light. Turn the pot around. In another few days, the stem bends toward the light again! This shows the plant's movement response to light.

**A handy comparison:**

| Feature | Animals | Plants |
| ------- | ------- | ------ |
| Kind of movement | Locomotion — the whole body travels | Parts of the plant move (leaves fold, stems bend, roots grow downward) |

## 2 — Nutrition

All living beings require **energy** to stay alive. This energy comes from food. The method by which an organism obtains food is called **nutrition**.

### Two Fundamental Strategies

**a) Autotrophs ("self-nourishing") — they manufacture their own food**

Green plants, certain algae, and some bacteria capture sunlight and use it to combine water and carbon dioxide into glucose (a sugar). This remarkable process is **photosynthesis**. The green pigment **chlorophyll**, found in leaves, makes it possible.

> Think of plants as nature's kitchens — they cook food using sunlight as fuel, water from the soil, and carbon dioxide from the air.

**b) Heterotrophs ("nourished by others") — they consume ready-made food**

Animals, fungi, and most bacteria cannot photosynthesise. They must eat plants or other animals.

*Categories of heterotrophs:*
- **Herbivores** — feed on plants only (buffalo, parrot, caterpillar)
- **Carnivores** — feed on animals only (leopard, hawk, frog)
- **Omnivores** — feed on both (humans, crow, monkey)
- **Decomposers** — feed on dead and decaying matter (fungi, bacteria)

*Familiar Indian scenarios:*
- A buffalo munching on hay in a village — herbivore.
- A hawk swooping down to catch a mouse — carnivore.
- You relishing chole-bhature followed by a bowl of raita — omnivore!

## 3 — Respiration

Once food is inside the body, organisms must **unlock the energy** stored in it. This energy-releasing process is called **respiration**.

> **Common confusion:** Breathing and respiration are not the same thing. Breathing is the physical act of drawing air in and pushing it out. Respiration is the chemical breakdown of glucose inside cells to release energy.

### Aerobic Respiration (using oxygen)

Most organisms use oxygen to break down glucose:

**Glucose + Oxygen → Carbon dioxide + Water + Energy**

This reaction occurs inside every living cell, continuously.

### Anaerobic Respiration (without oxygen)

Certain organisms can release energy even in the absence of oxygen:
- **Yeast** ferments sugar without oxygen, producing **alcohol and carbon dioxide**. This is the science behind fluffy idlis, soft dosas, and risen bread dough — the CO₂ bubbles make them airy.
- During an intense cricket match or a long run, your leg muscles may not get oxygen fast enough. They switch briefly to anaerobic respiration, producing **lactic acid** — the culprit behind that painful cramp!

### Breathing Organs Across the Animal Kingdom

| Organism | Breathing organ |
| -------- | --------------- |
| Humans, cows, dogs | Lungs |
| Fish, sharks | Gills |
| Tadpoles (young frogs) | Gills and skin; adult frogs use lungs |
| Grasshoppers, beetles | Tiny openings called **spiracles** |
| Plants | Microscopic pores on leaves called **stomata** |
| Earthworms, leeches | Moist skin surface |

> *Think about it:* After climbing four flights of stairs, you pant heavily. Why? Your muscles demand extra energy, so respiration speeds up, and your body needs more oxygen and must expel more carbon dioxide — hence the rapid breathing.

## 4 — Growth

**All living organisms grow.** A non-living object like a stone does not add material to itself from within.

A tulsi seed sprouts into a small herb that eventually becomes a bushy plant. A human baby weighing around 3 kg grows into an adult weighing 50–70 kg. A kitten turns into a full-grown cat.

*Indian observations:*
- A coconut seedling planted in Kerala takes years to grow into a towering palm that produces coconuts for decades.
- A newborn Indian elephant calf weighs roughly 90–100 kg; by adulthood it can weigh over 4,500 kg!

### Growth: Plants vs Animals

| Aspect | Plants | Animals |
| ------ | ------ | ------- |
| Duration of growth | Grow throughout their life at special points called **meristems** (tips of roots and shoots) | Grow mainly during childhood and adolescence; growth slows or stops in adulthood |

### Lifespan — How Long Do Organisms Live?

Every species has an expected **lifespan** — the period from birth to natural death.

| Organism | Typical Lifespan |
| -------- | ---------------- |
| Mayfly | 24 hours (one of the shortest!) |
| Housefly | About 25 days |
| Hen | 6–8 years |
| Dog | 10–14 years |
| Indian elephant | 55–70 years |
| Giant tortoise | 150–250 years |
| Peepal tree | Several hundred years |
| Bristlecone pine | Over 5,000 years |

> The great **peepal tree** at Bodh Gaya, under which Gautama Buddha is said to have attained enlightenment, is revered as a descendant of the original tree that stood there more than 2,500 years ago. Trees can outlive countless human generations!

## 5 — Excretion

Life processes generate waste substances that can be toxic if they accumulate. The removal of these wastes is called **excretion**.

*In humans:*
- **Kidneys** filter blood and produce **urine** (containing urea, excess salts, and water).
- **Lungs** expel **carbon dioxide** produced during respiration.
- **Skin** releases salt and water as **sweat** — especially noticeable on a hot summer day in Delhi!
- **Liver** breaks down and neutralises many harmful chemicals.

*In plants:*
- Plants release **oxygen** through stomata — a by-product of photosynthesis.
- Many plants shed their **leaves** periodically, carrying stored waste products with them.
- Certain trees exude waste in the form of **gums, resins, or latex** (like the rubber tree).

*In other organisms:*
- Fish excrete waste through gills and kidneys.
- Birds produce **uric acid** — a nearly solid white paste — instead of liquid urine. This clever adaptation conserves water, which is vital during long flights.

> **Fun fact:** Have you noticed white splashes on a car parked under a tree? That white substance in bird droppings is **uric acid** — the bird's way of getting rid of nitrogenous waste while saving precious water.

## 6 — Sensitivity — Responding to the Environment

Living organisms detect changes around them and **react** accordingly. Any environmental change that triggers a reaction is called a **stimulus** (plural: stimuli).

*Stimulus–response examples from daily life:*
- **Bright sunlight → your pupils** shrink to protect the retina.
- **Touch → chhui-mui leaves** fold inward instantly.
- **Sound of a horn → a stray dog** jumps to the side of the road.
- **Cold weather → you** shiver, which generates body heat.
- **Aroma of biryani → you** start salivating even before tasting a morsel.
- **Gravity → plant roots** grow downward.

### Jagadish Chandra Bose and the Secret Life of Plants

For centuries, people assumed only animals could feel and respond. An extraordinary Indian scientist shattered that belief.

**Jagadish Chandra Bose (1858–1937)**, a Bengali polymath, designed an ultra-sensitive device called the **crescograph**. It could magnify the tiniest plant movements thousands of times — movements far too subtle for the naked eye.

His ground-breaking discoveries:
- Plants **react to touch, heat, cold, and chemical irritants** in ways strikingly similar to animals.
- When a plant stem is wounded — say by a cut or a flame — the cells at the injured spot generate an **electrical signal** that travels through the plant, much like a nerve impulse in an animal.
- Even outwardly calm plants are constantly sensing and responding to light, moisture, and temperature.

> **Bose's legacy:** He proved that the line between plant life and animal life is thinner than anyone had imagined. He also pioneered research in **radio and microwave waves**, demonstrating wireless signalling years before Guglielmo Marconi's famous radio experiments. A true genius across multiple disciplines.

## 7 — Reproduction

No individual organism lives forever. For a species to persist on Earth, organisms must produce **new individuals** of their kind. This is **reproduction**.

### Two Pathways of Reproduction

**a) Asexual reproduction** — a single parent produces offspring identical to itself

*Examples:*
- **Amoeba** divides into two equal halves (binary fission).
- **Yeast** sprouts small buds that grow into new yeast cells (budding).
- **Hydra** develops buds on its body that detach and become independent organisms.
- **Plants** can be propagated from cuttings (gulab / rose, chameli / jasmine), leaves (money plant), or underground stems (ginger, haldi / turmeric).

**b) Sexual reproduction** — two parents (male and female) contribute; offspring show a blend of both parents' traits

*Examples:*
- Humans, dogs, parrots, fish — most animals reproduce sexually.
- Flowering plants — pollen from the male part of one flower fertilises the egg in the female part → a seed forms → the seed grows into a new plant.

*Everyday Indian examples:*
- When you plant a mango seed (guthli) and a tree eventually grows — that is sexual reproduction, since the seed came from fertilisation.
- When your grandmother breaks off a piece of a pudina (mint) stem and plants it in a pot, and a new mint plant sprouts — that is asexual reproduction.
- The "eyes" (buds) on a potato can each give rise to a new potato plant — also asexual reproduction.

## The Curious Case of Viruses

Are viruses living or non-living? This puzzle fascinates scientists even today.

**Viruses** (such as those causing dengue, chickenpox, or the common cold):
- **Can reproduce** — but only by hijacking a living host cell. On their own, they are inert.
- **Cannot** carry out nutrition, respiration, or excretion independently.
- Are **not made of cells**.

Viruses occupy a strange twilight zone between the living and the non-living. Most biologists do not classify them as true living organisms, yet they are not entirely non-living either.

## Cells — The Fundamental Building Blocks

If you could zoom in on any living thing with a powerful microscope, you would eventually see its smallest functional unit — the **cell**.

> **All living organisms are built from cells.** The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.

Some organisms consist of just **one cell** — they are called **unicellular** organisms (e.g., amoeba, bacteria, paramecium).

Most organisms we see around us — trees, insects, fish, humans — are composed of **millions or billions of cells**. They are **multicellular** organisms.

| Unicellular organisms | Multicellular organisms |
| --------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Just 1 cell | Millions to trillions of cells |
| Amoeba, bacterium, yeast | Human beings, banyan tree, goldfish, mushroom |
| One cell handles every life function | Different cells specialise in different tasks |

### From Cells to a Complete Organism

In multicellular organisms, cells are organised in layers of increasing complexity:

**Cell → Tissue → Organ → Organ System → Organism**

*Example using the human digestive system:*
- **Epithelial cells** line the inner wall of the stomach → together they form **epithelial tissue**
- Epithelial tissue + muscle tissue + glandular tissue → **stomach** (an organ)
- Stomach + oesophagus + intestines + liver + pancreas → **digestive system** (organ system)
- All organ systems working in harmony → **you** (the organism)

*Example in a plant:*
- **Photosynthetic cells** → **leaf tissue** → **leaf** (organ) → **shoot system** → **the whole plant**

> Think of it like building a house: bricks (cells) form walls (tissues), walls form rooms (organs), rooms form floors (organ systems), and all floors together make the complete building (organism).

## Quick Recap

| Characteristic | What it means |
| -------------- | ------------- |
| **Movement** | Living things move — animals travel from place to place; plants show bending, folding, and growing movements |
| **Nutrition** | Living things need food/energy — autotrophs make food (plants); heterotrophs consume food (animals) |
| **Respiration** | Living things release energy from food, mostly using oxygen |
| **Growth** | Living things increase in size and complexity over time |
| **Excretion** | Living things remove harmful waste products |
| **Sensitivity** | Living things detect and respond to changes in their surroundings |
| **Reproduction** | Living things produce new individuals — asexually (one parent) or sexually (two parents) |

## Important Terms

| Term | Meaning |
| ---- | ------- |
| **Stimulus** | An environmental change that triggers a response in a living organism |
| **Photosynthesis** | The process by which green plants manufacture food using sunlight, water, and CO₂ |
| **Autotroph** | An organism that produces its own food (e.g., plants, algae) |
| **Heterotroph** | An organism that depends on other organisms for food (e.g., animals, fungi) |
| **Respiration** | The chemical breakdown of glucose within cells to liberate energy |
| **Excretion** | The elimination of metabolic waste from the body |
| **Locomotion** | Movement of the entire body from one location to another |
| **Reproduction** | The biological process of generating offspring |
| **Unicellular** | Composed of a single cell (e.g., amoeba, bacteria) |
| **Multicellular** | Composed of numerous cells (e.g., humans, trees) |
| **Cell** | The smallest unit of life — the fundamental building block of all organisms |
| **Crescograph** | A sensitive instrument devised by J.C. Bose to detect minute plant movements |
| **Lifespan** | The duration of an organism's life from birth to natural death |
