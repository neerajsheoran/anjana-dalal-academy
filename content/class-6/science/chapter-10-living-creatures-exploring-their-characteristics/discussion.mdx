## What Makes Something Alive?

**Child:** Maa, I was watching ants carrying crumbs on our kitchen floor. They move, they eat, they seem to know where they are going. But my toy car also moves and my balloon also gets bigger when I blow air into it. So how do we know what is really alive?

**Mother:** That is a wonderful question! Scientists have identified seven traits that every living thing must show — movement, nutrition, respiration, growth, excretion, sensitivity to surroundings, and reproduction. Something is considered alive only when it displays all seven together.

**Child:** My balloon grows bigger, but it does not eat or breathe on its own. And my toy car moves, but it cannot grow or have baby toy cars!

**Mother:** Exactly, beta. Now think about the tulsi plant on our balcony. It may seem quiet, but it absorbs water and minerals through its roots, makes food using sunlight, grows taller each month, releases oxygen, responds to light by bending toward it, and produces seeds that can grow into new tulsi plants. It quietly does all seven things!

**Child:** So even though the tulsi does not run around, it is just as alive as our dog Bruno?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Bruno runs, barks, eats his roti, breathes, grows, removes waste, reacts when you call his name, and can have puppies. Both Bruno and the tulsi are fully alive — they just carry out life processes in their own unique ways.

## Autotrophs, Heterotrophs, and the Food Connection

**Child:** Maa, you always say "eat your sabzi — plants are important." But why are plants so special?

**Mother:** Because plants are the original food-makers of our world. They capture sunlight using the green pigment chlorophyll in their leaves and combine carbon dioxide from the air with water from the soil to produce glucose — a kind of sugar that stores energy. This whole process is called photosynthesis.

**Child:** So plants are like little solar-powered factories?

**Mother:** Exactly! They are called **autotrophs** — self-nourishing organisms. Every animal, including us, depends on them. We are **heterotrophs** — we must eat other organisms because we cannot photosynthesise.

**Child:** Then when I eat a paratha with aloo, I am eating wheat from a plant and potato from a plant — I am a heterotroph eating autotrophs!

**Mother:** Yes! And if you add some chicken curry, you are eating an animal that itself ate grains — so even that energy originally came from plants. Herbivores like our neighbour Sharma ji's cow eat grass directly. Carnivores like the owl that sits on our neem tree hunt mice. Omnivores like us eat both plants and animals. And decomposers like the tiny mushrooms that pop up in our garden during monsoon break down dead leaves and return nutrients to the soil.

**Child:** So every meal I eat connects back to a plant and to the Sun. That is amazing, Maa!

**Mother:** It is, beta. That is why protecting our forests and farmland matters so much — they are the foundation of all food on Earth.

## Respiration — The Hidden Energy Machine

**Child:** Maa, my teacher said respiration is not the same as breathing. But I always thought they were the same!

**Mother:** Many people confuse them. Breathing is the mechanical act — your chest rises and falls, air flows in through your nose and out again. But respiration is the chemical reaction happening inside each of your cells, where glucose is broken down using oxygen to release energy that your body actually uses.

**Child:** So breathing just delivers the oxygen, and the real work happens inside the cells?

**Mother:** Precisely. The equation is: glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide, water, and energy. This happens in every single cell — your brain cells, your heart cells, your skin cells — nonstop, day and night.

**Child:** What about when I ran the 200-metre race at school Sports Day and my legs started cramping badly?

**Mother:** Your leg muscles were working so hard that they used up oxygen faster than your lungs could supply it. So your muscle cells switched to **anaerobic respiration** — breaking down glucose without oxygen. This produces **lactic acid** instead of carbon dioxide. That lactic acid buildup is what made your legs burn and cramp. Once you stopped and caught your breath, oxygen cleared away the lactic acid.

**Child:** And Maa, you told me that the dosa batter rises overnight because of yeast. Is that also anaerobic respiration?

**Mother:** Very good memory! Yes, the yeast in the batter ferments sugar without oxygen, producing carbon dioxide gas and a tiny bit of alcohol. The CO₂ bubbles get trapped in the batter and puff it up — that is why our dosas are crispy and our idlis are soft and fluffy. The alcohol evaporates during cooking.

## J.C. Bose — The Scientist Who Listened to Plants

**Child:** Maa, our chapter mentions Jagadish Chandra Bose. Did he really prove that plants can feel things?

**Mother:** He did something extraordinary. In the late 1800s and early 1900s, almost every scientist believed that plants were passive — they could not sense or respond to anything. Bose refused to accept that. He built a delicate instrument called the **crescograph** that could magnify tiny plant movements by thousands of times.

**Child:** What did the crescograph show?

**Mother:** It revealed that when a plant is touched, heated, or even cut, the cells at the affected spot generate an electrical signal — similar to nerve impulses in animals. The signal travels through the plant, and the plant responds. The chhui-mui plant folding its leaves is the most dramatic example, but Bose showed that even plants that seem perfectly still — like a fern or a rose — are constantly sensing temperature, light, and moisture.

**Child:** So plants are quietly aware of their surroundings, just like animals?

**Mother:** In their own way, yes. Bose said that the wall between plant life and animal life is much thinner than we imagined. And here is something even more impressive — Bose also did pioneering work on **wireless radio waves**. He demonstrated electromagnetic wave transmission years before Marconi became famous for it. Yet he never patented his inventions because he believed scientific knowledge should be shared freely.

**Child:** He sounds like a true hero of Indian science, Maa.

**Mother:** He absolutely was. Next time you see a plant, remember — it is quietly sensing the world around it, just as Bose showed us over a century ago.

## Cells — How Life Is Organised

**Child:** Maa, if I keep zooming in on a leaf with a very powerful microscope, what will I see?

**Mother:** You will eventually see the leaf's individual **cells** — tiny compartments, each one carrying out life processes. The cell is the smallest unit that is still alive. Every living thing is made of cells.

**Child:** Are all organisms made of the same number of cells?

**Mother:** Not at all! An amoeba, which lives in pond water, is just **one cell** — it is called a **unicellular** organism. That single cell eats, breathes, grows, excretes, and reproduces, all by itself. On the other hand, your body contains about 37 lakh crore cells — 37 trillion — each specialised for a different job. You are a **multicellular** organism.

**Child:** That is mind-boggling! But how do trillions of cells work together without chaos?

**Mother:** Through beautiful organisation. Cells of the same type group together to form **tissues** — like muscle tissue. Different tissues join to form **organs** — like your heart. Organs cooperate as **organ systems** — like your circulatory system with the heart, arteries, and veins. And all your organ systems together make up the complete **organism** — you!

**Child:** So it is like — cells are like individual workers, tissues are teams, organs are departments, organ systems are divisions, and the whole organism is the company!

**Mother:** What a clever way to put it! And just like a company runs well only when every worker does their job, your body stays healthy only when every cell functions properly. That is why good food, clean water, exercise, and rest are so important — they keep your cells in top shape.
