## Living Things and What Makes Them Special

Step outside your front door and look around. You might spot a pigeon sitting on an electric wire, a stray dog resting under a parked car, weeds pushing through cracks in the footpath, and a crow calling from a rooftop. Now look at the non-living things nearby — a brick wall, a plastic bottle, a metal gate, a puddle of rainwater. What separates the pigeon from the plastic bottle? What makes the weed alive but the brick wall not?

Living things share certain features that set them apart from non-living objects. Scientists call these the **characteristics of life**.

### Growth

All living things grow. A kitten becomes a cat. A tiny mango seedling grows into a towering tree over many years. Even you — compare your height today to your height when you were three years old! Non-living things do not grow on their own. A stone does not become a bigger stone by itself.

### Respiration

Every living organism needs energy, and it gets this energy through **respiration** — a process where food is broken down using oxygen to release energy. You breathe in air, your body uses the oxygen, and you breathe out carbon dioxide. Plants also respire, though they do it more quietly. Even a tiny ant crawling across your kitchen counter is respiring with every step.

### Reproduction

Living things produce new individuals of their own kind. A hen lays eggs that hatch into chicks. A tomato plant in your balcony garden produces seeds inside its fruit, and each seed can grow into a new plant. A mother cat gives birth to kittens. Non-living things cannot do this — a chair cannot produce a smaller chair.

### Response to Stimuli

Touch a "touch-me-not" plant (chui-mui) and its leaves fold shut instantly. Shine a torch near a cockroach at night and it scurries away. Your own eyes shrink their pupils when you walk from a dark room into bright sunlight. Living things **respond** to changes around them — light, heat, touch, sound, or smell. A stone does not react when you touch it.

### Movement

Animals move from place to place — a dog runs, a fish swims, a bird flies. Plants cannot walk, but they still show movement. A sunflower turns its face towards the sun during the day. The roots of a plant always grow downward into the soil, while the shoot grows upward towards light.

### Excretion

When living things use food for energy, waste products are created. Removing these wastes from the body is called **excretion**. You sweat on a hot day and visit the washroom regularly — both are forms of excretion. Plants release oxygen as a waste product of photosynthesis, and they also store some wastes in their falling leaves.

> **Key idea:** If something grows, breathes, reproduces, responds to its surroundings, moves, and gets rid of waste — it is alive.

## Biodiversity — The Variety of Life on Earth

Walk through any vegetable market in India and notice the incredible variety — potatoes, brinjals, lady fingers, spinach, bottle gourd, bitter gourd, drumsticks, fresh coriander, green chillies, and dozens more. And that is just vegetables! Think about all the different fruits, grains, pulses, flowers, trees, insects, birds, fish, and animals found across our country. This enormous variety of life is called **biodiversity**.

The word comes from "bio" (life) and "diversity" (variety). Biodiversity includes every living organism on Earth — from microscopic bacteria in a spoonful of soil to the blue whale in the deep ocean.

### Why is India so rich in biodiversity?

India is one of the world's great treasure houses of life because our country has so many different landscapes and climates packed into one landmass.

**The Western Ghats** — This mountain chain running along India's western coast is a global biodiversity hotspot. Its moist forests are home to the Malabar giant squirrel, the lion-tailed macaque, hundreds of species of frogs, and thousands of flowering plants found nowhere else on the planet.

**The Sundarbans** — Where the rivers Ganga and Brahmaputra meet the Bay of Bengal, vast mangrove forests grow in the salty tidal waters. The Royal Bengal Tiger swims between islands here, and saltwater crocodiles glide through the channels. The tangled mangrove roots shelter countless fish and crabs.

**The Thar Desert** — In Rajasthan, the dry sandy landscape might seem lifeless at first glance, but look closer. The Indian bustard, one of the heaviest flying birds, strides across the scrubland. Desert foxes hunt at dawn. Hardy plants like khejri and cactus survive on almost no rainfall.

> **Think about it:** A single neighbourhood park in your city might contain neem trees, pigeons, squirrels, ants, spiders, earthworms, butterflies, sparrows, crows, and several types of grass and flowering plants — dozens of species sharing one small patch of land.

### Why does biodiversity matter?

Every organism, no matter how small or ordinary it seems, has a role in keeping nature balanced.

- **Honeybees and butterflies** visit flowers and carry pollen, which is how many of our food crops — mangoes, apples, mustard, sunflowers — produce fruit and seeds. Without pollinators, our plates would be half empty.
- **Earthworms** burrow through soil, mixing it and allowing air and water to reach plant roots. Farmers call them "nature's plough."
- **Vultures** clean up dead animals. When India's vulture population crashed years ago, rotting carcasses piled up and disease spread.
- **Mangrove forests** act like a living wall against cyclones and tsunamis, protecting coastal villages from storm surges.

When forests are cleared, rivers are polluted, or animals are hunted beyond recovery, the web of life weakens. Species that are in danger of disappearing are called **endangered**. Those that have vanished forever are **extinct**. Protecting biodiversity means protecting the systems that give us clean air, fresh water, and food.

## Habitats, Adaptations, and Classification

### What is a habitat?

Every living thing has a "home address" in nature — the surroundings where it finds food, water, shelter, and suitable conditions to survive and reproduce. This natural home is called a **habitat**.

Habitats come in three broad types:

**Terrestrial habitats (land)** — forests, grasslands, deserts, mountains

**Aquatic habitats (water)** — rivers, lakes, ponds, oceans, wetlands

**Aerial habitats (air)** — while no organism lives permanently in the air, many birds, bats, and insects spend a significant part of their lives flying and depend on the sky as their feeding and travelling space.

### How organisms fit their habitat — Adaptations

Over many generations, organisms develop special body features, behaviours, or internal processes that help them survive in their particular habitat. These features are called **adaptations**.

**Desert adaptations**
- The **cactus** stores water in its thick, fleshy stem and has spines instead of broad leaves to reduce water loss through evaporation.
- The **camel** can drink over 100 litres of water at one time and then survive for days without drinking again. Its wide, padded feet stop it from sinking into loose sand. Thick eyelashes shield its eyes from blowing sand.

**Mountain and cold region adaptations**
- The **snow leopard** of the Himalayas has a long, thick tail it wraps around its body like a scarf for warmth. Its fur-covered wide paws act like natural snowshoes, spreading its weight on snow.
- **Pine and deodar trees** have narrow, needle-shaped leaves so that snow slides off instead of piling up and snapping branches.

**Forest adaptations**
- **Monkeys** have long, flexible fingers and toes with excellent grip for swinging between branches high above the ground.
- **Thick canopy trees** in tropical forests grow very tall to compete for sunlight, while shade-loving ferns and mosses thrive on the dim forest floor below.

**Freshwater adaptations**
- The **lotus** plant in village ponds has hollow, spongy stems that help it float, and waxy leaves that repel water so they stay dry on the surface.
- **Fish** have a sleek, streamlined body that cuts through water with minimum effort, and **gills** that extract dissolved oxygen from water — something our lungs cannot do.

**Ocean adaptations**
- **Dolphins** have a smooth, torpedo-shaped body for fast swimming and must surface regularly to breathe air through a blowhole on top of their heads.
- **Octopuses** can change their skin colour in an instant to blend into rocks and coral, hiding from predators.

> **Key idea:** An adaptation is like a custom-built solution. A camel's body solves the problems of heat and sand. A fish's body solves the problems of moving and breathing underwater. Move either animal to the other's habitat and it would not survive.

### Why do we classify living things?

Imagine your school library had no sections — science books mixed with storybooks mixed with dictionaries, all tossed randomly on shelves. Finding anything would be a nightmare. Scientists face the same problem with millions of species, so they **classify** (sort and group) living things based on shared features.

**Grouping plants — some common ways:**

| Feature | Group | Examples you see around you |
|---------|-------|-----------------------------|
| Small, soft stem | **Herb** | Pudina (mint), dhaniya (coriander), wheat |
| Medium, many woody branches from the base | **Shrub** | Mehendi (henna), gulab (rose), hibiscus |
| Tall, single thick trunk | **Tree** | Peepal, ashoka, coconut palm |
| Weak stem, climbs upward on support | **Climber** | Money plant, pea plant, grape vine |
| Weak stem, spreads along the ground | **Creeper** | Pumpkin, tarbuz (watermelon), kheera (cucumber) |

Plants can also be grouped by their leaf vein pattern (**venation**), root type (**taproot** vs **fibrous root**), and the number of seed leaves (**monocot** with one cotyledon vs **dicot** with two).

**Grouping animals — a basic division:**

| Group | Key feature | Examples |
|-------|-------------|----------|
| **Vertebrates** | Have a backbone (spine) | Fish, frogs, lizards, sparrows, cows, humans |
| **Invertebrates** | No backbone | Ants, spiders, earthworms, snails, jellyfish, butterflies |

A surprising fact: more than 95 out of every 100 animal species on Earth are invertebrates. Beetles alone have more species than all mammals, birds, reptiles, and amphibians put together.

Animals can also be grouped by how they move (walk, fly, swim, crawl, jump) or where they live (land, water, both).

> **Remember:** Classification does not change what an organism is — it simply helps us study and talk about the enormous variety of life in an organised way.

## Key Terms at a Glance

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Characteristics of life** | Features shared by all living things — growth, respiration, reproduction, response to stimuli, movement, excretion |
| **Biodiversity** | The total variety of living organisms on Earth |
| **Habitat** | The natural environment where an organism lives |
| **Adaptation** | A body feature or behaviour that helps an organism survive in its habitat |
| **Vertebrate** | An animal with a backbone |
| **Invertebrate** | An animal without a backbone |
| **Venation** | The pattern of veins in a leaf |
| **Cotyledon** | A seed leaf found inside a seed; monocots have one, dicots have two |
| **Endangered** | A species whose numbers are so low it risks disappearing |
| **Extinct** | A species that no longer exists anywhere on Earth |
