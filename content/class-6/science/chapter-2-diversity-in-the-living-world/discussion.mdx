## Living Things and What Makes Them Special

**Meera:** Arjun, we had a school trip to Lodhi Garden today. Teacher asked us to make a list of every living and non-living thing we could see. I wrote pigeons, trees, ants, mushrooms, a stray dog, a butterfly. But then I got stuck on one thing — is a dried leaf on the ground still alive?

**Arjun:** Hmm, good question yaar. I think once it falls and dries up, it is no longer alive. When it was on the tree, it was growing, making food, breathing. After it falls, all that stops.

**Meera:** So how do we even decide if something is living or not? What are the rules?

**Arjun:** Our teacher gave us a list — living things grow, breathe, reproduce, respond, move, and remove waste. All six.

**Meera:** Wait, grow I get — the tomato plant on my balcony was tiny when Mummy brought it home, now it is taller than my brother. But plants do not move, na?

**Arjun:** They do, just slowly. The money plant in our window slowly bends towards sunlight. And roots always grow down, stems always grow up. That is movement, just not running around.

**Meera:** Okay. And response — like when I touch a hot pan and pull my hand back?

**Arjun:** Exactly. Or when chui-mui plants close their leaves the second you touch them. We tried that in the school garden last month.

**Meera:** That was so cool! And excretion is just getting rid of waste, right?

**Arjun:** Yes. We sweat, we go to the washroom, we breathe out carbon dioxide. Plants do it too. They release oxygen when they make food, and they store waste in old leaves before dropping them.

**Meera:** So that dried leaf I saw was actually full of the plant's old waste before it fell. Funny.

**Arjun:** All six things together — that is what makes something alive. Grow, breathe, reproduce, respond, move, excrete.

## Biodiversity — The Variety of Life on Earth

**Meera:** Yaar, I watched a nature show last night with Daadi. The man on TV said Earth has more than 80 lakh species, and crores more we have not even found yet. Is that real?

**Arjun:** It is real. Daadu has a whole book about it. Even in our colony park, count for one minute — neem trees, gulmohar, bougainvillea, sparrows, crows, mynas, that fat squirrel near the bench, ants in every crack, earthworms in the wet mud, butterflies on the flowers, spiders in the corners.

**Meera:** Just one park! Now imagine all the forests, oceans, deserts, mountains in the whole world.

**Arjun:** That is why the word is biodiversity. "Bio" means life, "diversity" means variety. Variety of life everywhere.

**Meera:** The TV show called the Western Ghats a "biodiversity hotspot." What does that mean?

**Arjun:** It means a place with a HUGE number of species, and many of them are not found anywhere else on Earth. The Western Ghats have moist forests with the lion-tailed macaque, the giant Malabar squirrel, hundreds of types of frogs, thousands of insects.

**Meera:** What about the Sundarbans? My uncle in Kolkata talks about them.

**Arjun:** Mangrove forests! Where the Ganga and Brahmaputra meet the sea. Royal Bengal Tigers actually swim between the islands there. Saltwater crocodiles, river dolphins, fish, crabs — all in those salty muddy waters.

**Meera:** And the Thar Desert? I always thought deserts have nothing in them.

**Arjun:** Big mistake, yaar! The Thar has the Great Indian Bustard, one of the biggest flying birds in the world. Desert foxes, lizards, khejri trees that survive on almost no rain.

**Meera:** So why should we care if one type of frog disappears from some forest? How does that affect me here in Delhi?

**Arjun:** Because everything is connected like threads in a cloth. Pull one thread, the cloth weakens. Frogs eat mosquitoes — if frogs vanish, mosquitoes go crazy, more diseases for everyone. Bees pollinate flowers — no bees means no mangoes in summer, no apples, no mustard.

**Meera:** No mangoes? That is scary.

**Arjun:** And vultures clean up dead animals. India lost most of its vultures because of one pesticide, and rotting carcasses started piling up, spreading disease. Every animal has a job. Saving them is not charity. It is saving ourselves.

## Habitats, Adaptations, and Classification

**Meera:** Arjun, Riya brought her goldfish bowl to school last year for show-and-tell. Then one day her mummy left the bowl in the sun by mistake. The water got warm and the fish almost died. Why does warm water hurt a fish?

**Arjun:** Because a goldfish is built for cool, clean water. Its body works only in a certain temperature range. When the water gets warm, the oxygen inside the water gets less, and the fish cannot breathe properly through its gills.

**Meera:** So every animal has its own kind of place where it survives?

**Arjun:** Exactly. That is called a habitat. It is like the animal's home address. The fish belongs in cool water. A camel belongs in the desert. A snow leopard belongs in the icy Himalayas. A lotus belongs in a pond.

**Meera:** And the things on their body that help them live in their habitat?

**Arjun:** Those are adaptations. Like custom-made solutions. The camel has wide padded feet so it does not sink in sand. It stores fat in its hump so it can survive days without water. Thick eyelashes to keep sand out of its eyes.

**Meera:** And the cactus has spines instead of leaves so water does not escape from it. We learnt that last week.

**Arjun:** Right! Same problem — heat and no water — two totally different solutions.

**Meera:** What about cold places?

**Arjun:** Snow leopards in Ladakh have super thick fur and a long bushy tail they wrap around themselves like a scarf. Their paws are wide and covered in fur, like built-in snowshoes. Pine trees in the mountains have thin needle-like leaves so snow slides off and does not break branches.

**Meera:** And in water? Why can a fish breathe under water but I cannot?

**Arjun:** Because a fish has gills. Gills can pull oxygen out of water. Our lungs can only pull oxygen from air. Fish are also shaped like a torpedo to slip through water easily. A lotus floats because its stems are hollow, and the leaves are waxy so water rolls off.

**Meera:** Okay, one more thing — why do we have to classify all these animals and plants? Why not just study each one?

**Arjun:** Because there are millions! Imagine if our school library had no sections — science books, storybooks, dictionaries, all dumped in one heap. You would never find anything.

**Meera:** Oh, so classification is like the library sections. Fiction here, history there, science over there.

**Arjun:** Exactly. We group plants as herbs, shrubs, trees, climbers, creepers based on stems. Animals as vertebrates and invertebrates based on backbones. Once you know the group, you can guess a lot about the animal without even seeing it.

**Meera:** And new species keep getting discovered, so the library keeps growing. That is actually exciting.

**Arjun:** Daadu always says — the more we learn, the bigger the unknown becomes. There are crores of species nobody has ever named yet. Maybe one of us will name one some day.
