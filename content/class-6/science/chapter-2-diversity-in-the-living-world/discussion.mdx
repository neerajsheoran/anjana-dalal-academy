## Living Things and What Makes Them Special

**Child:** Maa, we went to Lodhi Garden today on a school trip. Our teacher told us to make a list of every living and non-living thing we could spot. I wrote down trees, grass, pigeons, a dog, ants, a butterfly, and even mushrooms growing near a log. But then I got confused — is a dried-up fallen leaf still alive?

**Mother:** That is a really thoughtful question. A fallen leaf is no longer alive. When it was attached to the tree, it was part of a living organism — it was growing, making food through sunlight, and exchanging gases. Once it fell and dried up, all those processes stopped. It is now dead organic matter.

**Child:** So how do we decide whether something is living or not? What are the rules?

**Mother:** Scientists look for certain characteristics. Living things grow — think about how small our tomato plant on the balcony was when we first brought it home, and how tall it is now. Living things breathe — we inhale oxygen and exhale carbon dioxide, and even the tiny ants on our kitchen counter are doing the same thing at their own scale. Living things reproduce — our cat Meera had kittens last year, remember? And living things respond to their surroundings. When you accidentally touch a hot pan, your hand pulls back instantly. That is a response to a stimulus.

**Child:** What about movement? Plants cannot walk anywhere.

**Mother:** True, plants do not walk. But they do move. Think about how the money plant on our window slowly bends towards the sunlight over a few days. Or how the roots of every plant push downward into soil while the stem grows upward. Movement in living things does not always mean walking from one place to another — it means any change in position or shape caused by the organism itself.

**Child:** And excretion — that is about getting rid of waste, right?

**Mother:** Exactly. When your body uses food for energy, waste products are created. You get rid of them through sweat, through going to the washroom, and even through breathing out carbon dioxide. Plants also excrete — they release oxygen during photosynthesis, and some plants store waste chemicals in their old leaves before dropping them. So all six features together — growth, respiration, reproduction, response, movement, and excretion — tell us something is alive.

## Biodiversity — The Variety of Life on Earth

**Child:** Maa, I was watching a nature documentary last night, and the narrator said Earth has over 80 lakh species that scientists have identified so far, and there could be crores more that we have not even discovered yet. Is that really true?

**Mother:** It is. And that is what makes our planet extraordinary. Think about just our own neighbourhood. In the park down the road, you can find neem trees, gulmohar trees, bougainvillea bushes, different types of grass, sparrows, crows, mynas, squirrels, stray cats, earthworms in the soil, ants in the cracks, spiders in their webs, and butterflies visiting flowers. That is already dozens of species in one small park. Now multiply that across forests, oceans, mountains, deserts, and rivers all over the world.

**Child:** The documentary showed the Western Ghats in India and called it a "biodiversity hotspot." What does that mean?

**Mother:** A biodiversity hotspot is a region with an exceptionally large number of species, many of which are found nowhere else on the planet. The Western Ghats have moist forests that are home to the lion-tailed macaque, the Malabar giant squirrel, hundreds of types of frogs and orchids, and thousands of insect species. Scientists have been exploring these forests for centuries and are still discovering new species.

**Child:** What about the Sundarbans? We read about them in class.

**Mother:** The Sundarbans are India's vast mangrove forests where the Ganga and Brahmaputra rivers empty into the Bay of Bengal. The Royal Bengal Tiger actually swims between the islands there. Saltwater crocodiles, river dolphins, and countless species of fish and crabs live in those tidal waters. The mangrove trees themselves are incredible — their tangled roots trap sediment, protect the coastline from cyclones, and serve as nurseries where young fish grow before heading out to the open sea.

**Child:** And then there is the Thar Desert in Rajasthan. I would have thought deserts have no life.

**Mother:** That is a common mistake. The Thar may look barren, but it is home to the Great Indian Bustard — one of the heaviest flying birds in the world — along with desert foxes, various lizards, and hardy plants like the khejri tree. Life finds a way in almost every corner of the Earth. That is the wonder of biodiversity.

**Child:** Why should we care about protecting all these species? If one type of frog disappears from the Western Ghats, how does that affect us?

**Mother:** Because every species is connected to others like threads in a fabric. Pull out one thread and the fabric weakens. Frogs eat mosquitoes and other insects — without them, insect populations can explode and spread diseases. Bees pollinate our food crops — no bees means no mangoes, no apples, no mustard. Vultures clean up dead animals — when India lost most of its vultures to a pesticide, rotting carcasses piled up and diseases increased. Every organism has a role. Protecting biodiversity is not charity towards animals — it is self-preservation.

## Habitats, Adaptations, and Classification

**Child:** Maa, my friend has a goldfish at home. She told me that when they once accidentally left the fish bowl in the sun for too long, the water got warm and the fish became very sluggish and almost died. Why would warm water hurt a fish?

**Mother:** Because the goldfish is adapted to live in cool, clean freshwater. Its body is designed for a specific set of conditions — a certain temperature range, a certain level of dissolved oxygen in the water. When the water got too warm, the amount of oxygen dissolved in it dropped, and the fish struggled to breathe through its gills. Every organism is adapted to its own habitat, and even small changes in conditions can be dangerous.

**Child:** So a habitat is like an organism's natural address?

**Mother:** Exactly. It is the specific place where an organism finds everything it needs — food, water, shelter, the right temperature, and mates for reproduction. A lotus grows in a pond. A camel roams the desert. A snow leopard prowls the snowy mountains of the Himalayas. Each one is suited to its own habitat.

**Child:** And adaptations are the features that make them suited, right?

**Mother:** Yes. Think of adaptations as custom-made solutions to specific problems. In the desert, the problem is extreme heat and almost no water. The camel solves this by storing fat in its hump that can be converted into energy and water, by having wide padded feet that do not sink into sand, and by having thick eyelashes and closeable nostrils to keep out blowing sand. The cactus solves the same problem differently — it stores water in its thick stem and has spines instead of broad leaves so that very little water evaporates.

**Child:** What about animals in cold places?

**Mother:** The snow leopard of Ladakh and Himachal Pradesh has an incredibly thick fur coat, a long bushy tail it wraps around its face while sleeping for warmth, and wide paws covered in fur that work like snowshoes. Pine trees in the mountains have narrow needle-shaped leaves — snow slides right off them instead of piling up and breaking branches.

**Child:** And in water? Why can fish survive underwater but we cannot?

**Mother:** Fish have gills — special organs that filter dissolved oxygen from water. Our lungs can only extract oxygen from air. Fish also have a streamlined body shaped like a torpedo, which lets them cut through water with very little effort. A lotus plant in a pond has hollow stems that help it float and waxy leaves that repel water. Every adaptation matches the organism to its environment perfectly.

**Child:** Maa, one more thing — why do we need to classify all these organisms into groups? Why not just study each one individually?

**Mother:** Because there are millions of species. Studying each one individually with no organisation would be like trying to find a specific book in a library where all the books are dumped in a giant pile on the floor. Classification creates an organised system. We group plants as herbs, shrubs, trees, climbers, and creepers based on their stem. We divide animals into vertebrates and invertebrates based on whether they have a backbone. These groups help scientists see patterns. For example, most plants with parallel leaf veins also tend to have fibrous roots and monocot seeds. Once you know the group, you can predict other features.

**Child:** So classification is like putting books into sections — fiction, science, history — so you can find what you need quickly?

**Mother:** That is a perfect comparison. And just like a library keeps getting new books and sometimes reorganises its sections, scientists keep discovering new species and updating their classification systems. The living world is so vast and varied that we will probably never finish cataloguing all of it — and that is part of what makes studying it so exciting.
