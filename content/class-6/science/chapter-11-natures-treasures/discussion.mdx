## Air — Easy to Forget, Impossible to Live Without

**Meera:** Yaar, everyone keeps shouting "save water, save trees." But nobody says "save the air." Why? Is air just always going to be there?

**Arjun:** The air itself is not going to disappear, Meera. But CLEAN air — the kind our bodies actually need — is becoming hard to find in cities.

**Meera:** Hmm. Like the smoke from buses and trucks?

**Arjun:** Exactly. Petrol and diesel engines release carbon monoxide, nitrogen oxides, and tiny tiny particles that go deep into our lungs. Factories add sulphur dioxide. And remember last Diwali when the whole sky outside school was orange-grey?

**Meera:** Haan! My eyes were stinging and Mummy did not let me go to the terrace for THREE days. I missed the kite festival because of it.

**Arjun:** That was air pollution hitting you directly. The atmosphere is like a thin blanket around the Earth. If you shrank Earth to the size of a football, the atmosphere would be thinner than the paint.

**Meera:** Whoa. We are pumping smoke into something that thin?

**Arjun:** And air is not just for breathing, yaar. Oxygen powers respiration in nearly every living thing. Carbon dioxide feeds plant photosynthesis. Nitrogen is converted by soil bacteria into food for crops. And the WIND moves rain clouds, pollinates flowers, even spins wind turbines.

**Meera:** Wait — India makes electricity from wind?

**Arjun:** One of the top five wind energy producers in the world. So air gives us breath, feeds our plants, AND powers our lights. Protecting it is just as urgent as protecting rivers and forests.

## Water — Abundant Yet Scarce

**Meera:** Arjun, our geography teacher showed us that water covers MOST of the Earth. Then why does my Nani's village in Rajasthan run out of water every summer?

**Arjun:** Because most of Earth's water — about 97 percent — is salty ocean water. You cannot drink it or use it for fields. Only 3 percent is freshwater, and most of THAT is locked up in glaciers and ice.

**Meera:** So the freshwater available in rivers and underground is...?

**Arjun:** A tiny tiny fraction. And that tiny fraction has to serve over 800 crore people, every farm, every factory in the world.

**Meera:** No wonder Nani never lets me leave the tap running for even one second!

**Arjun:** Your Nani's generation understood water deeply. In Rajasthan, people built kunds — underground rainwater tanks — centuries ago. In Gujarat, they carved magnificent stepwells like Rani ki Vav, where you can walk down stone steps to reach water even in scorching summer.

**Meera:** And in Meghalaya?

**Arjun:** Tribal farmers there created bamboo drip irrigation. Hollow bamboo channels carry spring water directly to each plant on steep hillsides. All of these are ancient water conservation systems.

**Meera:** And today we have rainwater harvesting on rooftops?

**Arjun:** Haan. Collect rain from the roof, filter it, store it in tanks or send it underground to recharge the water table. Many Indian cities now make it compulsory in new buildings.

**Meera:** Every litre of rainwater we capture means one litre less pumped from underground.

**Arjun:** Your Nani would approve.

## Forests — A Whole World in the Woods

**Meera:** Arjun, if forests are so important, can we not just plant rows of trees everywhere and call it a forest?

**Arjun:** A row of planted trees is a great thing, yaar. But it is not a forest.

**Meera:** What is the difference?

**Arjun:** A forest is an entire LIVING COMMUNITY. Tall trees form the canopy. Smaller trees and bushes form the layer below. Herbs and grasses cover the ground. Mosses cling to bark. Fungi break down fallen wood. Insects pollinate flowers. Birds spread seeds. Predators keep herbivore numbers in check.

**Meera:** Wah. So everything depends on everything else?

**Arjun:** Exactly. That whole web takes decades — sometimes centuries — to develop. You cannot create it in a weekend tree-plantation drive.

**Meera:** And on the forest floor — yaar, it always looks like such a mess. Dead leaves everywhere.

**Arjun:** That "mess" is actually nature's composting system. Fallen leaves, dead branches, animal droppings — all of it gets broken down by fungi, bacteria, termites, and earthworms into rich, dark humus.

**Meera:** And humus feeds the soil, which feeds the trees, which drop more leaves...

**Arjun:** A perfect cycle. Nothing wasted. Cutting forests breaks this. Without tree roots, monsoon rains wash away the topsoil — floods downstream, landslides on hills. Animals lose their homes. Carbon stored in trees escapes into the air.

**Meera:** And that is why the Chipko Movement happened?

**Arjun:** In 1973, in the hills of Uttarakhand, women led by Gaura Devi physically HUGGED trees so loggers could not cut them. They understood that the forest's survival was their own survival.

**Meera:** Yaar, that takes serious courage.

**Arjun:** Their courage changed India's forest laws and inspired environmental movements across the globe. Ordinary people CAN protect nature.

## Fossil Fuels and the Promise of Renewable Energy

**Meera:** Arjun — the cooking gas cylinder Mummy uses, the petrol in Papa's scooter, the coal that makes electricity. Are these all fossil fuels?

**Arjun:** Haan. Fossil fuels are the remains of plants and tiny sea creatures that lived hundreds of MILLIONS of years ago. Layers of sediment buried them, and heat and pressure deep underground slowly transformed them into coal, petroleum, and natural gas.

**Meera:** So when Papa fills petrol, he is using energy that the Sun gave to ancient sea creatures millions of years ago?

**Arjun:** Exactly. Stored ancient solar energy. But there are two serious problems.

**Meera:** Yeh dono kya hain?

**Arjun:** First, fossil fuels took millions of years to form, and we are burning through them in just a few centuries. They WILL run out. Second, burning them releases carbon dioxide. CO2 traps heat in the atmosphere like a thick blanket, warming up the planet. Climate change.

**Meera:** That is why our school put solar panels on the roof last year!

**Arjun:** Exactly. Solar panels convert sunlight directly into electricity. No burning, no smoke, no CO2. Wind turbines capture moving air. Hydroelectric dams use flowing river water. These are all renewable — they do not run out.

**Meera:** Can India really replace fossil fuels with renewable energy?

**Arjun:** Big challenge, but we are moving. Electric scooters are showing up everywhere. CNG already powers buses and autos in Delhi. Rooftop solar is getting affordable for middle-class families. Government is targeting 500 gigawatts of renewable energy by 2030.

**Meera:** Every panel, every EV, every tree planted moves us in the right direction?

**Arjun:** That is the whole idea.

## What Can Each of Us Do?

**Meera:** Yaar, listening to all this — air pollution, water shortages, forests disappearing, fossil fuels running out — it sounds huge and scary. What can I, one kid in Class 6, possibly do?

**Arjun:** More than you think, Meera. Switch off the fan and lights every time you leave a room. That saves electricity AND reduces the coal burned at a power plant somewhere.

**Meera:** And close the tap while brushing teeth?

**Arjun:** A running tap wastes around 5 litres per minute. Just shutting it for two minutes a day saves serious water across a year.

**Meera:** Carry a cloth bag when Mummy goes shopping — one less plastic bag in some landfill.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Use both sides of your notebook paper — fewer trees cut for paper pulp.

**Meera:** And I water the tulsi and neem on our terrace — they absorb CO2!

**Arjun:** Compost kitchen peels instead of throwing them in the bin. Walk to the corner shop instead of asking Papa to take you on the scooter for two minutes of laziness.

**Meera:** None of these is very difficult on its own.

**Arjun:** That is the beautiful truth, yaar. If 140 crore Indians each make a handful of small everyday choices — turn off a light, fix a leak, plant a sapling, carry a cloth bag — the combined effect is ENORMOUS.

**Meera:** Nature's treasures — air, water, soil, forests, minerals — they don't just belong to us.

**Arjun:** They belong to our children and their children too. Protecting them is not somebody else's job. It starts with awareness. It starts with each one of us.

**Meera:** Starting with understanding the science behind WHY they matter.

**Arjun:** Yaar, you just said the whole chapter in one line.
