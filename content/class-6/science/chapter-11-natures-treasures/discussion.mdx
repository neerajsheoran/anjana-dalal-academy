## Air — Easy to Forget, Impossible to Live Without

**Child:** Maa, everyone talks about saving water and saving forests. But nobody says "save the air." Is air always going to be there?

**Mother:** The air itself is not going to disappear, beta. But clean air — the kind our bodies need — is becoming harder to find, especially in cities. The atmosphere is like a very thin blanket around the Earth. If you shrank the Earth to the size of a football, the atmosphere would be thinner than the paint on its surface. And we keep pumping pollutants into this thin layer.

**Child:** Like the smoke from buses and trucks?

**Mother:** Exactly. Engines burning petrol and diesel release carbon monoxide, nitrogen oxides, and tiny particles so small they go deep into our lungs. Factories add sulphur dioxide and toxic chemicals. And remember last Diwali when the city was covered in smog? Part of that was from firecrackers, and part was from farmers in neighbouring states burning paddy stubble after harvest.

**Child:** Yes, Maa! My eyes were stinging and you did not let me go outside for three days.

**Mother:** That was air pollution directly harming us. But air is not just for breathing. Oxygen in air powers respiration in nearly every organism. Carbon dioxide feeds plant photosynthesis. Nitrogen is converted by soil bacteria into nutrients for crops. And wind — moving air — drives rain clouds, pollinates flowers, and even generates electricity through wind turbines. India is one of the top five wind energy producers in the world!

**Child:** So air gives us breath, feeds our plants, and can even power our lights?

**Mother:** All of that. Protecting air quality is just as urgent as protecting our rivers and forests.

## Water — Abundant Yet Scarce

**Child:** Maa, our geography teacher showed us that water covers most of the Earth. Then why do Nani's village in Rajasthan face water shortages every summer?

**Mother:** Because most of Earth's water — about 97 percent — is salty ocean water. You cannot drink it, cook with it, or irrigate fields with it. Only 3 percent is freshwater, and most of that is locked in glaciers and ice sheets. The freshwater available in rivers, lakes, and underground is a tiny fraction. That tiny fraction has to serve over 800 crore people, every farm, and every factory in the world.

**Child:** No wonder Nani is so careful about not wasting a single drop!

**Mother:** Your Nani's generation understood water's value deeply. In Rajasthan, people built **kunds** — underground rainwater tanks — centuries ago. In Gujarat, they carved magnificent **stepwells** like Rani ki Vav, where you could walk down stone steps to reach the water even in scorching summers. In Meghalaya, tribal farmers created **bamboo drip irrigation** — using hollow bamboo sections to channel spring water directly to each plant on steep hillsides. All of these are ancient water conservation systems.

**Child:** And today we have rainwater harvesting?

**Mother:** Yes. Collecting rain from rooftops, filtering it, and either storing it in tanks or directing it underground to recharge the water table. Many Indian cities now require it in new buildings. Every litre of rainwater we capture means one litre less pumped from a shrinking underground aquifer. Your Nani would approve!

## Forests — A Whole World in the Woods

**Child:** Maa, if forests are so important, why can't we just plant rows of trees everywhere and call it a forest?

**Mother:** A row of planted trees is wonderful, but it is not a forest. A forest is an entire living community — tall trees form the canopy, smaller trees and bushes make up the understorey, herbs and grasses cover the ground, mosses and ferns cling to bark, fungi break down fallen wood, insects pollinate flowers, birds spread seeds, and predators keep herbivore populations in check. Every species depends on others. That complex web takes decades — sometimes centuries — to develop.

**Child:** What happens on the forest floor? It always looks messy with dead leaves.

**Mother:** That "mess" is nature's composting system! Fallen leaves, dead branches, and animal droppings decompose — broken down by fungi, bacteria, termites, and earthworms into rich, dark **humus**. Humus nourishes the soil, the soil feeds the trees, the trees drop more leaves — a perfect cycle of recycling where nothing is wasted.

**Child:** And cutting down forests breaks this cycle?

**Mother:** Completely. Without tree roots anchoring the soil, heavy monsoon rains wash away the topsoil — causing floods downstream and landslides on hillsides. Without the canopy, the forest floor dries out. Animals lose their homes. Carbon stored in trees escapes into the atmosphere, accelerating climate change. That is why the **Chipko Movement** in 1973 was so powerful — women in the hills of Uttarakhand physically hugged trees to stop loggers.

**Child:** Gaura Devi and the other women risked their safety for the trees!

**Mother:** They understood that the forest's survival was their survival. Their courage changed India's forest laws and inspired environmental movements across the globe. Even today, ordinary people taking a stand can protect nature.

## Fossil Fuels and the Promise of Renewable Energy

**Child:** Maa, the cooking gas cylinder we use, the petrol in Papa's scooter, the coal that makes electricity — are these all fossil fuels?

**Mother:** Yes, beta. Fossil fuels are the remains of plants and tiny sea creatures that lived hundreds of millions of years ago. Over immense spans of time, layers of sediment buried them, and heat and pressure deep underground transformed them into coal, petroleum, and natural gas.

**Child:** So when Papa fills petrol, he is using energy that the Sun gave to ancient sea creatures millions of years ago?

**Mother:** That is exactly right — stored ancient solar energy! But there are two serious problems. First, fossil fuels took millions of years to form, yet we are burning through them in just a few centuries. They will run out. Second, burning them releases carbon dioxide and other pollutants. CO₂ traps heat in the atmosphere like a thick blanket, causing the planet to warm — what we call climate change.

**Child:** That is why our school has solar panels on the roof now?

**Mother:** Precisely. Solar panels convert sunlight directly into electricity — no burning, no smoke, no CO₂. Wind turbines capture the energy of moving air. Hydroelectric dams use flowing river water. These are all **renewable** — they do not run out and produce little or no pollution. India's Bhadla Solar Park in Rajasthan is one of the largest solar installations on the entire planet!

**Child:** Can India actually replace all fossil fuels with renewable energy?

**Mother:** It is a massive challenge, but we are making progress. Electric scooters and cars are appearing on our roads. CNG — compressed natural gas — already powers autorickshaws and buses in Delhi, Mumbai, and many other cities as a cleaner stopgap fuel. Rooftop solar panels are becoming affordable for middle-class families. The government targets 500 gigawatts of renewable energy capacity by 2030. Every panel installed, every EV sold, every tree planted moves us in the right direction.

## What Can Each of Us Do?

**Child:** Maa, air pollution, water shortages, disappearing forests, fossil fuels running out — everything feels so big and scary. What can I possibly do?

**Mother:** More than you think, beta. Switch off the fan and lights every time you leave a room — that saves electricity and reduces the coal burned at a power plant. Close the tap while brushing your teeth — a running tap wastes around 5 litres per minute. Carry a cloth bag when we go shopping — one less plastic bag in a river or a landfill. Use both sides of your notebook paper — fewer trees cut for paper pulp.

**Child:** And I can water the tulsi and neem plants on our terrace — they absorb CO₂!

**Mother:** Absolutely. Composting our kitchen peels and vegetable scraps turns waste into rich fertiliser instead of adding to a garbage dump. Walking to school on days when weather is fine instead of taking an auto reduces exhaust fumes. Telling your friends about these choices multiplies the impact.

**Child:** None of these things is very difficult on its own.

**Mother:** That is the beautiful truth. If 140 crore Indians each make a handful of small, everyday choices — turn off a light, fix a leak, plant a sapling, carry a cloth bag — the combined effect is enormous. Nature's treasures — air, water, soil, forests, minerals — are not just ours. They belong equally to your children and their children. Protecting them is not somebody else's job. It starts with awareness, and it starts with each one of us.

**Child:** Starting with understanding the science behind why they matter.

**Mother:** Exactly, beta. That is the real treasure of this chapter.
