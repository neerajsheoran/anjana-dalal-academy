## Stars — Blazing Suns in the Darkness

**Meera:** Yaar, when we went to my Nani's village last Diwali, I lay on the terrace and saw THOUSANDS of stars at night. Back home in the city, I can barely count ten. Why?

**Arjun:** Light pollution, Meera. Streetlights, shop signs, building lights — all that artificial glow drowns out the faint starlight. In your Nani's village, away from all that, the sky looks the way our ancestors saw it.

**Meera:** Our textbook calls the Milky Way "Akasha Ganga" — the river of the sky!

**Arjun:** What a lovely name, na? It looks like a hazy white band stretching overhead, but it is actually our galaxy — a colossal spiral of two hundred to four hundred BILLION stars.

**Meera:** And our Sun is just one of those billions?

**Arjun:** Just one. Sitting about 26,000 light years from the centre. When you gaze at the Milky Way, you are peering into the dense heart of your own galaxy from the inside.

**Meera:** And a light year — that is the distance light covers in one year, right? About 9.46 lakh crore kilometres!

**Arjun:** The closest star after the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is 4.24 light years away. Even light, which is the fastest thing in the universe, takes over four years to get there.

**Meera:** The distances are crazy.

**Arjun:** And each of those twinkling dots is a gigantic ball of hot gas — many far bigger than our Sun — shining through nuclear fusion. The red star Betelgeuse in Orion is so huge that if it replaced our Sun, it would swallow Mercury, Venus, Earth, AND Mars.

**Meera:** Yaar, I am never going to look at the night sky the same way again.

## Constellations and India's Ancient Sky Watchers

**Meera:** Arjun, the chapter talks about Saptarishi. Is that the same thing as the Big Dipper?

**Arjun:** Haan! The seven brightest stars of Ursa Major — what the West calls the Big Dipper — are known in India as the Saptarishi, named after seven legendary sages: Marichi, Atri, Angiras, Pulaha, Kratu, Pulastya, and Vashistha.

**Meera:** Indian sky watchers were doing this thousands of years ago?

**Arjun:** Long before telescopes existed. They were mapping the heavens, predicting eclipses, building calendars — all by eye.

**Meera:** And the Pole Star — Dhruva Tara — stays in one place while everything else moves?

**Arjun:** It appears almost fixed because it sits nearly directly above Earth's North Pole. As the Earth spins, every other star seems to sweep across the sky, but Polaris barely budges.

**Meera:** How do I find it from my own terrace?

**Arjun:** Look at the two stars that form the front edge of the Big Dipper's bowl. Draw an imaginary line through them and extend it about five times that distance. The star you reach — that is Polaris.

**Meera:** The story goes that young Dhruva meditated with such determination that he was granted an unshakeable place in the sky!

**Arjun:** Beautiful connection between our stories and astronomy. Indian astronomers also divided the sky into 27 nakshatras — lunar mansions — based on where the Moon appears each night.

**Meera:** And Jantar Mantar?

**Arjun:** Built by Maharaja Jai Singh II in the 1700s — in Jaipur and Delhi. Gigantic stone instruments that tracked stars, measured time, predicted eclipses with astonishing accuracy. And ALL of this without a single telescope.

**Meera:** Yaar, that is genius.

## The Solar System — Our Cosmic Neighbourhood

**Meera:** Arjun, I know the planet order — Meri Very Energetic Maa Joyfully Saw Uttar Nakshatra! But why did Pluto get kicked out of the list?

**Arjun:** In 2006, the International Astronomical Union set a precise definition for what counts as a planet. Three rules: it must orbit the Sun, have enough mass to be roughly spherical, and have cleared its orbital neighbourhood of other objects.

**Meera:** And Pluto failed the third one?

**Arjun:** Exactly. Pluto's orbit passes through a crowded zone of icy bodies — it has not cleared its neighbourhood. So it was reclassified as a dwarf planet.

**Meera:** Poor Pluto! It must feel left out.

**Arjun:** Ha! Lots of people felt sentimental about it. But science needs precise definitions. There are now five officially recognised dwarf planets, and Pluto is the most famous one. The important thing is that our knowledge keeps evolving.

**Meera:** Arjun, here is something that really puzzles me. Venus is FARTHER from the Sun than Mercury, yet Venus is the hottest planet. How?

**Arjun:** Good question, yaar. Venus has an extremely dense atmosphere made almost entirely of carbon dioxide, which is a powerful greenhouse gas. Heat from the Sun enters but cannot escape.

**Meera:** Like being inside a car with all windows rolled up on a hot summer afternoon!

**Arjun:** Exactly. Mercury, on the other hand, has almost no atmosphere, so heat radiates straight back into space at night. It is a perfect example of how atmosphere matters more than mere distance from the Sun.

## India on the Moon — The Chandrayaan Story

**Meera:** Arjun, tell me about Chandrayaan-3! I watched the landing on TV with my whole family. Mummy was crying. Papa was clapping. Even Nani was clutching her dupatta!

**Arjun:** On 23 August 2023, the entire nation held its breath, yaar. The Vikram lander, carrying the little Pragyan rover, descended toward the Moon's south pole. When it touched down safely, India became the FIRST country ever to land near the lunar south pole — and the fourth nation to achieve a soft landing on the Moon.

**Meera:** Why is the south pole so special?

**Arjun:** Scientists believe that permanently shadowed craters near the south pole may have water ice. If water exists on the Moon, future astronauts could use it for drinking, growing food, and even splitting it into hydrogen and oxygen for rocket fuel.

**Meera:** Whoa. So the Moon could be a refuelling station?

**Arjun:** The south pole could be the gateway to humans living on the Moon for long stretches.

**Meera:** And Chandrayaan-1 in 2008 actually found water on the Moon FIRST?

**Arjun:** Haan! Its Moon Impact Probe detected water molecules on the lunar surface — a discovery that shook scientists worldwide. Before that, most assumed the Moon was bone dry. India's contribution to lunar science has been truly pioneering.

**Meera:** And now ISRO is working on Gaganyaan — sending Indian astronauts to space?

**Arjun:** That is right. Indian astronauts will be called Vyomanauts. When Gaganyaan succeeds, India will join the very small club of nations that have sent humans beyond Earth's atmosphere.

**Meera:** From Chandrayaan to Mangalyaan to Gaganyaan — ISRO does these things on much smaller budgets than other space agencies.

**Arjun:** Yaar, that is the proudest part. It proves vision and dedication matter more than money.

## How Big Is the Universe, Really?

**Meera:** Arjun, I keep trying to imagine the distances in space, but my brain just gives up. Help.

**Arjun:** Let us try a scale model. Imagine the Sun is a large amla — about 3 centimetres across.

**Meera:** Okay.

**Arjun:** At that scale, Earth would be a tiny mustard seed roughly 3 metres away. Jupiter would be a small ber fruit about 15 metres away. Neptune would be about 90 metres away.

**Meera:** And the nearest star, Proxima Centauri?

**Arjun:** Another amla — about 800 KILOMETRES away.

**Meera:** 800 kilometres away from a 3-centimetre amla?? The emptiness between stars is unbelievable!

**Arjun:** And the Milky Way, at this scale, would span tens of crores of kilometres. The observable universe contains hundreds of billions of galaxies like the Milky Way, each with hundreds of billions of stars.

**Meera:** How old is all of this?

**Arjun:** The universe is about 13.8 billion years old. It started with the Big Bang. And it is still expanding right now — galaxies moving farther apart even as we speak.

**Meera:** Arjun, are we alone? Is there life somewhere out there?

**Arjun:** That is one of the most profound unanswered questions in all of science, yaar. Researchers are hunting for signs of life on Mars, on Jupiter's moon Europa — which has a liquid ocean hidden beneath its icy crust — and on Saturn's moon Enceladus, which actually shoots water geysers into space.

**Meera:** And exoplanets?

**Arjun:** Thousands have been discovered orbiting other stars. Some are in the habitable zone — where liquid water could exist on the surface.

**Meera:** With so many stars and so many planets, it seems weird that only Earth would have life.

**Arjun:** Many scientists feel exactly the same way. But until we find evidence, we cannot be certain. The search continues — through telescopes, space probes, rovers on Mars, and the boundless curiosity of young people.

**Meera:** Maybe one of us will be the one to find that answer?

**Arjun:** Yaar, do not say "maybe." Just keep looking up.
