## Stars — Blazing Suns in the Darkness

**Child:** Maa, when we went to Nani's village last Diwali, I saw thousands of stars at night. Back home in the city, I can barely count ten. Why is that?

**Mother:** That is light pollution, beta. Streetlights, shop signs, building lights — all of that artificial glow drowns out the faint starlight. In the village, away from all that, the sky looks the way our ancestors saw it — packed with stars, and the Milky Way stretching overhead like a shimmering river.

**Child:** Our textbook calls the Milky Way "Akasha Ganga" — the river of the sky!

**Mother:** What a lovely name, na? It looks like a hazy white band, but it is actually our galaxy — a colossal spiral of two hundred to four hundred billion stars. Our Sun is just one of those billions, sitting about 26,000 light years from the centre. When you gaze at the Milky Way, you are peering into the dense heart of our own galaxy from the inside.

**Child:** And a light year is the distance light covers in a whole year — about 9.46 lakh crore kilometres! The closest star after the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is 4.24 light years away. Even light would take over four years to get there!

**Mother:** The distances are staggering. And each of those twinkling dots is a gigantic ball of hot gas — many far larger than our Sun — shining through nuclear fusion. The red star Betelgeuse in Orion is so immense that if it replaced our Sun, it would swallow Mercury, Venus, Earth, and even Mars inside it.

## Constellations and India's Ancient Sky Watchers

**Child:** Maa, the chapter talks about Saptarishi. Is that the same as the Big Dipper?

**Mother:** Yes! The seven prominent stars of Ursa Major — what the West calls the Big Dipper or the Plough — are known in India as the **Saptarishi**, named after seven legendary sages: Marichi, Atri, Angiras, Pulaha, Kratu, Pulastya, and Vashistha. Indian sky watchers have been mapping the heavens for thousands of years, long before telescopes existed.

**Child:** And the Pole Star — Dhruva Tara — stays in one place while everything else moves?

**Mother:** It appears almost fixed because it sits nearly directly above Earth's North Pole. As the Earth spins, every other star seems to sweep across the sky, but Polaris barely budges. To find it, look at the two stars forming the front edge of the Big Dipper's bowl, draw an imaginary line through them, and extend it about five times that distance — the star you reach is Polaris.

**Child:** The story says young Dhruva meditated with such determination that he was granted an unshakeable place in the sky!

**Mother:** A wonderful connection between our stories and astronomy. Indian astronomers also divided the sky into 27 **nakshatras** — lunar mansions — based on where the Moon appears each night during its orbit. And the **Jantar Mantar** observatories in Jaipur and Delhi, built by Maharaja Jai Singh II in the 1700s, used gigantic stone instruments to track stars, measure time, and predict eclipses with astonishing accuracy — and all this without any telescope at all!

## The Solar System — Our Cosmic Neighbourhood

**Child:** Maa, I know the planet order — Meri Very Energetic Maa Joyfully Saw Uttar Nakshatra! But why was Pluto removed from the list?

**Mother:** In 2006, the International Astronomical Union set a precise definition for what counts as a planet: it must orbit the Sun, have enough mass to be roughly spherical, and have cleared its orbital neighbourhood of other significant objects. Pluto satisfies the first two, but its orbit passes through a crowded zone of icy bodies — it has not cleared its neighbourhood. So it was reclassified as a dwarf planet.

**Child:** Poor Pluto! It must feel left out.

**Mother:** Ha! Many people felt sentimental about it. But science requires precise definitions. There are now five officially recognised dwarf planets, and Pluto is the most famous among them. The important thing is that our knowledge keeps evolving.

**Child:** Maa, here is something that puzzles me — Venus is farther from the Sun than Mercury, yet Venus is the hottest planet. How?

**Mother:** Great question. Venus has an extremely dense atmosphere made almost entirely of carbon dioxide, which is a powerful greenhouse gas. Heat from the Sun enters but cannot escape — like being inside a car with all windows rolled up on a scorching summer afternoon. Mercury, on the other hand, has virtually no atmosphere, so heat radiates away into space at night. It is a perfect example of how atmosphere matters more than mere distance from the Sun.

## India on the Moon — The Chandrayaan Story

**Child:** Maa, tell me about Chandrayaan-3! I watched the landing on TV and everyone in the room was cheering and crying!

**Mother:** On 23 August 2023, the entire nation held its breath. The Vikram lander, carrying the little Pragyan rover, descended toward the Moon's south pole. When it touched down safely, India became the first country ever to land near the lunar south pole — and the fourth nation to achieve a soft landing on the Moon.

**Child:** Why is the south pole so special?

**Mother:** Scientists believe that permanently shadowed craters near the south pole may harbour **water ice**. If water exists on the Moon, future astronauts could use it for drinking, growing food, and even splitting it into hydrogen and oxygen for rocket fuel. The south pole could be the gateway to a sustained human presence on the Moon.

**Child:** And Chandrayaan-1 in 2008 actually found water on the Moon first?

**Mother:** Yes! Its Moon Impact Probe detected water molecules on the lunar surface — a discovery that transformed our understanding. Before that, many scientists assumed the Moon was bone dry. India's contribution to lunar science has been truly pioneering.

**Child:** And now ISRO is working on Gaganyaan — sending Indian astronauts to space?

**Mother:** That is right. Indian astronauts will be called **Vyomanauts**. When Gaganyaan succeeds, India will join the very exclusive club of nations that have sent humans beyond Earth's atmosphere. From Chandrayaan to Mangalyaan to Gaganyaan — ISRO achieves extraordinary feats with comparatively modest budgets. It proves that vision and dedication matter more than money.

## How Big Is the Universe, Really?

**Child:** Maa, I try to imagine the distances in space, but my brain just gives up. Can you help?

**Mother:** Let us try a scale model. Imagine the Sun is a large amla — about 3 centimetres across. At that scale, Earth would be a tiny mustard seed roughly 3 metres away. Jupiter would be a small ber fruit about 15 metres away. Neptune would be about 90 metres away. And the nearest star, Proxima Centauri? It would be another amla — about 800 kilometres away!

**Child:** 800 kilometres away from a 3-centimetre amla? The emptiness between stars is unbelievable!

**Mother:** And the Milky Way, at this scale, would span tens of crores of kilometres. The observable universe contains hundreds of billions of galaxies like the Milky Way, each with hundreds of billions of stars. The universe is about 13.8 billion years old, it started with the Big Bang, and it is still expanding — galaxies are moving farther apart right now.

**Child:** Are we alone, Maa? Is there life somewhere out there?

**Mother:** That is one of the most profound unanswered questions in all of science. Researchers are hunting for signs of life on Mars, on Jupiter's moon Europa — which has a liquid ocean hidden beneath its icy crust — and on Saturn's moon Enceladus, which shoots water geysers into space. Thousands of exoplanets orbiting other stars have been discovered, some in the habitable zone where liquid water could exist.

**Child:** With so many stars and so many planets, it seems strange that only Earth would have life.

**Mother:** Many scientists share that feeling. But until we find evidence, we cannot be certain. The search continues — through telescopes, space probes, rovers on Mars, and the boundless curiosity of young people like you. Who knows, beta — perhaps your generation will be the one to find that answer.
