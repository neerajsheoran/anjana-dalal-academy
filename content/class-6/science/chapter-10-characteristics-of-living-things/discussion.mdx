## What Makes Something Alive?

**Meera:** Yaar, I was watching ants on the kitchen floor today. They were carrying tiny crumbs in a perfect line. They move, they eat, they look like they know where they are going. But my toy car also moves and my balloon also gets bigger when I blow air in. So what makes the ants alive and the toy car not?

**Arjun:** Good question. My science teacher said scientists have a checklist — seven traits. Move, eat, breathe, grow, throw out waste, react to surroundings, and reproduce. Something is alive only if it does ALL seven.

**Meera:** My balloon grows bigger, but it does not eat or breathe on its own. And my toy car moves, but it cannot grow or have baby toy cars!

**Arjun:** Exactly. Now think about the tulsi plant on your balcony. Looks quiet, na? But it pulls water through its roots, makes its own food using sunlight, grows taller each month, gives out oxygen, bends toward light, and produces seeds that grow into new tulsi.

**Meera:** Wait — tulsi quietly does all seven things? Even though it does not run around?

**Arjun:** Haan. Just like Bruno the street dog who eats roti, barks, runs, grows, breathes. Both tulsi and Bruno are fully alive. They just do life differently.

**Meera:** So "alive" is not about how exciting something looks. It is a checklist.

## Autotrophs, Heterotrophs, and the Food Connection

**Meera:** Arjun, Mummy keeps saying "eat your sabzi, plants are important." But why ARE plants so special compared to other food?

**Arjun:** Because plants are the only ones who can make food from scratch. They grab sunlight using the green stuff in their leaves — chlorophyll — and combine carbon dioxide from air with water from soil. Out comes glucose, a kind of sugar.

**Meera:** And that whole process has a name?

**Arjun:** Photosynthesis. Plants are basically little solar-powered kitchens.

**Meera:** Wah! And we cannot do that?

**Arjun:** No way. No chlorophyll, no solar panels in our skin. Plants are called autotrophs — self-feeders. We are heterotrophs — we have to eat other living things.

**Meera:** So when I eat aloo paratha — wheat is a plant, aloo is a plant — I am a heterotroph eating autotrophs!

**Arjun:** Exactly. And even if you add chicken curry, the chicken itself ate grain. So the energy still started in a plant. Buffaloes eat grass — herbivores. Owls hunt mice — carnivores. We eat both — omnivores. And the little mushrooms that pop up in your garden after monsoon? Decomposers — they break down dead leaves.

**Meera:** So every single meal I eat connects back to a plant and to the sun. That is amazing.

**Arjun:** And that is exactly why protecting forests and farmland matters. They are the base of every plate of food on Earth.

## Respiration — The Hidden Energy Machine

**Meera:** Yaar, teacher said today that respiration is NOT the same as breathing. But all my life I thought they were the same thing!

**Arjun:** Most people mix them up. Breathing is the physical part — chest goes up, air goes in through your nose, then out. Respiration is the chemical reaction happening inside each of your cells.

**Meera:** Inside the cells?

**Arjun:** Haan. Glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide, water, and energy. That energy is what your body actually uses. Brain cells, heart cells, leg cells — all of them are doing respiration non-stop. Day and night.

**Meera:** Okay then explain this — last week during PT period I ran the 200-metre race and my legs started cramping like crazy. Why?

**Arjun:** Your leg muscles were working so hard that they used up oxygen faster than your lungs could deliver it. So the muscle cells switched to anaerobic respiration — breaking down glucose WITHOUT oxygen.

**Meera:** And that produces something different?

**Arjun:** Lactic acid instead of carbon dioxide. That is what made your legs burn. Once you stopped running, oxygen came back and cleared it up.

**Meera:** And Mummy told me the dosa batter rises overnight because of yeast — is THAT also anaerobic respiration?

**Arjun:** Very good memory! Yes, yeast in the batter eats sugar without oxygen, makes carbon dioxide gas and a tiny bit of alcohol. The CO2 bubbles get trapped in the batter and puff it up. That is why our dosas are crispy and idlis are soft.

**Meera:** So the same thing that cramped my legs at Sports Day is making my breakfast fluffy. Crazy.

## J.C. Bose — The Scientist Who Listened to Plants

**Meera:** Arjun, the chapter mentions Jagadish Chandra Bose. Did he REALLY prove that plants can feel things?

**Arjun:** He did something extraordinary, yaar. In the late 1800s, almost every scientist in the world believed plants were just... passive. Like rocks but green. They could not sense anything, could not respond.

**Meera:** That sounds wrong even to me.

**Arjun:** Bose thought so too. He refused to believe it. He built a delicate instrument called the crescograph that could magnify tiny plant movements by thousands of times.

**Meera:** And what did it show?

**Arjun:** When a plant is touched, heated, or cut, the cells at that spot send an electrical signal — kind of like nerve impulses in animals. The signal travels through the plant, and the plant responds. The chhui-mui plant folding its leaves is the most dramatic example, but Bose proved even peepal trees and roses are constantly sensing temperature, light, and moisture.

**Meera:** So plants are quietly aware of their surroundings, just like animals?

**Arjun:** In their own way, yes. Bose said the wall between plant life and animal life is much thinner than anyone imagined. And here is the impressive part — he also did pioneering work on wireless radio waves, years before Marconi got famous for it. But he refused to patent his inventions because he believed scientific knowledge should be shared freely.

**Meera:** Yaar, he sounds like a true hero. Next time I pass the peepal tree in school yard, I will not just walk past it.

**Arjun:** That is the whole point. Bose taught us to LOOK at plants properly.

## Cells — How Life Is Organised

**Meera:** Arjun, if I keep zooming in on a tulsi leaf with a super powerful microscope, what would I see in the end?

**Arjun:** You would eventually see tiny compartments — cells. Each cell is its own little factory carrying out life processes. The cell is the smallest unit that is still alive. Every living thing is made of cells.

**Meera:** Are all organisms made of the same number of cells?

**Arjun:** Not at all! An amoeba living in a pond is just ONE cell. It is a unicellular organism. That single cell does everything by itself — eats, breathes, grows, reproduces.

**Meera:** And we?

**Arjun:** Your body has about 37 lakh crore cells. 37 trillion. Each one doing a specialised job. You are multicellular.

**Meera:** Yaar, that number does not even feel real. How do trillions of cells work together without total chaos?

**Arjun:** Through beautiful organisation. Cells of the same type group together to form tissues — like muscle tissue. Different tissues form organs — like your heart. Organs work together as organ systems — like your circulatory system. And all the systems together make YOU.

**Meera:** Oh! So cells are like individual workers, tissues are small teams, organs are departments, organ systems are whole divisions, and the entire organism is the company!

**Arjun:** Yaar, that is such a sharp way to put it.

**Meera:** And just like a company runs well only when every worker shows up, my body stays healthy only when every cell is doing its job?

**Arjun:** Exactly. That is why food, water, sleep, exercise matter so much. They keep your tiny workers in top shape. Now come, my Daadi made aloo tikki. Time to send fresh glucose to all 37 trillion of them.
