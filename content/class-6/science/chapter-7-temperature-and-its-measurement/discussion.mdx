## Can We Trust Our Skin?

**Meera:** Arjun, try this with me right now. I have three bowls in the kitchen — one with warm water from the geyser, one with ice cubes melting, and one with normal tap water.

**Arjun:** Some weird experiment your science teacher told you?

**Meera:** Just do it, yaar. Put your right hand in the warm bowl. Left hand in the cold one. Keep them there for thirty seconds.

**Arjun:** ...okay, this cold one is making my fingers tingle. The warm one feels nice.

**Meera:** Now quickly take both hands out and dip them together into the tap water.

**Arjun:** Whoa! Wait. My right hand says this water is cool. My left hand says it is warm. SAME water!

**Meera:** I know! Crazy, no?

**Arjun:** But how?

**Meera:** Sir said our skin doesn't actually measure temperature. It only feels the change from what it was used to. Your right hand was warm, so tap water felt cool. Your left hand was cold, so tap water felt warm.

**Arjun:** So our hands are not reliable!

**Meera:** That is why we have thermometers. They give the same number no matter who is holding them.

**Arjun:** Otherwise mummy says "the dal is hot enough" and daadi says "no, it is still cold". Both could be right based on their own hands.

**Meera:** Exactly. The thermometer ends the argument.

## Two Thermometers in Our Home

**Meera:** Arjun, we have that beeping digital thermometer mummy uses when I get fever. And in our science kit at home there is a glass one. They look totally different.

**Arjun:** Different jobs, different design. The digital one is a clinical thermometer. Only made to measure body temperature.

**Meera:** Range?

**Arjun:** Only 35°C to 42°C. Because our body never goes below 35 or above 42, even in really bad fever.

**Meera:** Tight range. And the glass one?

**Arjun:** Laboratory thermometer. Wider range — minus 10°C to 110°C. You use it for science experiments. Hot water, ice melting, anything in a beaker.

**Meera:** Yaar, my teacher said something about a "kink" in the old mercury fever thermometers. What is that?

**Arjun:** It is a tiny narrow twist in the glass tube just above the silver bulb. When the mercury rises because of your fever, the kink works like a one-way gate. Mercury goes up but cannot slide back down.

**Meera:** Oh! So you can take the thermometer out of your mouth and still read it properly.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Otherwise the moment it comes out into cool air, the mercury would fall and you would get the wrong reading.

**Meera:** Then how do you reset it?

**Arjun:** Shake it firmly. The force pushes mercury back past the kink into the bulb. Mummy used to do it before sticking it under my tongue.

**Meera:** Digital ones are simpler then.

**Arjun:** Yes. Press a button, beep, done. No mercury, no shaking, no broken glass on the floor.

## The Difference Between Heat and Temperature

**Meera:** Arjun, in tiffin break today my friend kept saying heat and temperature are the same thing. I felt something was wrong but couldn't explain.

**Arjun:** They are not the same! Very common confusion. Let me try.

**Meera:** Go on.

**Arjun:** Imagine a single burning matchstick. And a big pressure cooker full of hot rajma on the stove.

**Meera:** Okay.

**Arjun:** Which one has the HIGHER temperature?

**Meera:** Matchstick flame for sure. It is super hot — maybe 700 degrees.

**Arjun:** Right. The rajma is only about 95 degrees. But which one has MORE total heat energy?

**Meera:** Hmm... the rajma? Because there is so much of it?

**Arjun:** Exactly! Pressure cooker has litres of rajma. Millions and millions of hot molecules. The matchstick has a tiny flame with very few hot gas molecules.

**Meera:** So if the matchstick touches my finger, it stings but the burn is small.

**Arjun:** Yes. If hot rajma spills on your hand — much worse burn. Because so much more heat energy gets transferred.

**Meera:** So temperature is the intensity of hotness. Heat is the total energy?

**Arjun:** Perfect way to say it.

**Meera:** Like a sparkler on Diwali! The tip is at like 1000 degrees, super high temperature. But when a tiny spark lands on my hand, almost nothing happens.

**Arjun:** Exactly! Because that one spark has almost no total heat. A bucket of warm water at 40 degrees has way more heat energy than a sparkler.

**Meera:** Mind blown, yaar.

## Temperature Scales and Conversions

**Meera:** Arjun, why do we have three different temperature scales? Celsius, Fahrenheit, Kelvin — pick one and stick with it, no?

**Arjun:** Different countries, different times. Anders Celsius from Sweden made the Celsius scale. Water freezes at zero, boils at hundred. Nice and simple.

**Meera:** Then who made Fahrenheit?

**Arjun:** Daniel Fahrenheit from Germany, even earlier. Water freezes at 32 and boils at 212 on his scale. Used a lot in America.

**Meera:** And Kelvin?

**Arjun:** Scientists made that one for very precise lab work. It starts from absolute zero.

**Meera:** Absolute zero! Sounds intense.

**Arjun:** It is! Minus 273.15 Celsius. At that temperature, the tiny particles inside everything basically stop moving. Nothing in the universe can be colder.

**Meera:** Wait. Even in space?

**Arjun:** Even space doesn't quite reach absolute zero. It is the floor of all temperatures.

**Meera:** How do I convert between scales?

**Arjun:** Easy. Celsius to Kelvin — just add 273.15. So 30 Celsius today is 303.15 Kelvin.

**Meera:** And Fahrenheit?

**Arjun:** Multiply Celsius by 9/5, then add 32. So 37 Celsius — our body temperature — becomes 98.6 Fahrenheit.

**Meera:** Let me try one! Water boils at 100 Celsius. So in Fahrenheit it is 100 times 9/5 = 180, plus 32 = 212!

**Arjun:** Perfect. You got it.

**Meera:** Yes! Tell me a hard one.

**Arjun:** Calm down, Einstein. Eat your tiffin first.

## Anna Mani — India's Weather Heroine

**Meera:** Yaar, our textbook mentioned someone called Anna Mani. I had never heard the name before.

**Arjun:** I read about her last month. Brilliant lady. Born in 1918 in Travancore, Kerala. Physicist who joined the India Meteorological Department.

**Meera:** Meteorological — that is weather, right?

**Arjun:** Yes. And at that time very, very few Indian women were in science. She designed and tested over a hundred different weather instruments. Devices for measuring temperature, wind speed, rainfall, sunshine.

**Meera:** Why are weather instruments so important?

**Arjun:** Think about Indian farmers. Their whole life depends on the monsoon. Will it rain on time? Will the temperature suit the crop? Weather forecasts try to answer these questions. And the forecast is only as good as the instruments.

**Meera:** So she connected physics in the lab to the fields of farmers across India.

**Arjun:** Exactly. They call her the "Weather Woman of India".

**Meera:** That is a beautiful title.

**Arjun:** Her story shows that measurement is not just classroom work. It saves crops, warns about cyclones, helps plan water for entire cities.

**Meera:** Next time I see the weather forecast on Papa's phone, I will think of her.

**Arjun:** Same, yaar. Science feels different when you know the real people behind it.
