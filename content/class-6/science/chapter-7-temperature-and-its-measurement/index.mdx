## Can Our Skin Be a Thermometer?

Imagine this: You come home after playing cricket under the blazing June sun in Delhi. Your hands are sweaty and warm. You rush to the kitchen and grab a steel glass of water from the fridge. It feels wonderfully cold! But your mother, who has been sitting inside with the cooler on, picks up the same glass and says, "This water isn't that cold."

The same glass, the same water — but two different feelings. Why?

> **Try this at home:** Fill three bowls — one with warm water (not too hot), one with ice-cold water, and one with tap water at room temperature. Put your right hand in the warm bowl and left hand in the cold bowl for about 30 seconds. Now dip both hands into the tap water bowl. Your right hand says "cool" and your left hand says "warm" — yet it is the same water!

This simple activity proves that our skin judges temperature by **comparison**, not by actual measurement. Our sense of touch is **unreliable** for knowing the true hotness or coldness of an object. We need a proper tool — and a proper number — to describe temperature accurately.

## Understanding Temperature

**Temperature** is a numerical measure of how hot or cold a body is. Think of it as a score on a scale — the higher the score, the hotter the object.

- A cup of chai at 65°C is hotter than lassi at 5°C.
- Ice cream at −18°C in the freezer is colder than water at 25°C.

Temperature is measured with a device called a **thermometer** — a word that comes from Greek: *thermos* (heat) + *metron* (measure).

The SI unit of temperature is the **kelvin (K)**. In daily life, Indians commonly use **degrees Celsius (°C)**. In countries like the USA, **degrees Fahrenheit (°F)** is popular.

## Three Temperature Scales

![Three thermometer scales side by side: Celsius, Fahrenheit, and Kelvin with freezing and boiling points of water marked on each](temperature-scales-comparison.png)

| Scale | Symbol | Where It Is Used |
|-------|--------|-----------------|
| Celsius | °C | Schools, hospitals, weather reports in India |
| Fahrenheit | °F | Body temperature in some countries, USA weather |
| Kelvin | K | Scientific research (SI unit) |

### Key Reference Points on Each Scale

| Condition | Celsius | Fahrenheit | Kelvin |
|-----------|---------|------------|--------|
| Water turns to ice | 0°C | 32°F | 273.15 K |
| Water starts boiling | 100°C | 212°F | 373.15 K |
| Healthy human body | 37.0°C | 98.6°F | 310.15 K |

### How to Convert Between Scales

> **Celsius to Kelvin:** Add 273.15 — so K = °C + 273.15
> **Celsius to Fahrenheit:** Multiply by 9/5 and add 32 — so °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32

**Example:** A summer afternoon in Jaipur is 42°C.
- In Kelvin: 42 + 273.15 = 315.15 K
- In Fahrenheit: (42 × 9/5) + 32 = 75.6 + 32 = 107.6°F

### The Coldest Possible Temperature

Scientists discovered that there is a lowest temperature that nothing in the universe can go below. This is called **absolute zero** — it equals **−273.15°C** or **0 K**. At this extreme cold, particles in matter virtually stop moving. No refrigerator, no machine, nothing can cool anything below absolute zero.

## Types of Thermometers

A **thermometer** is an instrument built to measure temperature accurately and give a numerical reading.

---

### 1. Clinical Thermometer — For the Human Body

![A clinical thermometer and a laboratory thermometer side by side with their ranges labeled: clinical 35-42 degrees C and laboratory minus 10 to 110 degrees C](clinical-and-laboratory-thermometers.png)

When you have a fever, your mother places a thermometer under your tongue or in your armpit. That is a **clinical thermometer**.

**Key features:**
- Its scale covers a **narrow range: 35°C to 42°C** — because the human body stays within this band in health and illness
- Older mercury-based ones have a tiny **kink** (a constriction in the glass tube near the bulb). This kink stops the mercury from sliding back down after you remove the thermometer from the mouth, so the reading stays visible
- After reading, you must shake the mercury back below 35°C before the next use
- Modern **digital clinical thermometers** are safer (no mercury), faster, and beep when the reading is ready

**Steps to use a mercury clinical thermometer:**
1. Wash the thermometer tip with clean water and shake it until the mercury drops below 35°C
2. Place the silver bulb under the tongue (or snugly in the armpit)
3. Wait quietly for 1–2 minutes
4. Remove and read the temperature where the mercury level sits

**Healthy body temperature** is around **37.0°C (98.6°F)**. Doctors consider a consistent reading above **38°C** as a definite fever.

---

### 2. Laboratory Thermometer — For Experiments

In your school science lab, you measure the temperature of hot water, cold mixtures, or chemical reactions using a **laboratory thermometer**.

**Key features:**
- Covers a **wide range: −10°C to 110°C** — enough for most school experiments
- Each small division on the scale is usually **1°C**
- Has **no kink** — so you must read the temperature while the bulb is still dipped in the liquid
- The bulb should hang freely in the liquid, not resting on the container's bottom

**Steps to use:**
1. Hold the thermometer vertically so the bulb is fully immersed in the liquid
2. Make sure the bulb does not touch the walls or bottom of the beaker
3. Wait until the liquid column becomes steady
4. Read the scale at **eye level** — looking from above or below gives a wrong reading (called **parallax error**)

---

### 3. Maximum–Minimum Thermometer

Weather stations use a special thermometer that records both the **hottest and the coldest** temperature reached during a 24-hour period. This helps meteorologists track daily temperature swings.

---

### 4. How Outdoor Air Temperature Is Measured

The thermometer measuring atmospheric temperature sits inside a **Stevenson Screen** — a white-painted box with slatted sides. The white colour reflects sunlight and the slats allow air to flow freely around the thermometer, ensuring an accurate air temperature reading without interference from direct sunlight or rain.

---

## Avoiding Mistakes While Reading a Thermometer

Three golden rules:

1. **Eye level alignment** — Your eyes must be at the same height as the liquid column. Looking up gives a reading that is too low, looking down gives one that is too high. This mistake is called **parallax error**.
2. **Keep the bulb in the substance** (for laboratory thermometers) — Remove it and the reading changes immediately.
3. **Count the scale markings carefully** — Each division could be 0.5°C or 1°C depending on the thermometer. Miscounting leads to wrong readings.

> **Classroom activity idea:** Use a laboratory thermometer to measure:
> - Water straight from a clay matka
> - Water from the kitchen tap
> - Water that has been sitting in the sun for an hour
>
> Record all three readings in a table. Which is warmest? Which is coolest? Can you explain why?

## Heat and Temperature — They Are Not the Same

![A matchstick with high temperature but low heat next to a large pot of warm water with lower temperature but more total heat](heat-vs-temperature.png)

Many students think "heat" and "temperature" mean the same thing. They do not.

| | Heat | Temperature |
|--|------|------------|
| **What is it?** | A form of energy that flows from hotter to colder objects | A measure of how hot or cold something is |
| **SI unit** | Joule (J) | Kelvin (K) |
| **Measured with** | Calorimeter | Thermometer |

**An everyday example to understand the difference:**

A single lit diya (oil lamp) during Diwali has a flame temperature of about 800°C. A large steel pot of dal on the stove is at 90°C. The diya's flame is much hotter (higher temperature), but the pot of dal holds far more total heat energy because it contains a huge amount of hot liquid. If you accidentally touch the diya flame, you feel a sharp sting but pull away quickly. If a full pot of hot dal spills on you, the burn is much worse — because the total heat energy delivered to your skin is enormous.

Temperature is about **intensity**. Heat is about **total energy**.

## Anna Mani — The Weather Pioneer

**Anna Mani (1918–2001)** grew up in Kerala with a deep love for reading and science. She went on to become one of India's most important meteorologists. Working at the **India Meteorological Department (IMD)**, she designed, built, and calibrated over 100 weather instruments — devices that measure temperature, wind speed, sunshine hours, and solar radiation.

Her instruments were installed at weather stations across the country, helping predict monsoons and weather patterns that millions of Indian farmers depend on. She is rightly celebrated as the **"Weather Woman of India"** — a reminder that the accurate measurement of temperature and other atmospheric conditions is not just classroom science, but a lifeline for our nation's agriculture and disaster preparedness.
