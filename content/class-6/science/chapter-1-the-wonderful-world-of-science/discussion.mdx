## What Counts as Science?

**Child:** Maa, people say "science proves this" and "science proves that." But how is science different from just having an opinion?

**Mother:** Good question. An opinion is what someone believes. Science is what can be tested and verified by anyone. If I say "I think mangoes are the best fruit" — that is an opinion, no one can test it. But if I say "a mango seed takes about 8 to 14 days to sprout" — anyone can plant a seed and check. That second kind of statement, which can be tested, belongs to science.

**Child:** So science is about claims that can be checked?

**Mother:** Exactly. And not just checked once — checked again and again by different people. If the same result shows up every time, it becomes reliable knowledge. That is what gives science its strength.

## Everyday Experiments You Already Do

**Child:** Maa, our teacher said everyone uses the scientific method without realising it. But I have never done a "method" — I do not even have a lab!

**Mother:** You used it this morning. Remember when the TV remote was not working?

**Child:** Yes — I pressed the button and nothing happened.

**Mother:** That was your observation. Then what did you do?

**Child:** I thought the batteries might be dead, so I replaced them. And it worked!

**Mother:** There — you observed a problem, made a guess about the cause, tested your guess by changing the batteries, and confirmed it when the TV switched on. That is observe, hypothesise, test, and conclude. You followed the scientific method at the breakfast table.

**Child:** I did not even think of it that way!

**Mother:** That is the point. Science is not a place or a profession — it is a habit of thinking. The vegetable seller at the market does it too. She puts tomatoes in the shade because she has observed that they go bad faster in the sun. She tested it over many days. That is the same logic a food scientist uses in a laboratory.

## Why Does "Fair Testing" Matter?

**Child:** We did an experiment in school today. We wanted to check whether sugar dissolves faster in hot water or cold water. I used hot water and stirred it, and my friend used cold water without stirring. My sugar dissolved first, so we said hot water is better.

**Mother:** Was that a fair test?

**Child:** I think so — my sugar dissolved faster.

**Mother:** But you stirred yours and your friend did not stir. So was it the hot water or the stirring that dissolved the sugar faster?

**Child:** Oh. I cannot tell because I changed two things at once.

**Mother:** Exactly. A fair test means you change only one thing — the variable you want to study — and keep everything else identical. In your case, both of you should use the same amount of water, the same amount of sugar, and the same stirring. The only difference should be the temperature.

**Child:** So if we redo it properly and the hot water still dissolves sugar faster, then we can actually trust the result.

**Mother:** Yes. The whole point of fair testing is to make sure your conclusion is honest. If the test is unfair, the conclusion might be wrong — and wrong conclusions can lead to bad decisions in real life.

## When Scientists Get It Wrong

**Child:** Maa, does science ever make mistakes?

**Mother:** All the time! But that is actually its strength. Science has a built-in way to correct itself. When a scientist shares a finding, other scientists around the world try to repeat the same experiment. If they get different results, they challenge the original finding. This checking process is called peer review.

**Child:** Like how the teacher checks our homework?

**Mother:** Similar, but stricter. Imagine a hundred teachers from different schools all checking the same homework independently. If all of them agree the answer is correct, you can be very confident. That is how scientific peer review works.

**Child:** What happens if a scientist fakes results?

**Mother:** Other scientists will eventually catch it because the fake results will not hold up when tested independently. There have been cases of fraud in science, and they were exposed precisely because science demands verification. No single person's word is accepted without evidence.

**Child:** So science is honest because lots of people check each other.

**Mother:** Yes. It is a system designed around the idea that anyone can be wrong — so everything must be tested by many people. That humility is what makes science trustworthy.

## The Kitchen as a Science Lab

**Child:** Can you give me one more example of science at home? Something I can see right now?

**Mother:** Look at the pressure cooker on the stove. When the lid is sealed, steam builds up inside. That steam increases the pressure, which raises the boiling point of water. So the water inside becomes hotter than 100 degrees, and the food cooks faster.

**Child:** Is that why dal cooks in 10 minutes in a pressure cooker but takes 40 minutes in an open pot?

**Mother:** Exactly. That is physics — the relationship between pressure and boiling point. Now look at the curd setting in the bowl. Last night, I added a spoonful of old curd to warm milk. The bacteria in that spoonful multiplied overnight and turned the whole bowl of milk into curd.

**Child:** That is biology!

**Mother:** And when you squeeze lemon over cut apples to stop them turning brown — that is chemistry. The citric acid in lemon prevents oxidation. Your kitchen demonstrates physics, chemistry, and biology every single day.

**Child:** I will never look at the kitchen the same way again.

## Science Is a Team Sport

**Child:** In movies, there is always one brilliant scientist working alone in a secret lab. Is that how it really works?

**Mother:** Almost never. Real science is teamwork. Think about India's Chandrayaan-3 mission. It took hundreds of scientists — rocket engineers, software developers, geologists, communication specialists — working together for years. No single person could have done it alone.

**Child:** Even small discoveries need teams?

**Mother:** Yes. When a new medicine is developed, chemists design it, biologists test it on cells, doctors run trials with patients, statisticians analyse the data, and regulators review the safety. Each person brings a skill that the others do not have.

**Child:** It is like a cricket team — you need batters, bowlers, fielders, and a wicket-keeper. No one can do every job.

**Mother:** That is a good way to think about it. And just like a cricket team reviews match footage to improve, scientists review each other's work to improve the quality of knowledge. Science grows when people collaborate, question respectfully, and build on each other's ideas.

**Child:** I like that science welcomes questions. You do not have to just accept what someone says.

**Mother:** That is perhaps the most important thing about science. Never stop asking "why" and "how." That curiosity is where every discovery begins.
