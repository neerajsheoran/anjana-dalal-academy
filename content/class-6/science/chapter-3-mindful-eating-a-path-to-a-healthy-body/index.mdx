## Nutrients and Their Role in Our Body

Every time you sit down for a meal — whether it is a steaming plate of rajma-chawal, a bowl of poha topped with peanuts and lemon, or a simple roti with aloo sabzi — your body begins breaking that food into tiny useful substances. These substances are called **nutrients**.

Nutrients are the chemical components in food that our body extracts and uses for energy, growth, repair, and protection against disease. There are seven major categories of nutrients:

1. **Carbohydrates** — the body's primary fuel
2. **Proteins** — builders and repairers of body tissues
3. **Fats** — concentrated energy reserves and protectors
4. **Vitamins** — regulators of body processes
5. **Minerals** — strengtheners of bones, blood, and organs
6. **Dietary fibre (roughage)** — the digestive system's cleaning crew
7. **Water** — the medium in which all life processes occur

> **Think about it:** A typical North Indian thali has roti (carbohydrates), dal (protein), a green vegetable (vitamins and minerals), a spoonful of ghee (fat), salad with cucumber and onion (fibre and water), and a glass of chaach or lassi (water, protein, calcium). Without even knowing nutrition science, generations of Indian families designed meals that covered nearly every nutrient group.

### Carbohydrates — The Body's Preferred Fuel

![Carbohydrates give energy for daily activities](carbohydrates.png)

Your body runs on energy the way a car runs on petrol. Carbohydrates are the nutrient your body turns to first when it needs quick energy — for walking to school, solving a maths problem, or playing kho-kho during recess.

Carbohydrates come in two forms:
- **Starch** — found in rice, wheat (roti, paratha), corn, potato, sweet potato, tapioca
- **Sugars** — found in fruits, sugarcane, honey, jaggery (gur), milk

When you eat a plate of curd-rice or a bowl of upma, your digestive system breaks the starch into glucose, which enters your blood and reaches every cell. Cells use glucose as fuel to carry out their work.

> A labourer carrying heavy loads at a construction site needs far more carbohydrates than a student sitting in a classroom. The amount of each nutrient we need depends on our age, activity level, and body size.

### Proteins — The Body's Construction Workers

![Proteins are the body's building blocks](proteins.png)

If carbohydrates are the fuel, proteins are the bricks and cement. Every muscle in your arm, every strand of your hair, your fingernails, the enzymes in your stomach that digest food, and even the haemoglobin in your blood — all are made from proteins.

Children need relatively more protein than adults because their bodies are actively growing — building new muscle, lengthening bones, and developing organs.

**Protein-rich foods common in Indian kitchens:**
- **Plant sources:** moong dal, masoor dal, chana (chickpeas), rajma (kidney beans), soybean, peanuts, sprouts
- **Animal sources:** eggs, milk, paneer, curd, fish, chicken

> Here is an everyday observation: when someone recovers from a fracture or a surgery, doctors recommend eating more dal, eggs, and paneer. Why? Because the body needs extra protein to rebuild damaged tissues.

### Fats — Stored Energy and Body Shields

![Fats provide concentrated energy and protect organs](fats.png)

Fats are the most concentrated source of energy. Gram for gram, fat provides more than double the energy of carbohydrates. But fat does much more than supply energy:

- A thin layer of fat beneath your skin insulates your body and keeps you warm during winter
- Fat cushions delicate organs like the kidneys, heart, and brain
- Certain vitamins (A, D, E, and K) dissolve only in fat — without dietary fat, your body cannot absorb them

**Common fat sources in Indian cooking:** mustard oil, groundnut oil, coconut oil, sesame oil (til), ghee, butter, cream, nuts (almonds, walnuts, cashews), seeds (flax, sunflower)

> Every region of India has its own traditional cooking fat. Kerala uses coconut oil, Bengal prefers mustard oil, Rajasthan relies on ghee, and Tamil Nadu often uses sesame oil. Each of these fats has a slightly different nutritional profile, but all serve the same basic functions.

### Vitamins — The Invisible Guardians

![Vitamins regulate essential body processes](vitamins.png)

Vitamins are needed in very small quantities, yet without them the body falls apart. Think of vitamins as traffic signals in a busy city — tiny compared to the roads and buildings, but if they stop working, chaos follows.

| Vitamin | What It Does | Good Indian Sources |
|---------|-------------|---------------------|
| Vitamin A | Maintains healthy eyesight, keeps skin and mucous membranes strong | Papaya, pumpkin, sweet potato, dark green leafy vegetables (methi, palak), milk, liver |
| Vitamin B complex | Helps cells produce energy from food, keeps the nervous system healthy | Whole wheat, bajra, jowar, pulses, groundnuts, milk, eggs |
| Vitamin C | Strengthens the immune system, helps wounds heal faster, keeps gums healthy | Amla (Indian gooseberry), guava, lime, tomato, capsicum, drumstick (moringa) |
| Vitamin D | Helps the body absorb calcium, essential for strong bones and teeth | Sunlight on skin (the best free source!), egg yolk, fish, fortified milk |
| Vitamin K | Helps blood clot properly when you get a cut | Green leafy vegetables, soybean oil |

> **Did you know?** Drumstick leaves (moringa) — commonly used in sambar in South India — contain significant amounts of Vitamin A, Vitamin C, calcium, and iron. A single tree in the backyard can be a nutritional powerhouse.

### Minerals — Small Quantities, Massive Impact

![Minerals are essential for bones, blood, and organs](minerals.png)

Minerals are inorganic elements that your body cannot manufacture on its own. You must get them from food.

- **Calcium** — The most abundant mineral in the body. It builds and maintains bones and teeth. Growing children who do not get enough calcium may develop weak, brittle bones. Sources: milk, curd, paneer, ragi (finger millet), sesame seeds (til), green leafy vegetables.

- **Iron** — A key component of haemoglobin, the molecule inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from your lungs to every tissue. Without enough iron, cells starve for oxygen and you feel exhausted. Sources: jaggery (gur), garden cress seeds (halim), dates (khajoor), beetroot, green leafy vegetables, organ meats.

- **Iodine** — Required by the thyroid gland to produce hormones that control metabolism and growth. Deficiency causes the thyroid to swell, a condition called goitre. Source: iodised salt (this is why the government promotes iodised salt across India).

- **Phosphorus** — Works alongside calcium to strengthen bones and teeth, and helps in energy production. Sources: dal, milk, eggs, fish, nuts.

### Dietary Fibre (Roughage) — The Unsung Hero

![Dietary fibre aids digestion](dietary-fibre.png)

Dietary fibre is the portion of plant food that our digestive enzymes cannot break down. It passes through the stomach and intestines largely intact. This might seem pointless — why eat something the body cannot digest?

The answer: fibre adds bulk to food, which helps the intestines grip and push food along. Without enough fibre, waste moves sluggishly, causing constipation and discomfort.

**Fibre-rich foods:** whole grains (brown rice, whole wheat atta, oats), vegetables (beans, carrots, brinjal), fruits eaten with skin (guava, apple, pear), pulses, sprouts

> A common dietary mistake in modern India: replacing whole wheat roti with maida-based naan or white bread. Maida is wheat flour stripped of its fibre and bran. It provides carbohydrates but almost no fibre, leading to digestive problems over time.

### Water — The River of Life Inside You

![Water is essential for all life processes](water.png)

About two-thirds of your body weight is water. Blood is mostly water. Saliva, digestive juices, sweat, and urine are all water-based. Every chemical reaction in your body takes place in a watery environment.

Water performs several critical jobs:
- Transports dissolved nutrients to cells and carries waste products away
- Regulates body temperature through sweating
- Lubricates joints and keeps tissues moist
- Aids digestion by helping dissolve food

You get water not only from drinking it directly but also from foods — watermelon, cucumber, oranges, buttermilk, dal, and cooked rice all contain significant water.

> **Practical tip:** On a hot summer day, traditional Indian drinks like nimbu paani (lemon water with salt and sugar), aam panna (raw mango drink), or jaljeera provide both hydration and essential minerals lost through sweating.

## Deficiency Diseases — When Nutrients Go Missing

When the body does not receive enough of a particular nutrient over a long period, it develops a **deficiency disease**. These diseases are entirely preventable — they occur not because of germs or infections, but simply because the diet lacks something essential.

| Disease | Nutrient Missing | Symptoms | Prevention |
|---------|-----------------|----------|------------|
| **Night blindness** | Vitamin A | Difficulty seeing in dim light, dry eyes | Eat papaya, pumpkin, carrots, dark leafy greens |
| **Scurvy** | Vitamin C | Bleeding gums, slow wound healing, weakness | Eat amla, guava, citrus fruits, tomatoes |
| **Rickets** | Vitamin D and Calcium | Soft, bent bones in children, bowed legs | Get regular sunlight, eat dairy products, ragi |
| **Anaemia** | Iron | Tiredness, pale skin, breathlessness, weakness | Eat jaggery, dates, green vegetables, beetroot |
| **Goitre** | Iodine | Swollen neck (enlarged thyroid gland) | Use iodised salt in all cooking |
| **Kwashiorkor** | Protein (severe) | Swollen belly, thin limbs, discoloured hair, lethargy | Ensure adequate dal, eggs, milk, pulses in diet |
| **Marasmus** | Protein and Calories (severe) | Extreme thinness, wasted muscles, very low body weight | Ensure sufficient food intake with protein and energy |

> **Kwashiorkor and Marasmus** are severe forms of malnutrition seen mostly in very young children who do not get enough food. Kwashiorkor occurs when a child gets some calories (usually from starchy foods) but almost no protein. Marasmus occurs when both calories and protein are severely lacking. Both are tragically common in regions affected by poverty and famine.

**An important distinction:** Being overweight is also a form of malnutrition. A child who eats too many biscuits, chips, and sugary drinks but very few vegetables and dal may look well-fed but can still be deficient in iron, calcium, and vitamins. This is sometimes called "hidden hunger."

## Testing Food for Nutrients

How do scientists determine which nutrients are present in a particular food? They use specific chemical tests. Here are three tests you can perform in your school laboratory or even at home (with supervision for the protein test).

### Test 1: Detecting Starch — The Iodine Test

**Principle:** Iodine solution reacts with starch to produce a distinctive blue-black colour.

**Materials needed:** Dilute iodine solution (available at a chemist), a white plate, food samples

**Procedure:**
1. Place a small amount of the food item on a white plate
2. Add two or three drops of iodine solution onto the food
3. Observe the colour change

**Interpreting results:**
- **Blue-black colour appears** — the food contains starch
- **No colour change (iodine stays brown)** — the food does not contain starch

**Try this at home:** Test cooked rice, a piece of roti, a slice of raw potato, and a piece of banana. Compare these with a slice of tomato, a piece of paneer, and a drop of cooking oil. Record which ones test positive for starch and which do not.

### Test 2: Detecting Fat — The Paper Rub Test

**Principle:** Fat seeps into paper and creates a translucent (semi-transparent) spot that does not dry out.

**Materials needed:** A sheet of plain white paper (not glossy), food samples

**Procedure:**
1. Take a small quantity of the food and press or rub it firmly against the paper
2. Set the paper aside for five minutes to let any water evaporate
3. Hold the paper up against a light source (window or lamp)

**Interpreting results:**
- **A greasy, translucent patch that does not disappear** — fat is present
- **No patch, or a wet patch that dries away** — no fat (or very little fat)

**Try this at home:** Rub a piece of cashew nut, a drop of ghee, a piece of fried pakora, and a slice of cucumber on separate areas of the same paper. After five minutes, observe which spots remain translucent.

### Test 3: Detecting Protein — The Biuret Test

**Principle:** Copper sulphate combined with an alkali (sodium hydroxide) reacts with protein to produce a violet or purple colour.

**Materials needed:** Copper sulphate solution, sodium hydroxide (caustic soda) solution, test tube, food sample dissolved or mashed in water

**Procedure:**
1. Crush or dissolve the food sample in a small amount of water in a test tube
2. Add ten drops of dilute sodium hydroxide solution and shake gently
3. Add two to three drops of dilute copper sulphate solution
4. Observe the colour

**Interpreting results:**
- **Violet or purple colour** — protein is present
- **Blue colour remains unchanged** — protein is absent

> **Safety note:** This test uses chemicals that can irritate skin and eyes. Always perform it under a teacher's supervision, wear gloves if available, and never taste the chemicals.

**Try this in the lab:** Test samples of boiled dal water, milk, egg white dissolved in water, and plain sugar water. Predict which will test positive for protein before running the test, then verify your predictions.

## Balanced Diet and Healthy Eating

### What Is a Balanced Diet?

![What is a balanced diet](balanced-diet.png)

A **balanced diet** is one that provides all seven nutrient groups — carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, fibre, and water — in appropriate quantities suited to a person's age, gender, and level of physical activity.

No single food contains all nutrients in the right proportions. That is why variety in our meals is not a luxury — it is a biological necessity.

### Building a Balanced Plate — The Indian Way

![A balanced plate with cereals, pulses, vegetables, dairy, and fats](balanced-plate.png)

India's traditional thali system is an excellent model of a balanced diet:

| Thali Component | Primary Nutrients Supplied |
|----------------|---------------------------|
| Roti or rice | Carbohydrates, some protein, B vitamins |
| Dal or sambar | Protein, iron, B vitamins, fibre |
| Seasonal vegetable sabzi | Vitamins A and C, minerals, fibre |
| Curd or raita | Calcium, protein, probiotics (good bacteria) |
| Salad (onion, carrot, cucumber, lemon) | Vitamin C, fibre, water |
| Small amount of ghee or oil | Fat, helps absorb vitamins A, D, E, K |
| Pickle or chutney (small quantity) | Flavour, some vitamins, aids appetite |

Different regions compose their thalis differently — a Gujarati thali includes kadhi and rotla, a Bengali thali features fish and mustard preparations, a Rajasthani thali has dal-baati-churma, and a South Indian meal may centre around rice with rasam, kootu, and poriyal — but the underlying nutritional principle remains the same: **include something from every food group**.

### Why Does a Balanced Diet Matter?

- **For children:** Growing bodies need adequate protein for muscle development, calcium and Vitamin D for lengthening bones, iron for increasing blood volume, and carbohydrates for the tremendous energy children expend
- **For adults:** Maintaining body weight, repairing daily wear-and-tear of tissues, and keeping the immune system strong
- **For elderly people:** Preventing bone loss (osteoporosis), maintaining muscle mass, and supporting heart health

> A common misconception is that eating more food means better nutrition. In reality, a child who eats three plates of plain rice gets plenty of carbohydrates but may be severely deficient in protein, vitamins, and minerals. **Quality and variety matter more than quantity.**

### The Role of Cooking in Nutrition

![Cooking plays important nutritional roles](cooking-nutrition.png)

Cooking does more than make food tasty. It plays several important nutritional roles:

- **Kills harmful germs:** Boiling, roasting, and frying destroy bacteria and parasites that may be present in raw food
- **Makes nutrients accessible:** Cooking softens plant cell walls, making it easier for our digestive system to extract nutrients. Raw dal is nearly impossible to digest; cooked dal releases its protein efficiently
- **Improves absorption:** Cooking tomatoes in oil, for example, actually increases the availability of lycopene (a beneficial compound). Adding a squeeze of lemon (Vitamin C) to cooked spinach helps the body absorb its iron better
- **Removes toxins:** Some foods contain natural toxins when raw — certain beans, for instance, must be soaked and boiled thoroughly before eating

However, overcooking — especially boiling vegetables in excess water and then discarding that water — can destroy heat-sensitive vitamins like Vitamin C and some B vitamins. Traditional Indian methods like steaming (idli), light sauteing (sabzi), and pressure cooking (dal) are generally good at preserving nutrients.

### Food Preservation — Making Food Last

![Methods of preserving food](food-preservation.png)

Fresh food spoils because bacteria, fungi, and enzymes break it down over time. Preservation methods slow or stop this spoilage.

| Method | How It Works | Indian Examples |
|--------|-------------|-----------------|
| Sun-drying | Removes moisture so microbes cannot grow | Papad, dried mango (amchur), dried methi, dried red chillies |
| Pickling | Acid (vinegar/lemon) and oil create an environment hostile to bacteria | Mango pickle (aam ka achaar), lemon pickle, mixed vegetable pickle |
| Adding salt or sugar | High salt or sugar concentration draws water out of microbes, killing them | Salted fish, murabba (amla or carrot preserved in sugar syrup) |
| Refrigeration | Low temperature slows bacterial growth | Storing milk, cooked food, fresh vegetables |
| Boiling and sealing | Kills microbes and seals food from air | Jam, tomato sauce, canned foods |

> Indian households have practised food preservation for centuries. Drying vegetables on rooftops during summer, preparing pickles and murabbas, and storing grains with dried neem leaves (a natural insect repellent) are all traditional preservation techniques based on sound scientific principles.

## Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Nutrients** | Chemical substances in food that the body uses for energy, growth, repair, and regulation |
| **Carbohydrates** | Energy-providing nutrients found in grains, potatoes, fruits, and sugar |
| **Proteins** | Body-building nutrients found in dal, pulses, eggs, milk, and meat |
| **Fats** | Concentrated energy-providing nutrients found in oils, ghee, nuts, and seeds |
| **Vitamins** | Nutrients required in small amounts that regulate body processes (A, B, C, D, K) |
| **Minerals** | Inorganic nutrients like calcium, iron, iodine, and phosphorus essential for body functions |
| **Dietary fibre** | Indigestible plant material that aids digestion and prevents constipation |
| **Balanced diet** | A diet containing all nutrients in appropriate proportions for a person's needs |
| **Deficiency disease** | A disease caused by prolonged lack of a specific nutrient in the diet |
| **Malnutrition** | A condition resulting from eating too little, too much, or the wrong combination of nutrients |
| **Food preservation** | Techniques used to slow or prevent spoilage of food |
