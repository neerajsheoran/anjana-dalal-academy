## Nutrients and Their Role in Our Body

**Meera:** Yaar, look at my tiffin today. Just two rotis and aloo sabzi. Mummy was in a hurry. That is enough, na?

**Arjun:** Hmm. Roti is wheat, so it gives you carbohydrates — energy basically. Aloo also has tons of starch, more carbohydrates. So your dabba has energy, energy, and more energy.

**Meera:** What is wrong with that? I will not feel hungry.

**Arjun:** Not feeling hungry and getting proper food are two different things. Our bodies are still growing. Bones getting longer, muscles getting stronger. For all that you need protein. Where is the protein in your dabba?

**Meera:** Oh. I did not add any dal or paneer or egg.

**Arjun:** Exactly. And what about vitamins and minerals? Eyes need Vitamin A. Bones need calcium. Blood needs iron. Plain aloo and roti cannot give you all that.

**Meera:** So what should I have packed?

**Arjun:** Let us redesign your dabba. Keep the roti, that is your carbohydrate. Instead of plain aloo sabzi, try beans-gajar sabzi. Carrots give Vitamin A, beans give fibre. Add a small box of dal for protein. Throw in a guava or one amla for Vitamin C. Maybe a small cup of dahi for calcium.

**Meera:** That sounds like a LOT of food, yaar! My dabba will not even close.

**Arjun:** No no, just a tiny portion of each. The idea is variety, not quantity. Think of your body like a building under construction. You cannot build it with only cement. You need bricks, steel rods, paint, wires, pipes.

**Meera:** And each nutrient is a different building material?

**Arjun:** Exactly. Skip any one and the building has a weak spot somewhere.

**Meera:** So a balanced diet just means a little of everything?

**Arjun:** Yes. All seven groups — carbs, protein, fat, vitamins, minerals, fibre, water — in the right amounts. Look at your plate and ask, do I have something from grains, something from dal or dairy, something green, something colourful, a little fat? If yes, you are on track.

## Deficiency Diseases — When Nutrients Go Missing

**Meera:** Arjun, today teacher told us about a disease called scurvy. Sailors used to get it on long sea trips. Their gums would bleed and wounds would not heal. So scary!

**Arjun:** I know about that one. Scurvy happens when the body goes without Vitamin C for too long. On those old ships, sailors ate only dried meat and biscuits for months. No fresh fruit, no vegetables. So they ran out of Vitamin C.

**Meera:** How did they finally figure it out?

**Arjun:** A British navy doctor noticed that sailors who ate lemons and limes did not get the disease. He started giving lime juice to all the sailors. The disease almost vanished. This was even before anyone knew what a vitamin was!

**Meera:** Whoa. So they fixed it without knowing the science behind it?

**Arjun:** Yes. Later, scientists figured out it was Vitamin C, and they found it in citrus fruits, amla, guava — basically everything Daadi tells us to eat in winter.

**Meera:** Are there other diseases like this — caused by missing nutrients?

**Arjun:** Yes. If a kid does not get enough Vitamin D and calcium, their bones stay soft and bend under the body's weight. It is called rickets. You sometimes see bowed legs. The simplest fix? Sunlight! When the sun hits your skin, your body actually makes its own Vitamin D.

**Meera:** That is why Mummy keeps telling me to play outside instead of being on the phone.

**Arjun:** And iron? Mummy makes me eat palak even though I hate it. She says it has iron.

**Meera:** Iron deficiency causes anaemia. Your red blood cells have a thing called haemoglobin, and iron sits inside it. Haemoglobin carries oxygen from lungs to everywhere — brain, muscles, heart. Without iron, less oxygen reaches your cells, and you feel tired all the time.

**Arjun:** That is why people who eat less iron feel constantly weak. And what about goitre? I saw a picture once — someone with a really swollen neck.

**Meera:** That is from no iodine. The thyroid gland in your neck needs iodine. Without it, the gland swells up trying to make up for the shortage. That is why the government made sure that all our table salt has iodine added.

**Arjun:** So all these diseases — scurvy, rickets, anaemia, goitre — all preventable just by eating right?

**Meera:** Every single one! No medicine needed. Just good food. That is the whole point of mindful eating.

## Testing Food for Nutrients

**Meera:** Arjun, today we did such a cool lab experiment! We tested different foods to see which nutrients they have.

**Arjun:** Wait what! Tell me everything. What did you test first?

**Meera:** The starch test. Teacher gave us a bottle of iodine solution — brownish-yellow liquid. We put a piece of boiled potato on a plate and added a few drops of iodine. It turned BLUE-BLACK! Like ink!

**Arjun:** That is because potato has a ton of starch, which is a carbohydrate. Iodine has this special reaction with starch that makes that colour.

**Meera:** We tested paneer next. The iodine just stayed brown. No reaction at all.

**Arjun:** Right. Paneer has protein and fat, but very little starch. What was the next test?

**Meera:** The fat test! This one was easy. We rubbed a peanut on white paper, pressed hard, and waited. When we held the paper to the window light, there was a greasy patch you could almost see through. Teacher called it translucent.

**Arjun:** Translucent patch means fat is present. Fat soaks into paper and stays there forever — it does not evaporate like water.

**Meera:** Yes. We also rubbed an apple piece. It made a wet spot but it just dried up after a few minutes. No oily patch. So apple has water but not much fat.

**Arjun:** Smart, you actually saw the difference. What about protein test?

**Meera:** That one teacher did for us because it uses chemicals. She mixed dal water in a test tube, added some sodium hydroxide, then a few drops of copper sulphate. The liquid turned VIOLET! She said violet means protein is there.

**Arjun:** That is called the biuret test. Cool name, na?

**Meera:** She also tested sugar water for comparison. It stayed blue, no violet. Because sugar has no protein.

**Arjun:** So now you know three tests — iodine for starch, paper rub for fat, biuret for protein. Quick challenge. If you tested MILK, which tests do you think it would pass?

**Meera:** Hmm. Milk has protein, so the biuret test should turn violet. Milk has fat, especially full-cream milk, so the paper test should show a greasy patch. But milk does not have much starch, so iodine should probably not change.

**Arjun:** Excellent! You just did exactly what scientists do — guessed first using what you know, then plan to test it. That is science right there.

## Balanced Diet and Healthy Eating

**Meera:** Arjun, I am going to the vegetable market with Mummy this evening. She said I can choose what to buy.

**Arjun:** Nice! But here is a challenge — instead of picking just whatever you like, try to build a balanced meal. What would you start with?

**Meera:** Rice or atta for rotis, so carbohydrates for energy.

**Arjun:** Good. Next?

**Meera:** Dal — maybe masoor or toor — for protein. And maybe some paneer too because paneer has both protein and fat.

**Arjun:** Great. Vitamins and minerals?

**Meera:** Palak because it has iron. Carrots for Vitamin A. Tomatoes too. And pumpkin — I think I read pumpkin has Vitamin A as well.

**Arjun:** You are building a SUPER colourful plate. Actually that is a great trick — the more colours on your plate, the more vitamins and minerals you are probably eating. Orange foods like pumpkin and carrot tend to have Vitamin A. Dark green leaves have iron and calcium. Red tomatoes have Vitamin C.

**Meera:** What about fruit?

**Arjun:** Guavas! They are in season. Guava has more Vitamin C than even oranges. And bananas for quick energy and potassium.

**Meera:** One thing I have been wondering. Does cooking destroy the nutrients? If yes, should we just eat everything raw?

**Arjun:** Good question. Cooking does reduce some nutrients — Vitamin C is heat-sensitive, so boiling vegetables for too long can kill it. But cooking also does great things. It kills germs. It breaks down tough fibres so your body can actually use the nutrients. Think about raw dal — could you even eat it?

**Meera:** No way! It would be like chewing stones.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Cooking makes dal soft and digestible, releasing all that protein. The trick is to cook smartly — do not overboil, use just enough water, eat fresh rather than reheated.

**Meera:** What about preservation? Mummy makes mango pickle every summer and dries papad on the terrace in May. Is that science too?

**Arjun:** Totally. When you dry papad in the sun, you are removing moisture. Bacteria and fungi need water to grow, so dried food lasts forever. Pickle works differently — the oil, salt, and spices make a place where harmful microbes cannot survive. Salt draws water out of the mango pieces through osmosis.

**Meera:** So Daadi was basically a food scientist all along?

**Arjun:** In a real way, yes. She knows from experience and tradition what science has only recently figured out. The goal is the same — keep food safe and tasty for as long as possible, especially for the seasons when something is not fresh. Daadis everywhere were running food labs in their kitchens long before there were labs.
