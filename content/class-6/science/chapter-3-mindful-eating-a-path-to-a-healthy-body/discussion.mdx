## Nutrients and Their Role in Our Body

**Child:** Maa, I packed my lunch box today — just two rotis and potato sabzi. That is enough, right?

**Mother:** Hmm, let me think about that. Roti is made from wheat, so it gives you carbohydrates — that is your energy for the day. Potato also has a lot of starch, which is another carbohydrate. So your lunch box has energy, energy, and more energy. But what about everything else your body needs?

**Child:** What else does it need? I will not feel hungry with two rotis!

**Mother:** Not feeling hungry and being properly nourished are two very different things. Your body is growing right now — your bones are getting longer, your muscles are getting stronger, your brain is developing. For all of that, you need protein. Where is the protein in your lunch box?

**Child:** Oh. I did not add any dal or paneer or egg.

**Mother:** Exactly. And what about vitamins and minerals? Your eyes need Vitamin A. Your immune system needs Vitamin C to fight off infections. Your bones need calcium. Your blood needs iron. Potato sabzi and roti alone cannot provide all of these.

**Child:** So what should I add?

**Mother:** Let us redesign your lunch box together. Keep the roti — that is your carbohydrate. Replace plain potato sabzi with a mixed vegetable — say beans and carrot sabzi, which gives you Vitamin A from the carrots and fibre from the beans. Add a small box of dal or a piece of paneer for protein. Throw in a guava or some amla for Vitamin C. And maybe a small cup of curd for calcium.

**Child:** That sounds like a lot of food, Maa!

**Mother:** Not really — small portions of each. The idea is variety, not volume. Think of your body as a building under construction. You cannot build it with only cement. You need bricks, steel rods, paint, wiring, plumbing. Each nutrient is a different building material. Skip any one of them and the building has a weakness.

**Child:** So a balanced diet means having a little of everything?

**Mother:** A balanced diet means having all seven nutrient groups — carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, fibre, and water — in the right amounts for your body. A simple test: look at your plate and ask, does it have something from grains, something from pulses or dairy, something green, something colourful, and a little fat? If yes, you are on the right track.

## Deficiency Diseases — When Nutrients Go Missing

**Child:** Maa, today our teacher told us about a disease called scurvy. Sailors used to get it on long sea voyages. Their gums would bleed and their wounds would not heal. It sounded terrible!

**Mother:** Scurvy happens when the body goes without Vitamin C for too long. On long voyages, sailors ate only dried meat and biscuits — no fresh fruits or vegetables for months. Their bodies ran out of Vitamin C, and without it, the connective tissues that hold the body together start breaking down. That is why gums bled and wounds would not close.

**Child:** How did they figure out it was Vitamin C?

**Mother:** A British naval doctor noticed that sailors who ate citrus fruits — lemons and limes — did not get scurvy. He began giving lime juice to all sailors, and the disease virtually disappeared. That was before anyone even knew what a vitamin was! Later, scientists identified the exact substance — Vitamin C — and traced it to citrus fruits, amla, guava, and other fresh produce.

**Child:** Are there other diseases like that — caused by missing nutrients?

**Mother:** Several. If a child does not get enough Vitamin D and calcium, their bones remain soft and bend under the body's weight. This is called rickets — you might notice bowed legs in affected children. The simplest prevention? Sunlight. When sunlight hits your skin, your body manufactures its own Vitamin D. That and eating calcium-rich foods like curd, milk, and ragi keeps bones strong.

**Child:** What about iron? You always tell me to eat my greens.

**Mother:** Iron deficiency causes anaemia. Your red blood cells contain a molecule called haemoglobin, and iron is at its core. Haemoglobin picks up oxygen from your lungs and delivers it everywhere — your brain, your muscles, your heart. Without enough iron, haemoglobin levels drop, less oxygen reaches your cells, and you feel constantly tired, dizzy, and weak. It is one of the most common nutritional problems in India, especially among girls and women.

**Child:** And what is goitre? I saw a picture in our textbook — someone with a swollen neck.

**Mother:** Goitre is caused by lack of iodine. Your thyroid gland, which sits in your neck, needs iodine to produce hormones that control how fast your body uses energy. Without enough iodine, the thyroid swells up, trying to compensate. That swelling is goitre. This is exactly why the government made sure that the salt we buy is iodised — a tiny amount of iodine is added to table salt so everyone gets enough.

**Child:** So all these diseases — scurvy, rickets, anaemia, goitre — they are all preventable just by eating the right foods?

**Mother:** Every single one of them. That is the power of mindful eating. When you understand what each nutrient does and where to find it, you can protect yourself from these diseases simply by choosing your meals wisely. No medicine needed — just good food.

## Testing Food for Nutrients

**Child:** Maa, today in the science lab we did the most interesting experiment! We tested different foods to find out which nutrients they contain.

**Mother:** Tell me everything. What did you test first?

**Child:** We started with the starch test. Our teacher gave us a bottle of iodine solution — it is a brownish-yellow liquid. We placed a piece of boiled potato on a plate and put a few drops of iodine on it. And guess what — it turned blue-black! Almost like ink!

**Mother:** That is because potato is loaded with starch, which is a type of carbohydrate. Iodine has a special chemical reaction with starch that produces that blue-black colour. Did you test anything that did not turn blue-black?

**Child:** Yes! We tried it on a piece of paneer and it stayed the same brownish colour. No reaction at all. So paneer does not have starch.

**Mother:** Right. Paneer has protein and fat, but very little starch. What was the next test?

**Child:** The fat test! This one was simple. We rubbed a peanut on a piece of white paper, pressed hard, and waited for a few minutes. When we held the paper against the window light, there was a greasy patch that you could almost see through — translucent, our teacher called it.

**Mother:** And that greasy translucent patch means fat is present. Fat soaks into paper and stays there — it does not evaporate like water does. Did you test something without fat?

**Child:** We rubbed a piece of apple on the paper. There was a slight wet spot but after a few minutes it dried up completely. No oily patch. So apple has water but not much fat.

**Mother:** Smart observation — distinguishing between a water spot and a fat spot. What about the protein test?

**Child:** That one the teacher did for us because it uses chemicals. She mixed some dal water in a test tube, added some sodium hydroxide solution, then a few drops of copper sulphate. The liquid turned violet! She said that violet colour means protein is present.

**Mother:** That is called the Biuret test. The violet colour appears because the copper sulphate and sodium hydroxide react with the protein molecules. Did she test anything that did not turn violet?

**Child:** She tested sugar water as a comparison. It just stayed blue — no violet at all. Because sugar has no protein.

**Mother:** So now you know three simple tests: iodine for starch, paper rub for fat, and biuret for protein. Here is a challenge for you — if you tested milk, which tests do you think it would pass?

**Child:** Hmm. Milk has protein, so the biuret test should turn violet. Milk has fat — especially full-cream milk — so the paper test should show a greasy patch. But I do not think milk has much starch, so the iodine test probably would not change colour.

**Mother:** Excellent reasoning. You just did something very scientific — you formed a hypothesis based on what you know, and now you could test it to confirm. That is exactly how science works, even in the kitchen.

## Balanced Diet and Healthy Eating

**Child:** Maa, we are going to the vegetable market today, right? Can I help choose what to buy?

**Mother:** Absolutely. But here is a challenge — instead of just picking what tastes good, try to pick ingredients that together make a balanced meal. What would you start with?

**Child:** Rice or wheat atta for rotis — that is our carbohydrate for energy.

**Mother:** Good. What next?

**Child:** Some dal — maybe masoor or toor dal — for protein. And maybe some paneer too, since I know paneer has both protein and fat.

**Mother:** Great choices. Now, what about vitamins and minerals? Those come mainly from fresh vegetables and fruits.

**Child:** I would pick spinach because you said it has iron. And carrots for Vitamin A. And maybe some tomatoes? Oh, and pumpkin — I remember reading that it has Vitamin A too.

**Mother:** You are building a very colourful plate, and that is actually a useful rule of thumb — the more colours on your plate, the wider the range of vitamins and minerals you are probably getting. Deep orange foods like pumpkin and carrot tend to have Vitamin A. Dark green leafy vegetables are rich in iron and calcium. Red tomatoes have Vitamin C and lycopene.

**Child:** What about fruit?

**Mother:** Let us get some guavas — they are in season right now, and guava is an outstanding source of Vitamin C. Even better than oranges. And some bananas for quick energy and potassium.

**Child:** Maa, one thing I have been wondering — does cooking destroy the nutrients in food? If it does, should we just eat everything raw?

**Mother:** That is a thoughtful question. Cooking does reduce some nutrients — Vitamin C is sensitive to heat, so boiling vegetables for a long time in a lot of water can reduce it. But cooking also does many beneficial things. It kills germs that could make you sick. It breaks down tough plant fibres so your body can actually access the nutrients inside. Think about raw dal versus cooked dal — could you eat raw dal?

**Child:** No way! It would be like chewing pebbles.

**Mother:** Exactly. Cooking makes dal soft and digestible, releasing all that protein for your body. The trick is to cook smartly — do not overboil vegetables, use just enough water, and eat freshly cooked food rather than reheated leftovers when possible. Steaming and light stir-frying preserve more vitamins than deep frying or prolonged boiling.

**Child:** What about food preservation? You make mango pickle every summer and dry papad in the sun. Is that science too?

**Mother:** It absolutely is. When you dry papad in the sun, you are removing moisture. Bacteria and fungi need moisture to grow, so dried food lasts much longer. When I make pickle, the combination of oil, salt, and spices like mustard and fenugreek creates conditions where harmful microbes cannot survive. Adding salt draws water out of the mango pieces through a process called osmosis. Sugar-based preserves like murabba work the same way.

**Child:** So our grandmothers were basically food scientists!

**Mother:** In a very real sense, yes. They understood preservation through experience and tradition. Modern food science has just given names and explanations to what they already practised. The goal is the same — keep food safe and nutritious for as long as possible, especially for seasons when certain ingredients are not available fresh.
