## Collecting and Organising Data

Imagine your school is planning its Annual Sports Day and the sports teacher wants to know which events students are most excited about. She could guess, or she could ask every student and record their answers. The second approach gives her **data** — solid facts she can rely on to make decisions.

> **Data** is a collection of numbers, facts, or observations gathered with a clear purpose in mind.

Data surrounds us in daily life. A vegetable vendor tracks how many kilograms of tomatoes, onions, and potatoes he sells each day. The Indian Railways records how many passengers travel on each route every month. Your school maintains attendance registers, mark sheets, and health records. All of these are examples of data being collected and used.

### Why Collect Data?

Without data, decisions become guesswork. Consider these situations:

- A school canteen manager wants to decide how many plates of *rajma-chawal* versus *chole-bhature* to prepare each day. If she tracks orders for two weeks, the data will guide her.
- A city corporation wants to know which neighbourhoods need more streetlights. They collect complaint data from residents to find the answer.
- Your class monitor wants to pick a movie genre for movie day. Instead of arguing, a quick survey settles the matter.

### Collecting Data Through a Survey

Suppose your class of 40 students is holding an election for class monitor. Three candidates are standing: Meera, Arjun, and Fatima. On voting day, each student writes their choice on a slip of paper. The teacher opens all 40 slips and reads out the names one by one:

*Meera, Arjun, Fatima, Meera, Meera, Arjun, Fatima, Fatima, Meera, Arjun, Fatima, Meera, Meera, Arjun, Meera, Fatima, Arjun, Fatima, Meera, Fatima, Arjun, Meera, Fatima, Fatima, Meera, Arjun, Meera, Fatima, Meera, Arjun, Fatima, Meera, Fatima, Arjun, Meera, Fatima, Meera, Arjun, Fatima, Meera*

This raw list is hard to make sense of. Who won? By how many votes? To answer these questions, we need to **organise** the data.

### Tally Marks — A Quick Counting Method

As the teacher reads each name aloud, a student at the board can keep count using **tally marks**. For every vote, draw a short vertical line. After every fourth line, draw the fifth line diagonally across the group. This bundles counts into groups of five, making it simple to total up later.

**How tally marks work:**
- One item: |
- Two items: ||
- Three items: |||
- Four items: ||||
- Five items: ||||̶ (the fifth stroke cuts diagonally across the first four)

**Class Monitor Election Results:**

| Candidate | Tally Marks | Votes |
|-----------|-------------|-------|
| Meera | ||||̶ ||||̶ ||||̶ | | 16 |
| Arjun | ||||̶ ||||̶ | 10 |
| Fatima | ||||̶ ||||̶ |||| | 14 |
| **Total** | | **40** |

Now it is clear: Meera wins with 16 votes, Fatima comes second with 14, and Arjun gets 10.

> **Always verify:** The sum of all counts must equal the total number of data points you started with. Here, 16 + 10 + 14 = 40. Correct!

### Frequency Tables

When we arrange data into a table showing each category and how often it appears, we create a **frequency table**. The word **frequency** simply means "how many times."

*Example — Favourite sport survey of 50 students in Class 6:*

The sports teacher asked every Class 6 student to name one favourite sport. Here is the organised result:

| Sport | Frequency |
|-------|-----------|
| Cricket | 18 |
| Football | 12 |
| Badminton | 9 |
| Kho-Kho | 7 |
| Swimming | 4 |
| **Total** | **50** |

From this table we can instantly see that Cricket is the most popular sport and Swimming is the least popular.

## Pictographs

A **pictograph** uses small pictures or symbols to represent data. Each symbol stands for a fixed number of items, and this number is stated in a **key** (also called the **scale**).

Pictographs make data visually appealing and easy to understand at a glance, even for someone who has not seen the original numbers.

### Reading a Pictograph

*Example — Monthly sale of umbrellas at a shop near Churchgate Station, Mumbai:*

| Month | Symbols | Number Sold |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| April | ☂☂ | 20 |
| May | ☂☂☂ | 30 |
| June | ☂☂☂☂☂☂☂☂ | 80 |
| July | ☂☂☂☂☂☂☂☂☂☂ | 100 |
| August | ☂☂☂☂☂☂☂ | 70 |
| September | ☂☂☂☂☂ | 50 |

**Key:** Each ☂ = 10 umbrellas

**Questions we can answer:**

- In which month were the most umbrellas sold? **July** (100 umbrellas — the row with the most symbols).
- How many more umbrellas were sold in June than in April? 80 − 20 = **60 more**.
- What is the total number of umbrellas sold from April to September? 20 + 30 + 80 + 100 + 70 + 50 = **350 umbrellas**.
- Why do sales shoot up in June and July? Because Mumbai receives heavy monsoon rain during these months, so more people need umbrellas.

### Drawing a Pictograph

Follow these steps to create your own pictograph:

1. **Collect and organise** your data in a frequency table.
2. **Choose a symbol** that relates to your data (use a ball for sports data, a book for library data, etc.).
3. **Decide the scale** — each symbol should represent a number that divides evenly into all your data values. If your values are 10, 25, 15, 30, a scale of 5 per symbol works well.
4. **Draw the symbols** in rows, one row per category.
5. **Write the key** clearly below or beside the pictograph.
6. **Add a title** that tells the reader what the pictograph is about.

> **Choosing a good scale:** If your data values are large (200, 350, 500), let each symbol stand for 50 or 100. If values are small (3, 7, 5), let each symbol stand for 1. The goal is to avoid drawing too many or too few symbols.

*Example — A housing society tracked how many litres of milk were delivered each day of the week:*

| Day | Litres of Milk |
|-----|---------------|
| Monday | 24 |
| Tuesday | 20 |
| Wednesday | 28 |
| Thursday | 16 |
| Friday | 24 |
| Saturday | 32 |
| Sunday | 36 |

If we choose a scale of 1 bottle symbol = 4 litres, the pictograph would show:

- Monday: 6 bottles (24 / 4)
- Tuesday: 5 bottles (20 / 4)
- Wednesday: 7 bottles (28 / 4)
- Thursday: 4 bottles (16 / 4)
- Friday: 6 bottles (24 / 4)
- Saturday: 8 bottles (32 / 4)
- Sunday: 9 bottles (36 / 4)

The scale of 4 works perfectly because every value is divisible by 4.

## Bar Graphs

While pictographs are attractive, they become impractical when data values are large or do not divide neatly by the scale. A **bar graph** solves this problem by using rectangular bars whose heights (or lengths) represent the data values exactly.

Bar graphs are more precise than pictographs. You can read exact values from the numbered axis instead of counting symbols.

### Parts of a Bar Graph

Every bar graph has these components:

1. **Title** — describes what the graph shows (e.g., "Vegetable Prices in Jaipur Market — January 2026").
2. **X-axis** (horizontal axis) — usually displays the categories (items being compared).
3. **Y-axis** (vertical axis) — displays the numerical values with a marked scale.
4. **Scale** — the intervals marked on the Y-axis (e.g., 0, 10, 20, 30...).
5. **Bars** — rectangular blocks, one per category, all with the same width and equal gaps between them.
6. **Labels** — names written along each axis to explain what it represents.

### Reading and Interpreting a Bar Graph

*Example — Prices of vegetables per kilogram at a local sabzi mandi:*

| Vegetable | Price per kg (Rs) |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Tomato | 40 |
| Onion | 35 |
| Potato | 25 |
| Capsicum | 60 |
| Brinjal | 30 |
| Lady Finger | 45 |

In a bar graph of this data, the X-axis would list the vegetables and the Y-axis would show prices from 0 to 70 (scale: each gridline = 10 rupees).

**What the graph tells us:**
- Capsicum is the most expensive vegetable (tallest bar, Rs 60/kg).
- Potato is the cheapest (shortest bar, Rs 25/kg).
- The difference between the costliest and cheapest vegetable is 60 − 25 = Rs 35.
- Tomato and Lady Finger are close in price (Rs 40 and Rs 45), so their bars would be nearly the same height.

### Drawing a Bar Graph — Step by Step

Suppose the monthly rainfall (in mm) recorded in Bhopal from March to August is:

| Month | Rainfall (mm) |
|-------|--------------|
| March | 15 |
| April | 10 |
| May | 25 |
| June | 180 |
| July | 310 |
| August | 280 |

**Step 1:** Draw the X-axis (horizontal) and Y-axis (vertical) on graph paper. Label the X-axis "Month" and the Y-axis "Rainfall (mm)."

**Step 2:** Choose a suitable scale for the Y-axis. The highest value is 310, so a scale where each gridline = 50 mm works well (0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 350).

**Step 3:** Write the month names equally spaced along the X-axis.

**Step 4:** For each month, draw a bar reaching up to its rainfall value. All bars must have the same width, with equal gaps between them.

**Step 5:** Add the title: "Monthly Rainfall in Bhopal (March–August)."

**Observations from the graph:**
- July has the tallest bar — it receives the most rain.
- March and April have very short bars — these are dry months before the monsoon arrives.
- Rainfall jumps sharply from May (25 mm) to June (180 mm) — this marks the arrival of the monsoon.

> **Horizontal bar graphs** are useful when category names are long. In a horizontal bar graph, categories go on the Y-axis and values on the X-axis, with bars extending sideways.

### Choosing Between a Pictograph and a Bar Graph

| Feature | Pictograph | Bar Graph |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| Best for | Small, simple datasets | Any size of dataset |
| Precision | Approximate (hard to show half-symbols) | Exact (read from the scale) |
| Visual appeal | High (uses pictures) | Moderate (uses plain bars) |
| When values are large | Too many symbols needed | Handles easily with a good scale |

Use a pictograph when you want a quick, eye-catching display for small numbers. Use a bar graph when precision matters or when data values are large and varied.

## Drawing Conclusions from Data

Collecting and displaying data is only the first step. The real power of data handling lies in the **conclusions** — the insights and decisions we draw from it.

### Looking for Patterns

*Example — Number of students absent each day in a school week:*

| Day | Absences |
|-----|----------|
| Monday | 18 |
| Tuesday | 9 |
| Wednesday | 7 |
| Thursday | 8 |
| Friday | 15 |

**Conclusions:**
- Monday has the highest number of absences. This could be because some students extend their weekend by skipping Monday.
- Wednesday and Thursday have the lowest absences — mid-week attendance is strongest.
- Friday absences are also higher than mid-week, possibly because some families leave early for weekend trips.

The school can use this pattern to avoid scheduling important tests on Mondays or Fridays.

### Comparing Groups

*Example — A coaching centre tracked how many students from each batch cleared an entrance exam:*

| Batch | Students Enrolled | Students Who Cleared | Percentage |
|-------|-------------------|---------------------|------------|
| Morning | 60 | 42 | 70% |
| Afternoon | 45 | 27 | 60% |
| Evening | 50 | 40 | 80% |

Simply looking at absolute numbers (42, 27, 40) would suggest the Morning batch performed best. But percentages tell a different story — the Evening batch had the highest success rate at 80%. Data must always be interpreted carefully.

### Asking the Right Questions

Good data analysis goes beyond "what is the highest?" or "what is the lowest?" It asks:
- **Why** is a particular value high or low?
- **Is the pattern consistent** over time, or is it a one-time event?
- **Could there be another explanation** for what the data shows?
- **What action** should be taken based on this data?

> When you see data, always think: "What story is this data telling me, and is there more to the story than what I see on the surface?"

## Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Data** | A collection of numbers, facts, or observations gathered for a purpose |
| **Survey** | A method of collecting data by asking questions to a group of people |
| **Tally marks** | A counting method using groups of five strokes for quick and accurate recording |
| **Frequency** | The number of times a particular value or category occurs in a dataset |
| **Frequency table** | A table listing categories alongside their frequencies |
| **Pictograph** | A graph that uses picture symbols to represent data, with a key stating what each symbol stands for |
| **Key (Scale)** | In a pictograph, the statement telling how many items each symbol represents |
| **Bar graph** | A graph using rectangular bars of equal width to represent data; bar height shows the value |
| **X-axis** | The horizontal line on a graph, usually showing categories |
| **Y-axis** | The vertical line on a graph, usually showing numerical values |
| **Scale** | The set of equally spaced markings on an axis that help read values accurately |
