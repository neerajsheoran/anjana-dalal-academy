## Why Measuring Shapes Matters in Daily Life

Think about Meena's father, who wants to build a boundary wall around their garden in Jaipur. He needs to know the total length of the wall — that is the boundary measurement. Now think about Meena's mother, who wants to lay new flooring tiles inside the kitchen. She needs to know how much floor surface to cover — that is the surface measurement.

Every day, people need two kinds of measurements for flat shapes:
- **Perimeter** — the total distance along the outer edge of a shape
- **Area** — the total flat surface enclosed within the shape

Let us explore both in detail.

---

## Perimeter

The **perimeter** of any flat shape is simply the **total distance around its boundary**. Picture an ant crawling along every edge of a postcard — the total path it walks is the perimeter.

> **Perimeter** = sum of all side lengths

**Units of perimeter:** cm, m, km (it is a length measurement)

![Rectangle with length and breadth labeled on all four sides and a dotted line tracing the perimeter around it](perimeter-rectangle-labeled.png)

### Perimeter of a Rectangle

A rectangle has two pairs of equal sides — two longer sides (length, l) and two shorter sides (breadth, b).

> **Perimeter of rectangle = 2 × (length + breadth) = 2(l + b)**

**Worked Example 1:** A notebook cover measures 24 cm in length and 18 cm in breadth. What is its perimeter?

> P = 2(l + b) = 2(24 + 18) = 2 × 42 = **84 cm**

**Worked Example 2:** Arjun's mother wants to stitch lace around a rectangular tablecloth that is 1.8 m long and 1.2 m wide. How much lace does she need?

> P = 2(1.8 + 1.2) = 2 × 3.0 = **6 m of lace**

**Worked Example 3 (finding a missing side):** A rectangle has a perimeter of 62 cm. If its breadth is 13 cm, what is the length?

> P = 2(l + b)
> 62 = 2(l + 13)
> 31 = l + 13
> l = 31 − 13 = **18 cm**

---

### Perimeter of a Square

Every side of a square is the same length, say s.

> **Perimeter of square = 4 × side = 4s**

**Worked Example:** A square courtyard in a temple has each side measuring 18 m. The caretaker wants to install decorative rope lights along the boundary. If rope lights cost ₹55 per metre, what is the total cost?

> P = 4 × 18 = **72 m**
> Cost = 72 × 55 = **₹3,960**

---

### Perimeter of a Triangle

> **Perimeter of triangle = sum of all three sides = a + b + c**

**Worked Example:** A triangular signboard has sides measuring 45 cm, 60 cm, and 75 cm. Find its perimeter.

> P = 45 + 60 + 75 = **180 cm = 1.8 m**

*Special case — Equilateral triangle* (all 3 sides identical, each = s):
> **P = 3s**

---

### Perimeter of Any Polygon

For any polygon, simply add up all the side lengths.

*Example:* A hexagonal (6-sided) flower bed has sides 3 m, 4 m, 5 m, 3 m, 4 m, and 5 m.
> P = 3 + 4 + 5 + 3 + 4 + 5 = **24 m**

---

## Perimeter in Indian Daily Life

- **Rangoli borders:** During Diwali, when you draw rangoli in a rectangular doorway area, knowing the perimeter helps you plan the decorative border pattern.
- **Stitching salwar suits:** A tailor measures the perimeter of a neckline or hemline to determine how much piping or lace to use.
- **Morning walks:** Rohit walks around a rectangular park that is 200 m long and 80 m wide. Each round covers 2(200+80) = 560 m. If he does 5 rounds, he walks 5 × 560 = 2,800 m = 2.8 km.

## Area

The **area** of a shape tells us **how much flat surface** it occupies.

![Rectangle on a square grid with unit squares shaded inside showing area as the count of squares](area-counting-unit-squares.png)

Imagine pasting square stickers (each 1 cm × 1 cm) on a shape until it is completely covered. The number of stickers you use is the area in square centimetres.

> **Units of area:** cm² (square centimetres), m² (square metres), km² (square kilometres)

*Note:* One square of side 1 cm has area = **1 cm²**.

### Area by Counting Squares

When a shape is drawn on grid paper, you can estimate its area by counting the grid squares inside it.

*Guidelines for counting:*
- A square that is fully inside the shape — count it as 1.
- A square that is roughly half inside — count it as ½.
- A square barely touching the shape — do not count it.

*Example:* A shape on grid paper covers 9 full squares and 6 half-covered squares.
> Area ≈ 9 × 1 + 6 × ½ = 9 + 3 = **12 square units**

---

### Area of a Rectangle

> **Area of rectangle = length × breadth = l × b**

**Worked Example 1:** A postcard is 15 cm long and 10 cm wide. Find its area.

> A = l × b = 15 × 10 = **150 cm²**

**Worked Example 2:** Sunita wants to get her drawing room (6 m long and 4.5 m wide) carpeted. The carpet costs ₹420 per m². What is the total cost?

> A = 6 × 4.5 = **27 m²**
> Cost = 27 × 420 = **₹11,340**

**Worked Example 3 (finding a missing side):** The area of a rectangular plot is 180 m². If the plot is 15 m long, find its breadth.

> A = l × b
> 180 = 15 × b
> b = 180 ÷ 15 = **12 m**

---

### Area of a Square

> **Area of square = side × side = s²**

**Worked Example:** A square chessboard has each side measuring 40 cm. Find its area.

> A = 40² = **1,600 cm²**

**Worked Example (reverse):** The area of a square garden is 225 m². Find the length of each side and the perimeter.

> s² = 225 → s = √225 = **15 m**
> Perimeter = 4 × 15 = **60 m**

## How Perimeter and Area Relate to Each Other

Here is a surprising fact: **two shapes with the same perimeter can enclose different amounts of space**, and **two shapes covering the same area can have different boundary lengths**.

### Same Perimeter, Different Areas

Consider all rectangles with perimeter = 20 cm:

| Length (cm) | Breadth (cm) | Perimeter (cm) | Area (cm²) |
|-------------|-------------|----------------|------------|
| 9 | 1 | 20 | 9 |
| 8 | 2 | 20 | 16 |
| 7 | 3 | 20 | 21 |
| 6 | 4 | 20 | 24 |
| 5 | 5 | 20 | **25** |

**Key insight:** Among all rectangles sharing the same perimeter, the **square always encloses the greatest area**!

> **Practical meaning:** If Ramesh has 60 m of fencing wire, he gets maximum enclosed land by making a square plot of 15 m per side (area = 225 m²), rather than a 20 m × 10 m rectangle (area = 200 m²).

### Same Area, Different Perimeters

Consider all rectangles with area = 48 cm²:

| Length (cm) | Breadth (cm) | Area (cm²) | Perimeter (cm) |
|-------------|-------------|------------|----------------|
| 48 | 1 | 48 | 98 |
| 24 | 2 | 48 | 52 |
| 16 | 3 | 48 | 38 |
| 12 | 4 | 48 | 32 |
| 8 | 6 | 48 | 28 |

**Key insight:** Among all rectangles with the same area, the one closest to a square shape has the **smallest perimeter**!

## Estimating Area of Irregular Shapes

Not every shape fits a neat formula. Think of a leaf, a lake outline on a map, or the shape of your palm print. For these, we use the grid method:

1. Place the shape on squared (grid) paper.
2. Count all squares that are **completely inside** the shape.
3. Count squares that are **more than half inside** — treat each as 1.
4. Ignore squares that are **less than half inside**.
5. Add up for your estimate.

*Simpler rule for quick work:* Count every square that is more than half covered as 1, skip everything else. This gives a reasonable approximation.

## Compound Shapes (Combined Shapes)

![An L-shaped figure split into two rectangles with dotted lines showing how to break compound shapes into simpler parts](compound-shape-area.png)

Real rooms, plots, and buildings are rarely perfect rectangles. Many are L-shaped, T-shaped, or U-shaped. The strategy is to **split them into simple shapes**, calculate each part, and combine.

**Worked Example:** Find the area of a T-shaped stage:

```
    ┌────────┐
    │   Top  │ 2 m
    │        │
┌───┴────────┴───┐
│    Bottom      │ 5 m
└────────────────┘
      10 m
```

Top part width = 6 m (centred within 10 m)

*Method 1: Add the parts*
- Top rectangle: 6 m × 2 m = 12 m²
- Bottom rectangle: 10 m × 5 m = 50 m²
- Total area = 12 + 50 = **62 m²**

*Method 2: Full rectangle minus corners*
- Full enclosing rectangle: 10 m × 7 m = 70 m²
- Two missing corners: each is 2 m × 2 m = 4 m², so total missing = 8 m²
- Area = 70 − 8 = **62 m²** ✓

## Unit Conversions for Area

| Length Relationship | Area Conversion |
|---------------------|----------------|
| 1 cm = 10 mm | 1 cm² = 100 mm² |
| 1 m = 100 cm | 1 m² = 10,000 cm² |
| 1 km = 1,000 m | 1 km² = 10,00,000 m² |

**Indian land measurement units:**

| Unit | Equivalent |
|------|-----------|
| 1 Hectare (ha) | 10,000 m² |
| 1 Are | 100 m² |
| 1 km² | 100 hectares |

*Example:* A village school playground covers 0.8 hectares. Express this in m².
> 0.8 hectares = 0.8 × 10,000 = **8,000 m²**

## Solved Problems — Practice Set

**Problem 1:** A rectangular cricket pitch is 22 m long and 3 m wide. Find:
(a) Its perimeter.
(b) Its area.
(c) The cost of rolling the pitch at ₹15 per m².

> (a) P = 2(22 + 3) = 2 × 25 = **50 m**
> (b) A = 22 × 3 = **66 m²**
> (c) Cost = 66 × 15 = **₹990**

**Problem 2:** A square park has a perimeter of 320 m. What is its area?

> P = 4s → 320 = 4s → s = 80 m
> A = s² = 80² = **6,400 m²**

**Problem 3:** Fatima buys a rectangular shawl 2.4 m long and 1.1 m wide. She wants to add silk edging all around it at ₹18 per metre. What is the cost?

> P = 2(2.4 + 1.1) = 2 × 3.5 = **7 m**
> Cost = 7 × 18 = **₹126**

**Problem 4:** Two squares have sides 5 cm and 12 cm. Can a single rectangle have the same combined area? If yes, list all possible whole-number dimensions.

> Area of first square = 25 cm²
> Area of second square = 144 cm²
> Total area = 169 cm²
> Rectangle dimensions (l × b = 169):
> → 169 × 1, 13 × 13 (only two options since 169 = 13²)

| **m²** | Square metre — a unit of area |
