## Perimeter — The Boundary Walk

**Mother:** Anu, come here. Look at our balcony. If I asked you to tie fairy lights along every edge of this balcony railing, how would you figure out how much wire to buy?

**Child:** Maa, I would measure each side and add them up!

**Mother:** Exactly. That total length around the edges is called the perimeter. Our balcony is 4 metres long and 2 metres wide. Can you work it out?

**Child:** It is a rectangle, so... 4 plus 2 plus 4 plus 2 equals 12 metres. Or I can use the shortcut — 2 times 4 plus 2 gives 2 times 6, which is 12 metres!

**Mother:** Perfect. Now the fairy light wire costs ₹35 per metre at the Sadar Bazaar shop. How much will we spend?

**Child:** 12 times 35... that is 420 rupees, Maa!

**Mother:** See how knowing perimeter saves you from buying too much or too little? Whether it is fencing a kitchen garden, stitching zari border on a saree, or putting tape around a cricket bat handle — it is all about perimeter.

## Area — Covering the Inside

**Child:** Maa, but what if we want to paint the balcony floor instead of decorating the railing? Do we still use perimeter?

**Mother:** No, beta. For painting the floor, you need to know the surface — how much flat space the floor has. That measurement is called area.

**Child:** So perimeter is the edge, and area is the inside?

**Mother:** Beautifully said. For our balcony floor, 4 metres long and 2 metres wide, the area is 4 multiplied by 2, which is 8 square metres.

**Child:** What does "square metres" mean exactly, Maa?

**Mother:** Imagine you have floor tiles, each exactly 1 metre by 1 metre. You would need 8 of those tiles to cover the whole balcony floor. Each tile is one square metre. So 8 square metres means 8 such tiles.

**Child:** Oh! So if our kitchen is 3 metres by 3 metres, the area is 9 square metres — we need 9 tiles of that size?

**Mother:** Spot on. And if each tile costs ₹500, the total cost of tiling the kitchen floor would be 9 times 500, which is 4,500 rupees. Builders and interior designers do exactly this calculation every single day.

## Same Boundary, Different Space Inside

**Child:** Maa, something confusing happened in class today. Our teacher gave everyone a 20-centimetre piece of string and asked us to make different rectangles with it. Everyone had the same length of string, but we got different areas!

**Mother:** That is one of the most fascinating things about geometry! Tell me what you found.

**Child:** With length 9 and breadth 1, the area was just 9. With length 7 and breadth 3, it jumped to 21. And when I made length 5 and breadth 5 — a square — the area was 25. That was the biggest!

**Mother:** Your square won! And this always happens. Among all rectangles with the same perimeter, the square shape gives the maximum area. Think about it this way — a very long, thin rectangle wastes its boundary on length but has almost no width. A square balances both dimensions perfectly.

**Child:** So if a farmer has a fixed amount of fencing, she should make a square field to get the most farming space?

**Mother:** Precisely. And the opposite is also true — if you have a fixed area, the square shape uses the least fencing. This is why many water tanks and rooms are built close to square shapes. It is the most efficient design.

## Breaking Complicated Shapes into Simple Ones

**Child:** Maa, our flat has an L-shaped living room. I cannot use the rectangle formula directly. How do I find its area?

**Mother:** Smart question. The trick is to mentally split the L-shape into two separate rectangles. Find each rectangle's area, then add them together.

**Child:** Let me try with our living room. The bigger part is roughly 6 metres by 4 metres, and the smaller part sticking out is about 3 metres by 2 metres. So the total area is... 24 plus 6, which is 30 square metres!

**Mother:** Excellent. There is another way too. Imagine the full rectangle that the L-shape fits inside, then subtract the missing corner piece.

**Child:** Oh, so the full rectangle would be 6 by 6 which is 36, minus the missing piece which is 3 by 2 which is 6, giving 30 square metres. Same answer!

**Mother:** Both methods always give the same result. Architects use this approach for every building plan — they break floors into simple shapes, calculate each, and add or subtract. Even for something as irregular as a lake, scientists use grid paper and count the squares to estimate the area.

## Why Hectares Exist

**Child:** Maa, I heard on the news that "500 hectares of forest were lost in the fire." What is a hectare?

**Mother:** A hectare is a unit for measuring large areas of land. One hectare equals 10,000 square metres. Picture a ground that is 100 metres on each side — that entire ground is 1 hectare.

**Child:** Why not just say 10,000 square metres?

**Mother:** For the same reason we say "2 kilometres" instead of "2,000 metres" — it is simpler. Farm sizes, forest areas, and city parks are usually measured in hectares because the numbers stay manageable. Our neighbourhood park might be about half a hectare, which sounds much neater than 5,000 square metres.

**Child:** Maa, one more thing. 1 metre is 100 centimetres, but 1 square metre is not 100 square centimetres, right? Our teacher said it is 10,000!

**Mother:** Your teacher is absolutely right, and this is where many students make mistakes. Since area involves two dimensions, you multiply the conversion factor twice. 1 metre equals 100 centimetres, so 1 square metre equals 100 times 100, which is 10,000 square centimetres. Always remember — for area conversions, square the conversion factor.

**Child:** That makes sense! Perimeter is one-dimensional so we convert once, area is two-dimensional so we convert twice. Maths really is logical, Maa.

**Mother:** It is, beta. Once you understand the reasoning, you never need to memorise blindly.
