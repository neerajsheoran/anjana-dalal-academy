## Points, Lines, Segments, and Rays

### The Humble Point

A **point** is the most basic building block of geometry. It represents an exact position in space — nothing more. A point has no length, no width, and no thickness. We mark it as a small dot and label it with a capital letter such as P, Q, or R.

*Everyday connection:* The tip of a sharpened pencil touching paper, the exact spot where two threads cross in a piece of fabric, or the precise location of a city on a map — each of these represents a point.

---

### Line Segment

A **line segment** is a straight path connecting two definite endpoints. It has a fixed, measurable length.

*Everyday connection:*
- The string of a kite between your hand and the kite
- The distance between two pillars of a bridge
- One side of a carrom board

> Written as: **AB** (with a bar on top) — the segment from endpoint A to endpoint B.
> A line segment has a beginning and an end, so you can always measure it with a ruler.

---

### Ray

A **ray** begins at a fixed starting point (called the **initial point**) and travels endlessly in one direction.

*Everyday connection:*
- Light from a lighthouse — it starts at the lighthouse and beams outward into the darkness without stopping
- The beam of a projector in a cinema hall
- A water jet from a garden hose — it starts at the nozzle and shoots forward

> Written as: **→PQ** (arrow on top, from P through Q and beyond)
> A ray has exactly one endpoint — where it begins. It has no end in the other direction.

---

### Line

A **line** stretches infinitely in **both directions** — it has no endpoints at all.

*How to picture it:* Take a thin thread and imagine pulling both ends forever — the thread would become a line. In reality, every physical object has boundaries, so a true geometric line exists only as a concept.

> Written as: **↔PQ** (arrows on both sides)
> Through any two distinct points, exactly one line can be drawn.

### Quick Comparison

| Figure | Endpoints | Length | Notation |
|--------|-----------|--------|----------|
| **Line Segment** | 2 (both fixed) | Finite — measurable | Bar on top (AB) |
| **Ray** | 1 (starting point only) | Infinite in one direction | Arrow one side (→PQ) |
| **Line** | 0 (none) | Infinite in both directions | Arrows both sides (↔PQ) |

## Types of Angles and Measurement

### What Is an Angle?

When two rays share the **same starting point**, the space between them is called an **angle**.

- The shared starting point is the **vertex**.
- The two rays are the **arms** of the angle.

*Everyday connection:* Open a pair of scissors. The pivot where the two blades are joined is the vertex. The two blades are the arms. As you open or close the scissors, the angle between the blades changes.

> **Angle** = the amount of **rotation** or **turning** from one arm to the other, measured in **degrees (°)**.

### Measuring Angles — The Protractor

A **protractor** is a semi-circular instrument marked from 0° to 180° along its curved edge. It has two scales — an inner scale and an outer scale — running in opposite directions.

**How to measure an angle PQR:**
1. Place the **centre mark** (small notch or hole) of the protractor exactly on the **vertex Q**.
2. Align the **baseline** (0° line) of the protractor along one arm, say **QP**.
3. Read the degree marking where the other arm **QR** crosses the curved edge.
4. Use whichever scale (inner or outer) starts from 0° on the arm you aligned.

> **Tip:** Before reading, estimate whether the angle looks smaller or larger than a right angle. This helps you pick the correct scale and avoid mistakes.

**How to draw an angle of 50°:**
1. Draw a ray starting at point O.
2. Place the protractor's centre on O, baseline along the ray.
3. Find 50° on the scale and mark a small point there.
4. Remove the protractor and draw a ray from O through the marked point.
5. The angle formed is 50°.

### Full Turns and Partial Turns

- A **complete rotation** (spinning all the way around to where you started) = **360°**
- A **half rotation** (turning to face the opposite direction) = **180°**
- A **quarter rotation** (turning to face sideways) = **90°**

*Everyday connection:* A ceiling fan blade completing one full spin covers 360°. If it stops halfway, it has turned 180°. If it stops after a quarter spin, it has turned 90°.

### Classifying Angles by Size

| Type | Measure | Everyday Example |
|------|---------|------------------|
| **Acute angle** | Greater than 0° but less than 90° | The angle between clock hands at 2 o'clock; the tip of a mango leaf |
| **Right angle** | Exactly 90° | The corner where two walls meet; the shape of the letter L |
| **Obtuse angle** | Greater than 90° but less than 180° | A reclining chair leaned back; the angle of an open laptop screen beyond 90° |
| **Straight angle** | Exactly 180° | A fully stretched skipping rope held taut; the horizon line |
| **Reflex angle** | Greater than 180° but less than 360° | The larger sweep when a door has been pushed open past the straight position |
| **Complete angle** | Exactly 360° | The full rotation of a spinning top; the minute hand going from 12 back to 12 |

*Memory aids:*
- **A**cute sounds like "a cute little angle" — small, less than 90°.
- **O**btuse means blunt or dull — it is wide and fat, more than 90°.
- **R**eflex — think "going back around," past the straight line.

## Perpendicular and Parallel Lines

### Perpendicular Lines

Two lines (or segments or rays) are **perpendicular** when they meet at a **right angle (90°)**. We use the symbol **⊥** to denote this.

*Everyday connection:*
- The vertical edge and horizontal edge of a door frame meet at 90° — they are perpendicular.
- The crossbar and upright post of a cricket stump stand perpendicular to each other.
- The red cross on an ambulance shows two perpendicular line segments.

> If line AB is perpendicular to line CD, we write **AB ⊥ CD**.

### Parallel Lines

Two lines are **parallel** when they stay the **same distance apart** everywhere and **never intersect**, no matter how far you extend them. We use the symbol **∥** to denote this.

*Everyday connection:*
- The two rails of a railway track running side by side
- The opposite edges of a notebook
- The horizontal bars of a ladder
- Lane markings painted on a highway

> If line PQ is parallel to line RS, we write **PQ ∥ RS**.

### Transversal

A **transversal** is a line that cuts across two or more lines at distinct points.

When a transversal crosses two parallel lines, it creates **8 angles** in total (4 at each intersection). These angles follow predictable rules:

| Angle Pair | Rule | How to Spot |
|------------|------|-------------|
| **Corresponding angles** | Equal | Look for an **F** shape (or its flipped version) |
| **Alternate interior angles** | Equal | Look for a **Z** shape (or **S** shape) |
| **Co-interior (same-side interior) angles** | Add up to 180° | Look for a **C** shape (or **U** shape) |

**Worked example:** A transversal cuts two parallel lines and one angle at the first crossing is 55°.
> - Vertically opposite angle at the same crossing = **55°**
> - Adjacent angle (linear pair) = 180° − 55° = **125°**
> - Corresponding angle at the second crossing = **55°** (F-rule)
> - Alternate interior angle = **55°** (Z-rule)
> - Co-interior angle = 180° − 55° = **125°** (C-rule)
> - All 8 angles: **55°, 125°, 55°, 125°** at the first crossing and **55°, 125°, 55°, 125°** at the second.

## Angle Relationships

### Complementary Angles

Two angles are **complementary** when their measures add up to **90°**.

*Memory trick:* **C**omplementary → **C**orner → a right-angle corner is 90°.

*Everyday connection:* When you fold a square piece of paper along a diagonal, the fold creates two angles at the corner. Those two angles are complementary because together they fill the 90° corner.

**Worked example:** What is the complement of 28°?
> Complement = 90° − 28° = **62°**
> Verification: 28° + 62° = 90° ✓

**Key fact:** Only acute angles (less than 90°) can have complements.

---

### Supplementary Angles

Two angles are **supplementary** when their measures add up to **180°**.

*Memory trick:* **S**upplementary → **S**traight → a straight angle is 180°.

*Everyday connection:* Picture a chapati on a flat tava. If you place a knife straight across the centre, the blade divides the space on each side into two angles. Those two angles are supplementary — they combine to form the straight line of the knife.

**Worked example:** What is the supplement of 134°?
> Supplement = 180° − 134° = **46°**
> Verification: 134° + 46° = 180° ✓

---

### Adjacent Angles

Two angles are **adjacent** when they:
1. Share the **same vertex**
2. Share one **common arm**
3. Lie on **opposite sides** of the common arm (no overlap)

*Everyday connection:* Open a book partway and stand it on a table. The left page and the right page each make an angle with the table. These two angles share the spine as a common arm and the table surface as their vertex region — they are adjacent.

---

### Linear Pair

When two adjacent angles sit on a straight line, they form a **linear pair**. A linear pair always adds up to **180°**.

**Worked example:** One angle of a linear pair is 63°. Find the other.
> Other angle = 180° − 63° = **117°**

---

### Vertically Opposite Angles

When two straight lines cross each other, they form **four angles**. The angles that face each other (diagonally across the intersection) are called **vertically opposite angles**, and they are always **equal**.

```
      Q
      |
 P ———O——— R
      |
      S
```
At intersection O: angle POQ = angle ROS (vertically opposite), and angle POS = angle QOR (vertically opposite).

**Worked example:** Two lines cross and one of the angles is 42°. Find all four.
> - Vertically opposite angle = **42°**
> - Adjacent angle (linear pair) = 180° − 42° = **138°**
> - Remaining angle (vertically opposite to 138°) = **138°**
> - **Four angles: 42°, 138°, 42°, 138°**

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Point** | An exact position in space — no size, no dimensions |
| **Line Segment** | A straight path between two endpoints — finite and measurable |
| **Ray** | Starts at one point and extends infinitely in one direction |
| **Line** | Extends infinitely in both directions — no endpoints |
| **Angle** | The rotation between two rays sharing a common starting point |
| **Vertex** | The point where two arms of an angle meet |
| **Protractor** | Semi-circular tool for measuring angles (0° to 180°) |
| **Acute angle** | More than 0° and less than 90° |
| **Right angle** | Exactly 90° |
| **Obtuse angle** | More than 90° and less than 180° |
| **Straight angle** | Exactly 180° |
| **Reflex angle** | More than 180° and less than 360° |
| **Complete angle** | Exactly 360° — one full rotation |
| **Perpendicular** | Lines meeting at exactly 90° |
| **Parallel** | Lines that never meet and stay equally spaced |
| **Complementary** | Two angles whose sum is 90° |
| **Supplementary** | Two angles whose sum is 180° |
| **Linear pair** | Adjacent angles on a straight line — sum is 180° |
| **Vertically opposite** | Angles across from each other at an intersection — always equal |
| **Transversal** | A line that crosses two or more other lines |
