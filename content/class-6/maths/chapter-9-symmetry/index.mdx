## The Balanced World of Symmetry

Hold up a fresh leaf and look at it closely. The left side mirrors the right. Glance at a kolam design drawn outside a house in Chennai — it looks the same from multiple angles. Observe a spinning ceiling fan — every position looks identical to the last.

All these share one property: **symmetry**. A figure has symmetry when it looks unchanged after a specific transformation — either a flip (reflection) or a turn (rotation).

Symmetry is not just about beauty. It governs how crystals grow, how bridges are engineered, and why certain patterns feel harmonious to our eyes.

---

## Line Symmetry (Mirror Symmetry)

A figure has **line symmetry** when you can draw a straight line through it so that the portion on one side is the exact mirror image of the other side.

That line is called the **line of symmetry** (also known as the **axis of symmetry** or **mirror line**).

*The folding test:* If you fold the figure along its line of symmetry, the two halves land perfectly on top of each other — no part sticking out.

### How Many Lines of Symmetry?

| Figure | Lines of symmetry |
|--------|-------------------|
| Scalene triangle (no equal sides) | 0 |
| Isosceles triangle (two equal sides) | 1 |
| Equilateral triangle (three equal sides) | 3 |
| Rectangle (not a square) | 2 |
| Square | 4 |
| Regular pentagon (5 equal sides) | 5 |
| Regular hexagon (6 equal sides) | 6 |
| Circle | Unlimited (every diameter is one) |
| Letter T | 1 |
| Letter H | 2 |
| Letter X | 2 |
| Letter O | Unlimited |

### Symmetry in English Letters

| Only horizontal symmetry | Only vertical symmetry | Both horizontal and vertical |
|--------------------------|------------------------|------------------------------|
| B, C, D, E, K | A, M, T, U, V, W, Y | H, I, O, X |

*Letters with no symmetry at all:* F, G, J, L, N, P, Q, R, S, Z

## Spotting Lines of Symmetry in Shapes

**Square:** Four lines — one vertical through the centre, one horizontal through the centre, and two along the diagonals. All four divide the square into matching halves.

**Equilateral triangle:** Three lines — each runs from a corner to the centre of the opposite side. These three lines are simultaneously the medians, altitudes, and angle bisectors.

**Rectangle (non-square):** Only two lines — the vertical and horizontal ones through the midpoints of opposite sides. The diagonals of a rectangle do NOT work as lines of symmetry because folding along a diagonal does not make the two halves match.

> **Remember:** A line counts as a line of symmetry only when every single point on one side has a matching partner at exactly the same distance on the other side.

## Reflection

When a figure is reflected across a mirror line, each point maps to a new position on the opposite side such that:
- The mirror line is the **perpendicular bisector** of the segment joining any point and its reflected image.
- The reflected image is congruent to the original — same shape, same size, just flipped.

*Everyday reflections:*
- A mountain reflected in a still lake.
- Your face in a bathroom mirror.
- The Taj Mahal mirrored in the long reflecting pool.
- A kolam pattern where one half is a mirror copy of the other.

## Rotational Symmetry

A figure has **rotational symmetry** when it can be turned by some angle less than 360° around a fixed central point and still look exactly the same as before.

### Important Terms

- **Centre of rotation** — the fixed point around which the figure turns.
- **Angle of rotation** — the smallest turn that makes the figure look identical.
- **Order of rotational symmetry** — how many times the figure matches itself during one complete 360° spin.

> **Formula:** Order = 360° ÷ Angle of rotation

### Shape-by-Shape Examples

**Equilateral triangle:**
- Matches itself after every 120° turn.
- Order = 360° ÷ 120° = **3**.

**Square:**
- Matches itself after every 90° turn.
- Order = 360° ÷ 90° = **4** (identical at 90°, 180°, 270°, 360°).

**Regular hexagon:**
- Matches itself after every 60° turn.
- Order = 360° ÷ 60° = **6**.

**Rectangle (non-square):**
- Matches itself after a 180° turn only.
- Order = 360° ÷ 180° = **2**.

**Circle:**
- Matches itself after any rotation at all.
- Order = **infinite**.

**Letters N, S, Z:**
- Each has rotational symmetry of order 2 (identical after 180°), yet none has any line of symmetry.

| Figure | Angle of rotation | Order |
|--------|-------------------|-------|
| Equilateral triangle | 120° | 3 |
| Square | 90° | 4 |
| Rectangle (non-square) | 180° | 2 |
| Regular pentagon | 72° | 5 |
| Regular hexagon | 60° | 6 |
| Circle | Any angle | Infinite |

> **Note:** Every figure trivially matches itself after a full 360° turn. We say a figure has rotational symmetry only when its order is 2 or higher.

## Figures With Both Types of Symmetry

Regular polygons enjoy both line symmetry and rotational symmetry:

| Figure | Lines of symmetry | Rotational order |
|--------|-------------------|------------------|
| Equilateral triangle | 3 | 3 |
| Square | 4 | 4 |
| Regular hexagon | 6 | 6 |
| Circle | Infinite | Infinite |

### Figures With Only One Type

- **Parallelogram (not a rectangle):** Zero lines of symmetry, but rotational order 2 — **rotational only**.
- **Isosceles triangle:** One line of symmetry, but rotational order 1 — **line symmetry only**.
- **Letter Z:** Zero lines of symmetry, rotational order 2 — **rotational only**.
- **Arrow shape (↑):** One line of symmetry, rotational order 1 — **line symmetry only**.

## Symmetry in Indian Art and Heritage

India has an extraordinarily rich tradition of symmetric design.

### Kolam and Rangoli

- Geometric patterns drawn on the ground using rice flour, chalk powder, or coloured sand.
- Common across India: Kolam in Tamil Nadu, Muggulu in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, Rangoli in Maharashtra and Gujarat, Alpana in Bengal.
- These designs often have 4-fold or 8-fold symmetry — multiple mirror lines and high rotational order.
- Many kolam designs can be drawn in one continuous stroke — a mathematical feat in itself.

### Mandalas

- Circular patterns radiating outward from a central point.
- Used in Hindu temple floors, Buddhist meditation art, and Jain cosmological diagrams.
- A mandala typically has many lines of symmetry (all passing through the centre) and high-order rotational symmetry.

### Mughal Geometric Patterns

- Monuments like the Taj Mahal, Humayun's Tomb, and Agra Fort display intricate stone inlay and lattice work (jali) based on star polygons and interlocking hexagons.
- The Charbagh (four-part) garden layout of the Taj Mahal has perfect four-fold symmetry — divided into four identical quadrants by water channels.

### Warli Art (Maharashtra) and Madhubani Art (Bihar)

- Both traditions repeat circles, triangles, and other geometric shapes in symmetric arrangements.
- Warli paintings use concentric circles and radial lines — natural rotational symmetry.

### Temple Architecture

- Hindu temple plans follow the **Vastu Purusha Mandala** — a grid-based square plan with four-fold symmetry.
- The shikhara (tower) above the sanctum has vertical line symmetry, often with intricate carvings that repeat symmetrically on either side.

## Tessellations — Covering a Surface Without Gaps

A **tessellation** (or tiling) is an arrangement of shapes that covers a flat surface completely, leaving no gaps and having no overlaps.

Only three regular polygons can tessellate a surface by themselves:
1. **Equilateral triangle** — six meet at every vertex (6 × 60° = 360°).
2. **Square** — four meet at every vertex (4 × 90° = 360°).
3. **Regular hexagon** — three meet at every vertex (3 × 120° = 360°).

> **Why hexagons are special:** Among all shapes that tile without gaps, the regular hexagon encloses the most area for the least boundary — the most efficient shape. This is exactly why bees construct hexagonal honeycomb cells: maximum honey storage, minimum wax used.

*Semi-regular tessellations* combine two or more types of regular polygons — for example, octagons paired with small squares.

## Symmetry in the Natural World

- **Snowflakes** — always 6-fold symmetry (6 lines, rotational order 6), because ice crystals form hexagonal lattices.
- **Flowers** — many have 3, 4, 5, or 6-fold symmetry depending on the species.
- **Starfish** — 5-fold symmetry, allowing movement in any direction along the sea floor.
- **Leaves** — bilateral (one line of) symmetry down the central vein.
- **Spider webs** — radial symmetry with threads spreading from a central hub.
- **Human body** — approximate bilateral symmetry (left side roughly mirrors the right).
- **Crystals and minerals** — each crystal system has its own specific symmetry group.

## Key Words to Remember

| Word | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Symmetry** | The property of looking the same after a reflection or rotation |
| **Line of symmetry** | A line that divides a figure into two mirror-image halves |
| **Reflection** | Flipping a figure across a line to create its mirror image |
| **Rotational symmetry** | A figure that looks identical after turning by less than 360° |
| **Centre of rotation** | The fixed point around which a figure is rotated |
| **Order of symmetry** | The count of times a figure matches itself in one full 360° turn |
| **Angle of rotation** | The smallest turning angle that makes a figure look the same |
| **Tessellation** | A gap-free, overlap-free covering of a surface using shapes |
| **Kolam / Rangoli** | Traditional Indian floor art featuring geometric symmetry |
| **Mandala** | A circular symmetric design used in Indian spiritual and artistic traditions |
