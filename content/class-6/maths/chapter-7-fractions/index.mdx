## What Is a Fraction?

Imagine your grandmother makes a rectangular barfi and cuts it into 5 equal pieces. You eat 2 of those pieces. How much of the barfi did you eat? You ate **two-fifths**, written as **2/5**.

![A circle divided into 4 equal parts with 3 parts shaded, showing the fraction three-fourths](fraction-parts-of-whole.png)

A **fraction** describes a part of something whole. It has two numbers:
- The **numerator** (top number) — how many parts you are talking about
- The **denominator** (bottom number) — how many equal parts the whole was split into

> **Fraction = Numerator / Denominator**

*Some everyday fractions:*
- 1/2 — one out of 2 equal parts (a half)
- 3/4 — three out of 4 equal parts (three-quarters)
- 5/8 — five out of 8 equal parts

**Crucial point:** The parts must be **equal**. If you tear a chapati into 3 uneven pieces and pick one, you cannot call it 1/3 because the pieces are different sizes.

---

## Fractions as Part of a Group

Fractions work for collections of things too, not just single objects.

*Example:* A cricket team has 11 players. If 4 of them are bowlers, the fraction of bowlers is 4/11.

*Example:* Out of 40 mangoes in a basket, 15 are ripe. The fraction of ripe mangoes = 15/40 = 3/8.

## Types of Fractions

### Proper Fractions

When the numerator is **smaller than** the denominator, we have a **proper fraction**.

> Proper fraction: numerator < denominator

*Examples:* 2/7, 4/9, 1/6, 10/13

A proper fraction always represents a quantity between 0 and 1 — less than one whole.

### Improper Fractions

When the numerator is **equal to or larger than** the denominator, the fraction is called **improper**.

> Improper fraction: numerator ≥ denominator

*Examples:* 7/4, 9/5, 6/6, 13/3

An improper fraction represents a quantity that is 1 or more.

### Mixed Numbers

A **mixed number** combines a whole number with a proper fraction.

*Examples:* 2⅓, 4¾, 1⅗, 7½

**Turning an improper fraction into a mixed number:**

*Example:* Express 19/4 as a mixed number.

> Divide: 19 ÷ 4 = **4 remainder 3**
> Mixed number: **4 3/4**
> (4 complete wholes and 3/4 remaining)

**Turning a mixed number into an improper fraction:**

*Example:* Write 3 5/8 as an improper fraction.

> Multiply whole part by denominator, then add numerator:
> (3 × 8) + 5 = 24 + 5 = **29**
> Improper fraction: **29/8**

## Placing Fractions on a Number Line

![Number line from 0 to 2 divided into thirds showing positions of one-third, two-thirds, one, four-thirds, five-thirds, and two](fractions-on-number-line.png)

Fractions sit between (and sometimes on) whole numbers on the number line.

To show fourths (denominator = 4), split each unit gap into 4 equal segments:
```
0     1/4    2/4    3/4     1     5/4    6/4    7/4     2
|------|------|------|------|------|------|------|------|
0                           1                           2
```

*Useful observation:* 4/4 = 1, 8/4 = 2, 12/4 = 3 — whenever the numerator is a perfect multiple of the denominator, the fraction equals a whole number.

## Equivalent Fractions

![Rectangles showing one-half, two-fourths, three-sixths, and four-eighths with equal shaded portions proving they are equivalent](equivalent-fractions-bars.png)

**Equivalent fractions** look different but stand for the exact same amount.

*Example:* 1/3 = 2/6 = 3/9 = 4/12 — all represent one-third!

**How to create them:** Multiply (or divide) both the numerator and denominator by the **same number** (not zero).

*Making equivalent fractions of 3/5:*
- 3/5 = (3×2)/(5×2) = **6/10**
- 3/5 = (3×4)/(5×4) = **12/20**
- 3/5 = (3×7)/(5×7) = **21/35**

**Quick test for equivalence:** Use cross multiplication.
- Are 2/3 and 8/12 equivalent? → 2×12 = 24 and 3×8 = 24. **Products match → Yes, equivalent.**
- Are 3/7 and 5/12 equivalent? → 3×12 = 36 and 7×5 = 35. **Products differ → Not equivalent.**

## Simplest Form (Lowest Terms)

A fraction is in its **simplest form** when the numerator and denominator share no common factor except 1 — their HCF is 1.

**Method:** Divide both numerator and denominator by their HCF.

*Example:* Reduce 42/56 to simplest form.

> HCF(42, 56):
> 42 = 2 × 3 × 7, 56 = 2³ × 7. HCF = 2 × 7 = 14.
> 42/56 = (42÷14)/(56÷14) = **3/4** ✓

*Example:* Reduce 60/84 to simplest form.

> HCF(60, 84):
> 60 = 2² × 3 × 5, 84 = 2² × 3 × 7. HCF = 2² × 3 = 12.
> 60/84 = (60÷12)/(84÷12) = **5/7** ✓

## Comparing Fractions

### When Denominators Are the Same

Simply compare the numerators. Bigger numerator means bigger fraction.

> 7/11 **>** 4/11 (because 7 > 4, denominator is the same)

### When Numerators Are the Same

Compare the denominators. **Smaller** denominator means **bigger** fraction (each piece is larger).

> 5/6 **>** 5/9 (sixths are bigger pieces than ninths)

### When Both Are Different — Cross Multiplication

To compare a/b and c/d: compute a×d and b×c.
- If a×d > b×c → a/b > c/d
- If a×d = b×c → a/b = c/d
- If a×d < b×c → a/b < c/d

*Example:* Compare 4/7 and 5/9.

> 4 × 9 = 36 and 7 × 5 = 35.
> 36 > 35, so **4/7 > 5/9**.

### Using a Common Denominator (LCM Method)

Convert both fractions so they share the same denominator (use LCM), then compare.

*Example:* Compare 5/6 and 7/9.

> LCM(6, 9) = 18.
> 5/6 = 15/18 and 7/9 = 14/18.
> 15 > 14, so **5/6 > 7/9**.

## Addition of Fractions

### Same Denominator

> **a/c + b/c = (a+b)/c**

*Example:* 4/11 + 5/11 = **9/11**

### Different Denominators

1. Find the **LCM** of the two denominators.
2. Rewrite each fraction with the LCM as denominator.
3. Add the numerators.
4. Simplify if possible.

**Worked Example:** 3/4 + 2/5

> LCM(4, 5) = 20.
> 3/4 = 15/20 and 2/5 = 8/20.
> 15/20 + 8/20 = **23/20 = 1 3/20**

**Worked Example:** 4/9 + 1/6

> LCM(9, 6) = 18.
> 4/9 = 8/18 and 1/6 = 3/18.
> 8/18 + 3/18 = **11/18**

### Adding Mixed Numbers

Handle the whole parts and fractional parts separately.

*Example:* 3 2/5 + 2 1/4

> Whole parts: 3 + 2 = 5.
> Fractional parts: 2/5 + 1/4. LCM(5,4) = 20. → 8/20 + 5/20 = 13/20.
> Total: 5 + 13/20 = **5 13/20**

---

## Subtraction of Fractions

### Same Denominator

> **a/c − b/c = (a−b)/c**

*Example:* 8/13 − 3/13 = **5/13**

### Different Denominators

Same approach as addition — find LCM, convert, then subtract.

**Worked Example:** 7/8 − 2/5

> LCM(8, 5) = 40.
> 7/8 = 35/40 and 2/5 = 16/40.
> 35/40 − 16/40 = **19/40**

**Worked Example:** 4 1/3 − 2 3/4

> Convert to improper: 4 1/3 = 13/3 and 2 3/4 = 11/4.
> LCM(3, 4) = 12. → 52/12 − 33/12 = **19/12 = 1 7/12**

## Multiplication of Fractions

> **a/b × c/d = (a×c)/(b×d)**

*Example:* 4/7 × 3/5 = **(4×3)/(7×5) = 12/35**

*Example:* Find 3/4 of 32 (meaning 3/4 × 32):

> 3/4 × 32 = (3×32)/4 = 96/4 = **24**

*Tip — cancel common factors before multiplying:*

> 5/12 × 4/15 = (5×4)/(12×15) → cancel 5 with 15 (÷5) and 4 with 12 (÷4) → 1/3 × 1/3 = **1/9**

**Worked Example (real life):** Priya's recipe calls for 2/3 cup of milk. She wants to make only 3/4 of the recipe. How much milk does she need?

> 3/4 × 2/3 = 6/12 = **1/2 cup of milk**

## Unit Fractions — A Glimpse into History

A **unit fraction** has numerator 1 (such as 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/7, ...).

The ancient Egyptians had an interesting rule: they expressed every fraction as a sum of distinct unit fractions.

*Example:* Instead of writing 2/3, the Egyptians would write 1/2 + 1/6.

> Verify: 1/2 + 1/6 = 3/6 + 1/6 = 4/6 = 2/3 ✓

**India's mathematical heritage:** The great Indian mathematician **Aryabhata** (476 CE) and later **Brahmagupta** (628 CE) developed systematic rules for fraction arithmetic — addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division — that form the basis of what we study today. They wrote fractions with one number above the other, and Arab scholars later introduced the horizontal line between them.

## Key Words to Remember

| Word | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Fraction** | A way to represent a part of a whole — numerator over denominator |
| **Numerator** | The top number — counts how many parts are being considered |
| **Denominator** | The bottom number — tells the total number of equal parts |
| **Proper fraction** | Numerator < denominator (value lies between 0 and 1) |
| **Improper fraction** | Numerator ≥ denominator (value is 1 or more) |
| **Mixed number** | A whole number paired with a proper fraction (e.g., 3⅖) |
| **Equivalent fractions** | Fractions that look different but represent the same quantity |
| **Simplest form** | A fraction where the HCF of numerator and denominator is 1 |
| **Unit fraction** | A fraction whose numerator is 1 (like 1/2, 1/5, 1/9) |
| **LCM method** | Using the Least Common Multiple to give fractions a shared denominator for adding or subtracting |
