## Why Whole Numbers Are Not Enough

**Child:** Maa, why do we even need fractions? Whole numbers are so much simpler.

**Mother:** Try this, beta. You have 2 parathas and 3 people at the table. Can you give everyone a whole paratha?

**Child:** No... there aren't enough parathas. Oh wait — we could cut each paratha into 3 parts and give everyone 2 parts. So each person gets 2/3 of a paratha!

**Mother:** Exactly. The world does not always split into neat whole numbers. When a shopkeeper weighs 250 grams of jaggery, that is 1/4 of a kilogram. When you finish 30 minutes of a 45-minute exam, you have used 2/3 of the time. Fractions describe all these in-between quantities.

**Child:** So the denominator says how many equal pieces the whole is cut into, and the numerator says how many pieces we picked?

**Mother:** Right. And remember — the word "equal" is important. If you slice a mango into 4 pieces but the slices are all different sizes, calling one piece "1/4" would be wrong. Fractions only work when every part is the same size.

## Equivalent Fractions — Different Face, Same Value

**Child:** Maa, how can 1/2 and 3/6 and 5/10 all mean the same thing? They have completely different numbers!

**Mother:** Let me show you. Take a chapati and cut it in half. You hold 1 piece out of 2 — that is 1/2. Now cut each half into 3 equal strips. You have 6 strips total, and you are holding 3 of them — that is 3/6. But has the amount of chapati in your hand changed?

**Child:** No! It is still the same half.

**Mother:** That is the entire idea. Equivalent fractions are just different ways to label the same portion. To get from one to another, multiply both numerator and denominator by the same number. 1/2 becomes 3/6 by multiplying both by 3, or 5/10 by multiplying both by 5.

**Child:** And to simplify, I divide both by their HCF? Like 24/36 — the HCF of 24 and 36 is 12, so it becomes 2/3?

**Mother:** Exactly. And see how HCF from your earlier chapter on factors connects here? Mathematics is like a chain — every topic links to the next. For a quick equivalence check, cross-multiply. If 2 times 36 equals 3 times 24 — both give 72 — then 2/3 and 24/36 are equivalent.

## Comparing Fractions — Settling the Debate

**Child:** Maa, my friend Aarav and I were arguing. He had 3/7 of a chocolate bar, and I had 2/5. Who got more?

**Mother:** Good question. There are two neat ways to find out. First method — cross multiplication. Multiply 3 by 5 to get 15, and 7 by 2 to get 14. Since 15 is bigger than 14, your friend's share, 3/7, is slightly larger.

**Child:** So Aarav wins! What is the second method?

**Mother:** Find a common denominator. The LCM of 7 and 5 is 35. Convert: 3/7 becomes 15/35, and 2/5 becomes 14/35. Now just compare numerators — 15 beats 14, so 3/7 is bigger.

**Child:** The difference is so tiny though — just 1/35 of the chocolate bar!

**Mother:** Exactly. In many situations these small differences really count. Think about a goldsmith — even a fraction of a gram changes the price of a gold chain. Or a pharmacist measuring medicine — getting the fraction wrong could be dangerous. Precision with fractions is a life skill, not just a school exercise.

## Adding and Subtracting Fractions

**Child:** Maa, adding fractions with the same denominator is simple — 2/9 plus 5/9 is 7/9. But what happens when the denominators do not match?

**Mother:** Think of it like adding different coins. If I say "I have 3 five-rupee coins and 4 two-rupee coins," can you just add 3 and 4 to get 7 and call it the answer?

**Child:** No, because five-rupee coins and two-rupee coins have different values. I need to convert to the same unit — 15 rupees plus 8 rupees is 23 rupees.

**Mother:** Fractions work the same way. The denominator is like the "type of coin." Before adding, you need matching denominators. To add 3/4 and 1/6, first find the LCM of 4 and 6, which is 12. Convert: 3/4 becomes 9/12, and 1/6 becomes 2/12. Now add: 9/12 plus 2/12 equals 11/12.

**Child:** And subtraction is the same idea but we subtract the numerators instead?

**Mother:** Exactly. 7/10 minus 1/4 — LCM of 10 and 4 is 20. Convert: 14/20 minus 5/20 equals 9/20. The process never changes — make denominators equal first, then operate on numerators.

## Fractions in India's Mathematical Story

**Child:** Maa, our textbook mentions that Indian mathematicians worked with fractions long ago. What exactly did they do?

**Mother:** India has a remarkable history with fractions. Aryabhata, a brilliant mathematician from Patna who lived around 476 CE, used fractions in his astronomical calculations. Later, Brahmagupta from Rajasthan, around 628 CE, wrote down detailed rules for adding, subtracting, multiplying, and dividing fractions in his book Brahmasphutasiddhanta.

**Child:** So the fraction rules we learn in school are over a thousand years old?

**Mother:** Even older! And here is something interesting — Indian mathematicians wrote fractions by placing one number above another, without any line between them. It was Arab mathematicians who later added the horizontal bar, and European scholars adopted this notation centuries afterwards.

**Child:** The textbook also talks about Egyptians using only fractions with numerator 1. That sounds so limiting!

**Mother:** It does. The Egyptians wrote 3/4 as 1/2 plus 1/4, and 2/7 as 1/4 plus 1/28. It worked, but it was clumsy. Indian mathematicians allowed any numerator, which made arithmetic far smoother and faster. That is why the Indian approach spread across the world.

**Child:** I am proud that our way of doing fractions became the global standard, Maa!

**Mother:** You should be, beta. Every time anyone writes a fraction anywhere in the world today, they are using a system that was refined right here in India.
