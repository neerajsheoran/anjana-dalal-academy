## Factors, Multiples, and Divisibility

**Child:** Maa, our teacher asked us to find all the factors of 28 and I listed 1, 2, 4, 7, 14, 28. But then she asked how I know I have not missed any. How do I make sure the list is complete?

**Mother:** Good question. Here is a reliable method. Start dividing 28 by 1, then 2, then 3, and so on. Each time a division comes out exact, you get a pair of factors. 28 divided by 1 is 28, so 1 and 28 are factors. 28 divided by 2 is 14, so 2 and 14 are factors. 28 divided by 4 is 7, so 4 and 7 are factors. Now notice that the next number to try would be 5, but 5 is already larger than 7, and we found 7 as a pair partner of 4. So we can stop.

**Child:** Why can we stop once the numbers cross over?

**Mother:** Because factors come in pairs that multiply to give 28. Once the smaller number in the pair exceeds the square root of 28, which is about 5.3, you have already found the larger partner in a previous step. Every factor beyond that point has already been captured as a pair. So your complete list is 1, 2, 4, 7, 14, 28 -- six factors, and you can be certain nothing is missing.

**Child:** Maa, and what about multiples? Are they related?

**Mother:** They are two sides of the same coin. Think of it this way -- when you say 4 is a factor of 28, you are also saying 28 is a multiple of 4. Factors break a number into equal groups; multiples build a number upward by repeated addition. If I buy bangles in sets of 4, the possible totals are 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, and so on -- all multiples of 4. The list never ends.

**Child:** So factors have a limit but multiples go on forever?

**Mother:** Exactly. The number 28 has only six factors, but there are infinitely many multiples of 28 -- you can always multiply by a bigger number. And those divisibility rules you learned -- like checking if the last digit is even for divisibility by 2, or adding digits to test for 3 -- those are just shortcuts to identify factors quickly without doing long division every time.

## Prime and Composite Numbers

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says 1 is not a prime number. But it only has one factor -- itself. Should that not make it extra special?

**Mother:** Let me explain with an everyday comparison. Imagine you are sorting beads into necklaces. A prime bead-count is one where you can only arrange the beads in a single row -- no other rectangular arrangement works. For 7 beads, the only option is 1 row of 7. For 5 beads, just 1 row of 5. Those are prime. But for 1 bead, the concept does not really apply -- you cannot meaningfully talk about arranging 1 bead in rows and columns.

**Child:** So 1 is just... different?

**Mother:** Yes. Mathematicians decided that a prime number must have exactly two distinct factors -- 1 and itself. The number 1 has only one factor, so it falls outside the definition. It is neither prime nor composite. This is not just a technicality -- if we allowed 1 to be prime, then the rule about unique prime factorisation would break. You could write 12 as 2 times 2 times 3, or as 1 times 2 times 2 times 3, or as 1 times 1 times 2 times 2 times 3, and the factorisation would no longer be unique.

**Child:** That makes sense. And composite numbers are the ones with extra factors?

**Mother:** Right. Take the number 18. Its factors are 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, and 18 -- that is six factors. Since 18 has factors beyond just 1 and itself, it is composite. It can be broken into smaller building blocks: 18 equals 2 times 3 times 3.

**Child:** What about co-prime numbers? Our teacher mentioned those.

**Mother:** Two numbers are co-prime when the only factor they share is 1. Take 16 and 27. Factors of 16 are 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. Factors of 27 are 1, 3, 9, 27. The only number in both lists is 1. So 16 and 27 are co-prime even though neither of them is actually a prime number.

**Child:** So co-prime is about the pair, not the individual numbers.

**Mother:** Precisely. Even two composite numbers can be co-prime as a pair if they share no common factor beyond 1.

## The Sieve of Eratosthenes

**Child:** Maa, we did the Sieve of Eratosthenes in class today. We wrote numbers from 2 to 50 and kept crossing out multiples. But why does crossing out multiples of just 2, 3, 5, and 7 catch every composite number up to 50?

**Mother:** Think about what makes a composite number composite -- it can be split into two smaller factors. If a number up to 50 is composite, at least one of those smaller factors must be fairly small. Specifically, it must be no larger than the square root of 50, which is roughly 7.

**Child:** Why the square root?

**Mother:** Because if both factors were larger than 7, their product would be more than 7 times 7, which is 49 -- already close to our limit. So at least one factor must be 7 or less. And if a number has a factor of 7 or less, that factor is either a prime (2, 3, 5, or 7) or is itself divisible by one of those primes. Either way, the sieve catches it.

**Child:** So for numbers up to 100, I would only need to cross out multiples of primes up to 10?

**Mother:** Exactly. The square root of 100 is 10. The primes up to 10 are 2, 3, 5, and 7. After eliminating their multiples, every survivor is prime. That is the beauty of the sieve -- a small amount of work reveals a large collection of primes.

**Child:** It is amazing that someone figured this out more than 2,000 years ago.

**Mother:** Eratosthenes was a remarkable thinker. He also estimated the circumference of the Earth using shadows and geometry. The sieve named after him remains one of the most efficient ways to generate a list of primes up to any limit.

## Prime Factorisation

**Child:** Maa, I am confused about factor trees. My friend and I made different trees for the same number but got different answers. Did one of us make a mistake?

**Mother:** Not necessarily. Let us try with 72. You can start by splitting 72 any way you like, as long as neither part is 1. How would you split it?

**Child:** 8 times 9.

**Mother:** Good. Now keep splitting. 8 becomes 2 times 4, and 4 becomes 2 times 2. 9 becomes 3 times 3. So you reach 2 times 2 times 2 times 3 times 3. Your friend might have started with 72 as 6 times 12. Then 6 splits into 2 times 3, and 12 splits into 2 times 6, and that 6 splits into 2 times 3. The leaves are still 2 times 2 times 2 times 3 times 3.

**Child:** The same primes every time!

**Mother:** That is guaranteed by the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic. No matter which path you take down the tree, you always arrive at the same collection of prime factors. For 72, it is always three 2s and two 3s. The tree shape may differ, but the destination is identical.

**Child:** What about the division ladder method? Is it better?

**Mother:** It is more systematic. You always divide by the smallest prime that works. For 72: divide by 2, get 36. Divide by 2, get 18. Divide by 2, get 9. Now 2 does not divide 9, so try 3. Divide by 3, get 3. Divide by 3, get 1. Done. The left column reads 2, 2, 2, 3, 3 -- the prime factorisation. This method is especially tidy for larger numbers where choosing good tree splits might not be obvious. Use whichever method feels clearer to you.

## HCF and LCM

**Child:** Maa, during Holi our neighbours gave us 40 gujiya and we made 56 thandai glasses. We want to arrange plates so each plate has the same number of gujiya and the same number of thandai glasses, with nothing left over. How many plates can we prepare at most?

**Mother:** You need the largest number that divides both 40 and 56 evenly. That is the Highest Common Factor, or HCF.

**Child:** Let me try using prime factorisation. 40 equals 2 cubed times 5. And 56 equals 2 cubed times 7.

**Mother:** Now pick out only the primes common to both, taking the lower power of each.

**Child:** 2 is common, with power 3 in both. 5 appears only in 40, and 7 only in 56. So HCF equals 2 cubed, which is 8. We can prepare 8 plates!

**Mother:** Each plate gets 40 divided by 8, which is 5 gujiya, and 56 divided by 8, which is 7 glasses of thandai. Now let me give you an LCM problem. Two traffic signals at a crossing reset on different cycles. One turns green every 40 seconds, the other every 56 seconds. If they both turn green at the same moment, after how many seconds will that happen again?

**Child:** I need the smallest number that both 40 and 56 divide into. That is the LCM. I take the highest power of each prime factor. 2 cubed from both, 5 from 40, and 7 from 56. LCM equals 8 times 5 times 7, which is 280 seconds.

**Mother:** Well done. And can you verify using the HCF-LCM relationship?

**Child:** HCF times LCM should equal the product of the two numbers. 8 times 280 equals 2,240. And 40 times 56 also equals 2,240. It checks out!

**Mother:** Perfect. Remember the pattern: HCF is for splitting things into the largest possible equal groups. LCM is for finding when repeating events line up again. In everyday life, HCF helps when you are distributing or cutting things equally, and LCM helps when you are scheduling or synchronising things that repeat at different intervals.

**Child:** Like arranging chairs in equal rows for a school assembly, or figuring out when two trains that depart at different intervals will leave at the same time again.

**Mother:** Exactly. Those are both classic uses.
