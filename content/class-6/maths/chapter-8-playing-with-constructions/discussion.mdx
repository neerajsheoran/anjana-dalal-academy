## Why Only Two Tools?

**Child:** Maa, in geometry class today the teacher said we should only use a compass and a plain ruler — no measuring! Why can't I just use my protractor and scale for everything?

**Mother:** Because compass constructions give you something measurement cannot — theoretical perfection. When you read a scale, your eye might misread by half a millimetre. But when a compass swings an arc, the distance stays locked in. There is zero reading error.

**Child:** So it is more accurate than a ruler?

**Mother:** In principle, yes. And this idea is ancient. The Greek mathematician Euclid wrote about it around 300 BCE. But beta, our Indian tradition goes back even further. The Sulba Sutras — texts from roughly 800 to 200 BCE — describe how priests built Vedic fire altars with precise shapes using only ropes and wooden pegs.

**Child:** Ropes and pegs? That sounds like a giant compass!

**Mother:** Exactly the same idea. Stretch a rope taut from a peg and you have a compass. Those priests needed altars of very specific areas — doubling a square's area, converting a rectangle into a square of equal area — and they worked it all out geometrically, centuries before the Greeks.

## Understanding the Perpendicular Bisector

**Child:** Maa, I drew the perpendicular bisector today in class, but I don't really get why those arcs give a line at 90°.

**Mother:** Let me walk you through the reasoning. You start with a segment — say from point A to point B. You open your compass wider than half that segment and draw arcs from both ends. The arcs cross above and below the segment.

**Child:** Yes, they cross at two points. I connected those points and got the bisector.

**Mother:** Now think about any point on the arc drawn from A. Every such point is the same distance from A — that is the definition of an arc. Similarly, every point on the arc from B is equidistant from B. Where the two arcs cross, that point is equally far from both A and B.

**Child:** Oh! So the crossing point sits exactly in between A and B?

**Mother:** Not just in between — it is on a special line where every single point is equidistant from A and B. That line must pass through the midpoint of AB and must stand perpendicular to it. It is not magic — it is pure logic.

**Child:** That is so satisfying! And we can use it to find the centre of anything?

**Mother:** Yes. Suppose you are making a banner for your school's annual day and you need to find the exact centre of a bamboo stick without a measuring tape. Just use the perpendicular bisector method with a thread and a chalk — same principle, different tools.

## Why 60° Works So Neatly

**Child:** Maa, when I construct a 60° angle, I draw an arc and then mark off the same radius on it. Why does that give exactly 60°?

**Mother:** Because you end up creating an equilateral triangle without realising it. The centre of the arc, the point where the arc starts on the ray, and the new mark — all three are exactly one compass-opening apart. Three equal sides means an equilateral triangle, and every angle in an equilateral triangle is 60°.

**Child:** And for 120°, I just step the compass twice along the arc?

**Mother:** Right — two 60° jumps give you 120°. For 30°, bisect 60°. For 45°, first build 90° and then bisect it. Each new angle grows from one you already know how to make. It is like building with blocks — once you have the basic shapes, you can combine them into anything.

**Child:** So I could make 75° too?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Build 60° and 90° from the same ray. The gap between them is 30°. Bisect that gap and you land at 75°. Or think of it as 45° + 30° — build each separately and add them. There are multiple routes to the same answer.

## When Triangles Refuse to Form

**Child:** Maa, I tried to construct a triangle with sides 2 cm, 3 cm, and 8 cm, but the arcs never met. What went wrong?

**Mother:** Nothing went wrong — you discovered something important. Those three lengths simply cannot form a triangle. Add the two shorter sides: 2 plus 3 is 5, and 5 is less than 8. The two shorter sides are not long enough to bridge the gap of the longest side.

**Child:** Like trying to stretch two small rubber bands between points that are too far apart?

**Mother:** Perfect analogy. This rule is called the **Triangle Inequality**: every side of a triangle must be shorter than the sum of the other two sides. If even one combination fails, no triangle exists.

**Child:** So before starting any SSS construction, I should check all three combinations?

**Mother:** Smart practice. Check that each side is less than the sum of the other two. If all three checks pass, your arcs will definitely cross and you will get a triangle.

## Geometry in Indian Art

**Child:** Maa, today the teacher showed us a regular hexagon inscribed in a circle. It was amazing — exactly six compass steps brought us back to the starting point!

**Mother:** That happens because the side of a regular hexagon equals the radius of the circle it sits inside. So each compass step covers exactly one side, and six sides make the full circle — 6 times 60° is 360°.

**Child:** Can hexagons be used to make patterns?

**Mother:** They are everywhere! Look at the honeycomb pattern — bees build hexagonal cells because hexagons fit together with no wasted space. In Indian architecture, the stone jali screens at places like Sidi Saiyyed Mosque in Ahmedabad use hexagonal and star patterns carved with geometric precision.

**Child:** And rangoli? We make rangoli every Diwali with circles and arcs.

**Mother:** Rangoli is pure geometry, beta. Kolam patterns in Tamil Nadu start with a dot grid and weave curves around them — circles, arcs, and symmetric loops. The artists may not call it "compass and straightedge construction," but the underlying principles are identical. When you practise these constructions in your notebook, you are learning the same skills that temple sculptors, rangoli artists, and architects have used for centuries.

**Child:** So maths class is really an art class in disguise!

**Mother:** And art is maths in disguise. They have always been inseparable.
