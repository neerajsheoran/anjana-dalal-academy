## The Blue Planet

If you look at a photograph of Earth taken from space, the most striking colour is **blue**. That blue is water — and there is a lot of it. Almost **three-fourths** of our planet's surface is covered with water. Only about one-fourth is land.

That is why the first astronauts who saw Earth from space gave it a loving nickname — **the Blue Planet**. It is the only planet we know that has so much liquid water on its surface.

The huge water bodies you see are called **oceans**. The brown and green patches in between are large pieces of land called **continents**.

## Oceans of the Earth

There are **five major oceans**. From largest to smallest:

1. **Pacific Ocean** — the biggest of them all
2. **Atlantic Ocean** — second largest
3. **Indian Ocean** — third largest
4. **Southern Ocean** (also called the Antarctic Ocean) — surrounds Antarctica
5. **Arctic Ocean** — the smallest, around the North Pole

### Are They Really Separate?

If you look closely at a globe, you will notice something interesting. The oceans are **not really separate**. They are all connected. The lines we draw between them on maps are just human conventions — agreed-upon names. The water itself flows freely from one ocean into another.

That is why a fish can technically swim from the Indian Ocean all the way to the Arctic Ocean — even if it would freeze along the way!

### India's Special Place — The Indian Ocean

India sits along the northern edge of the Indian Ocean. On India's western side, this ocean is called the **Arabian Sea**. On its eastern side, it is called the **Bay of Bengal**.

These are not separate oceans — they are parts of the larger Indian Ocean, with their own names because they touch India's coastline.

India also has two important island groups:
- The **Andaman and Nicobar Islands** in the Bay of Bengal
- The **Lakshadweep Islands** in the Arabian Sea

### Salt Water vs Fresh Water

The water in oceans is **salty**. We cannot drink it directly. Our bodies need **fresh water** — the kind found in rivers, lakes, glaciers, and below the ground.

But here is the surprising part. Of all the water on Earth:
- About **97%** is salty ocean water
- Only about **3%** is fresh water
- And most of that fresh water is locked up in **glaciers** and **icecaps**

That is why even though our planet looks blue and watery from space, fresh water is actually quite precious. Saving water at home, at school, and in our communities matters a great deal.

## The Many Faces of the Ocean

The ocean is full of life. The plant life in the sea — like algae and seaweeds — is called **marine flora**. The animal life — like fish, dolphins, whales, sharks, turtles, octopuses, and corals — is called **marine fauna**.

Some marine creatures live near the surface where sunlight reaches. Others live deep down in the dark where almost no light penetrates. Strange glow-in-the-dark fish, giant squids, and creatures we still do not fully understand live in those deep waters.

### Oceans and the Weather

Oceans do not just hold life — they also shape the weather. Heat from the Sun warms the ocean's surface, and the water evaporates into the air. This water vapour rises, forms clouds, and falls back as rain.

The **monsoon rains** that we wait for every summer in India come from this process. Warm winds from the Indian Ocean carry moisture inland, and that moisture becomes the rain that grows our crops. Without the ocean, there would be no monsoon. Without the monsoon, our farms would dry up.

### When the Ocean Turns Dangerous

Oceans can also cause disasters:

- **Cyclones** — violent rotating storms with very strong winds and heavy rain, formed over warm ocean waters. The east coast of India, especially Odisha and Andhra Pradesh, often faces cyclones from the Bay of Bengal.

- **Tsunamis** — gigantic waves caused by earthquakes or volcanic eruptions under the sea. A tsunami can travel thousands of kilometres and crash into coasts with terrible force.

On **26 December 2004**, a powerful earthquake near Indonesia triggered a tsunami across the Indian Ocean. More than **two lakh people** lost their lives across 14 countries. In India, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and the coasts of Tamil Nadu and Kerala were badly hit.

After this tragedy, India and several other countries built the **Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning System**. Today, sensors in the ocean can detect a tsunami early and warn coastal communities so that lives can be saved.

India also has its own **National Disaster Management Authority** to plan and respond to such emergencies.

## Continents of the Earth

Counting continents is trickier than you might think. The answer depends on how you group the landmasses. Most commonly used today is the **seven-continent system**:

1. **Asia** — the largest continent; includes India
2. **Africa** — second largest
3. **North America**
4. **South America**
5. **Antarctica** — covered in ice, no permanent human population
6. **Europe**
7. **Australia** — the smallest

Some scientists prefer five or six continents because Europe and Asia are really one connected landmass (called **Eurasia**), and North and South America are connected too. But for practical purposes, most schoolbooks and atlases use seven.

### The Olympic Connection

Have you noticed the **Olympic rings** symbol? Five interlocking rings — blue, yellow, black, green, red. They stand for the **five inhabited continents** that send athletes to the Games: Africa, America (counted as one), Asia, Australia, and Europe. Antarctica is left out because no one permanently lives there.

## What if There Were No Oceans?

Sit and think for a moment. If the oceans disappeared, what would happen?

- No clouds, no rain — our crops would die, and so would we.
- No fish or seafood — millions of people who eat from the sea would lose food.
- The weather would become extremely hot in some places and freezing cold in others, because oceans help spread heat around the planet.
- Hundreds of thousands of marine species would vanish forever.

The oceans are not just a beautiful blue background. They are a working part of the planet that keeps everything else alive.

## Before We Close

- Earth is mostly water — about three-fourths of its surface.
- There are five oceans: Pacific (largest), Atlantic, Indian, Southern, Arctic.
- India lies on the **Indian Ocean**, with the **Arabian Sea** to its west and the **Bay of Bengal** to its east.
- There are about seven continents in the most common counting.
- Oceans bring us rain (the monsoon), food, and life — but also cyclones and tsunamis.
- The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami led to the building of an early warning system that protects countries today.
