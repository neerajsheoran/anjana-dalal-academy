## What Strikes a Visitor First

If a traveller from another country lands in India, the first thing they notice is **diversity**. Walk down the street, and you may hear three different languages within five minutes. Pass a few shops, and you may see clothes, food, and decorations from very different traditions. Visit two cities and you may feel you have visited two countries.

This is one of the things people love about India — its **rich diversity**.

But here is the puzzle. Despite all these differences, India is **one country**. How does that work?

The answer is captured in a phrase that has become almost a national motto: **"unity in diversity"** — or as the philosopher-poet Sri Aurobindo put it, **"the Many in the One."**

This chapter explores what that means.

## The Sheer Scale of Indian Diversity

India is home to about **1.4 billion people** — roughly **18% of the world's population**. With such a huge population, diversity is natural. But the scale of it is still astonishing.

The **People of India project** by the Anthropological Survey of India studied **4,635 communities** across all states. They counted:

- **325 languages** — used by everyday people
- **25 scripts** — different writing systems
- Countless dialects, customs, traditions

A child in Tamil Nadu might grow up speaking Tamil, eating sambar-rice, celebrating Pongal. A child in Punjab grows up speaking Punjabi, eating roti-dal, celebrating Lohri. A child in Assam speaks Assamese, eats bhaat-doi, celebrates Bihu. All three are Indian. Yet their daily lives can feel quite different.

How do we hold all of this together?

The British historian Vincent Smith once wondered:

> "How, in the face of such bewildering diversity, can a history of India be written? ... The answer is found in the fact that India offers unity in diversity."

Let us look at a few specific examples.

## Food — Same Ingredients, Endless Dishes

Step into any Indian kitchen, and you will likely find some common ingredients:

- **Grains**: rice, wheat, barley
- **Millets**: jowar, bajra, ragi
- **Pulses**: dals — moong, masoor, chana, arhar, rajma
- **Spices**: turmeric, cumin, cardamom, ginger
- **Oils**: mustard, coconut, groundnut, sesame

These are the **staple ingredients** used across the country. So in one sense, every Indian kitchen has much in common with every other.

But what gets made from these ingredients varies enormously. A handful of rice can become:

- **Idli or dosa** in Tamil Nadu
- **Khichdi** in Uttar Pradesh
- **Pakhala** (fermented rice) in Odisha
- **Pulao** in Mughal-style kitchens
- **Bisi bele bath** in Karnataka
- **Patishapta** in Bengal

So the same ingredients (unity) produce an endless variety of dishes (diversity). This is exactly the principle of "unity in diversity" on a plate.

## Clothing — One Sari, A Thousand Styles

The **sari** is perhaps the perfect example. It is a long piece of cloth — usually about 5 to 9 metres — that can be draped in many ways. The exact same idea (one length of cloth) becomes countless different dresses depending on:

- **The fabric**: cotton, silk, synthetic, jute, khadi
- **The weave and design**: Banarasi, Kanjivaram, Paithani, Patan Patola, Muga, Mysore, Chanderi, Pochampally, Sambalpuri
- **The colour and print**: hand-printed, block-printed, woven patterns, embroidery, mirror-work
- **The drape**: the Nivi (most common today), the Bengali style, the Coorgi style, the Maharashtrian "nauvari," the Gujarati "seedha pallu," and many more

A stone carving from **Vaishali** in Bihar, more than 2,000 years old, shows a woman wearing a sari. So this dress is at least that ancient — and is still worn today.

### Indian Cotton in the World

India produced some of the finest **cotton textiles** in the world for centuries. In the 17th century, a printed Indian cotton called **chintz** became so popular in Europe that European dressmakers feared losing their business. England and France actually **banned the import of Indian chintz** to protect their own products!

Indian fabric was once a global luxury — well before the Industrial Revolution.

The **dhoti** is another example — one length of cloth, draped in many regional ways. The point is the same: a simple base, dressed up in many styles.

## Festivals — Same Day, Many Names

Here is something fascinating. The same festival is often celebrated **across India on the same day**, but under different names and with different local customs.

Take **Makara Sankranti** (around January 14), which marks the beginning of the harvest season:

| Region | Name |
|---|---|
| North India | Makar Sankranti / Khichdi Parv |
| Punjab | Lohri / Maghi |
| Gujarat | Uttarayan |
| Bengal | Poush Songkranti |
| Tamil Nadu | Pongal |
| Andhra | Pedda Panduga |
| Karnataka | Makara Sankramana |
| Kerala | Makara Vilakku |
| Assam | Magh Bihu |
| Kashmir | Shishur Saenkraat |

All celebrate the same astronomical event — the Sun moving into the zodiac sign of Capricorn (Makara). All mark the harvest. All involve gathering, special foods, and gratitude. But each region has its own name, food, songs, and traditions.

Similar patterns exist for many festivals — **Diwali, Holi, Navratri, Onam, Baisakhi, Dussehra**. The same human experiences — harvest, new year, victory of good over evil, change of seasons — find expression through endless local variations.

## The Two Great Epics

India's two epics — the **Ramayana** and the **Mahabharata** — are perhaps the most powerful examples of unity in diversity.

### Stories Older Than Most Civilisations

The **Ramayana** tells the story of Rama, his wife Sita, his brother Lakshmana, and the help of Hanuman in defeating the demon-king Ravana who kidnapped Sita.

The **Mahabharata** narrates the great war between the **Pandavas** (with Krishna's help) and their cousins the **Kauravas**, fought over a kingdom and over what is right.

Together they fill about **7,000 pages** in their original Sanskrit versions. Within these epics are hundreds of smaller stories that explore values, dilemmas, and what it means to live a good life.

### Travelled Far Beyond India

These two stories have spread across India and far beyond — to **Indonesia, Cambodia, Thailand, Vietnam, Sri Lanka, Nepal**. The 9th-century Indonesian Hindu epic poem the **Kakawin Ramayana** is one of the great pieces of Javanese literature. The Cambodian **Reamker** and Thai **Ramakien** are local Ramayanas. Each country adapted the story to its own culture.

### A Hundred Versions of the Mahabharata in One Indian State Alone

A scholar once surveyed **Tamil Nadu** alone and found **about a hundred different folk versions** of the Mahabharata. Different regions, different communities, different storytellers — each with their own version. Now imagine the number for **all of India**.

Tribal communities — the **Bhils, Gonds, Mundas**, and many tribes of the Northeast and the Himalayas — have their **own versions** of the Ramayana and Mahabharata. Each version connects the great epics to **local landmarks**, **local heroes**, **local legends**. According to one tribal story, the Pandavas passed through their region; according to another, Rama and Sita rested under a specific tree.

This is unity in diversity at its richest. **One story, told in thousands of ways across thousands of years**, all over the country — yet recognised everywhere.

### What the Epics Carried

The anthropologist K.S. Singh observed:

> "There is hardly a place in the country which the epic heroes such as the Pandavas did not visit according to folklore."

The epics created a **dense web of cultural connection** across India. Different regions, different languages, different customs — yet a shared imagination, shared heroes, shared questions about right and wrong.

## The Panchatantra — Indian Stories That Conquered the World

Another great Indian collection of stories is the **Panchatantra** — animal tales with deep moral lessons. The original Sanskrit version is at least **2,200 years old**.

Over time, the Panchatantra has been **translated into more than 50 languages**, with around **200 different adaptations** worldwide. Stories that started in India inspired story collections in **Persia, the Arab world, and Europe**. Many tales we hear from European storytellers today have roots in the Panchatantra.

A single ancient Indian story collection, woven into the storytelling traditions of half the world.

## Why Does Indian Diversity Not Divide India?

Here is the deepest question of this chapter. Many countries with this much diversity would have fallen apart. Why has India stayed together?

A few reasons:

**1. Shared values across traditions.**
Whether it is Vedanta, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, folk traditions, or tribal beliefs — they share core ideas: dharma, karma, ahimsa, compassion, respect for elders, respect for nature, family bonds, the search for liberation.

**2. Shared sacred geography.**
Pilgrimage routes connect India end-to-end. A pilgrim from Tamil Nadu may visit Varanasi. A devotee from Punjab may travel to Rameswaram. The Char Dham yatra goes to four corners of the country. Indians have always thought of the whole Subcontinent as one sacred land.

**3. Shared stories.**
As we saw, the Ramayana and Mahabharata are everywhere, in countless versions. Other texts like the Panchatantra, the Jataka tales, and the Puranas have travelled across all regions.

**4. Shared festivals.**
Same astronomical events, same harvest seasons, same change of seasons — celebrated under many names but with shared feeling.

**5. A culture of acceptance.**
Indian thought has always recognised "the One in the Many." The Vedic verse **ekam sat vipra bahudha vadanti** — "Truth is one, sages call it by many names" — captures this attitude. It allows different traditions to coexist without one trying to destroy another.

**6. Constant exchange.**
Indians have intermarried, traded, migrated, and shared cultural ideas across regions for thousands of years. The diversity is not separated into sealed boxes — it flows between groups and enriches everyone.

## Before We Close

- India is one of the **most diverse countries** in the world — 325 languages, 25 scripts, 4,635 communities studied in the People of India project.
- This diversity is visible in **food, clothing, festivals, languages, dances, music, customs**.
- But there is also a **deep underlying unity** — shared staples, shared festivals (under many names), shared epics (in many versions), shared values.
- The **sari** and the **Makara Sankranti** festival are great examples of "unity in diversity."
- The **Ramayana and Mahabharata** have been retold in hundreds of forms across India and Asia — creating a dense web of cultural connection.
- The **Panchatantra** travelled from India to half the world.
- India celebrates diversity as **enrichment**, not division. The Many in the One.
