## What Makes a "Civilisation"?

In the previous chapter, we saw humans move from wandering hunter-gatherers to settled farmers in villages. The next big leap happened when some of those villages grew so large and organised that they became something new — a **civilisation**.

A civilisation is more than just a big village. It usually has:

- **Government and administration** — to manage many people and decisions
- **Cities and town planning** — buildings, roads, water supply, drainage
- **Specialised crafts** — pottery, jewellery, metalwork
- **Trade** — exchange of goods locally and with distant places
- **Writing** — to keep records and communicate
- **Cultural ideas** — art, architecture, religion, customs
- **Productive agriculture** — enough food to feed not just farmers but also city-dwellers

Civilisations did not appear at the same time everywhere. **Mesopotamia** (today's Iraq and Syria) had a civilisation by about **4,000 BCE**. **Egypt** followed a few centuries later. And in India, our first great civilisation rose around **2600 BCE** — about 4,500 years ago — in the northwest part of the Subcontinent.

## The Indus-Sarasvati Civilisation

You may have heard names like **Indus Valley civilisation**, **Harappan civilisation**, **Indus civilisation**, or **Sindhu-Sarasvati civilisation**. They all refer to **the same** civilisation.

Why so many names?

- **Harappan** — because the first city of this civilisation was discovered in **1920–21** at a place called **Harappa** (in today's Pakistani Punjab). That early discovery gave the whole civilisation its first name.
- **Indus** — because the first cities to be excavated were along the **Indus River**.
- **Indus Valley** — early scholars thought the civilisation was limited to the Indus valley.
- **Sindhu-Sarasvati** or **Indus-Sarasvati** — used today because we now know the civilisation also extended along a once-mighty river called the **Sarasvati**, which flowed further east.

So we will use **Indus-Sarasvati civilisation** or **Harappan civilisation** in this chapter.

### When and Where

- **Time period:** about **2600 BCE to 1900 BCE** — flourishing for about 700 years
- **Region:** today's Pakistan, north-west and western India (Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, Gujarat) and parts of Afghanistan

### Some of the Main Cities

| City | Modern location |
|---|---|
| **Harappa** | Punjab, Pakistan |
| **Mohenjo-daro** | Sindh, Pakistan |
| **Dholavira** | Gujarat, India |
| **Rakhigarhi** | Haryana, India |
| **Kalibangan** | Rajasthan, India |
| **Lothal** | Gujarat, India |
| **Ganweriwala** | Cholistan, Pakistan |

There were hundreds of smaller settlements too. This was one of the **largest civilisations** of the ancient world by area.

### The Sarasvati River

The map of these settlements shows two clusters — one along the **Indus** and its tributaries (Harappa, Mohenjo-daro), and another along a river called the **Sarasvati** (Rakhigarhi, Kalibangan, Banawali).

The Sarasvati is first mentioned in the **Rig Veda**, where it is worshipped both as a goddess and as a river flowing "from the mountain to the sea." Later texts describe it drying up. Today, this once-mighty river survives only as a seasonal stream — called **Ghaggar** in India and **Hakra** in Pakistan. Many Harappan cities along its banks were abandoned when the river dried up — and this may be one reason the civilisation declined.

## Town Planning — Cities Built to a Plan

The Harappan cities were not chaotic, organic settlements. They were **planned**.

### Wide, Straight Streets

The streets ran in grid patterns — often aligned with the **cardinal directions** (north-south and east-west). Wide main roads were crossed by smaller lanes at right angles, much like modern planned cities like Chandigarh.

### Two Parts to Each City

Most cities had two clearly separated zones:

- **The Upper Town (Citadel)** — usually on a raised platform, surrounded by thick walls. This area probably housed the **rulers, priests, administrators** (the **elite**) and had important public buildings.
- **The Lower Town** — for common people. Larger area, smaller buildings, but still well-organised.

Some cities like **Dholavira** had three zones — an upper, middle, and lower town.

### Houses for Everyone

A surprising thing — the **quality of construction was similar** for big and small houses. Bricks of standard sizes were used everywhere. Most houses had:

- Multiple rooms
- An open courtyard
- A bathroom with sloping floor and outlet
- A well in some cases

This suggests Harappan society was not extremely unequal. Rich and poor lived nearby with similar basic comforts.

### The Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro

The most famous Harappan structure is the **Great Bath** at Mohenjo-daro — a small but beautifully made tank, about **12 metres long and 7 metres wide**, lined with carefully fitted bricks and waterproofed with natural tar (bitumen). It had steps leading in and out, small rooms around it, a well to fill it, and a drain to empty it.

What was it for? Scholars still debate this. Some say it was for **religious or ritual bathing**. Others say it was for the **royal family** or the elite. Since most houses had private bathrooms, it probably wasn't a public swimming pool. Whatever its purpose, it shows how seriously the Harappans treated water and cleanliness.

## Water Management — The Real Engineering Marvel

If there is one thing the Harappans were truly remarkable at, it was **managing water**.

### Drainage

Almost every house was connected to a network of **covered drains** that ran beneath the streets. Wastewater from baths and kitchens flowed into these drains, then to larger covered channels, and finally out of the city. **No other ancient civilisation had drainage this advanced** until much later.

### Wells

In Mohenjo-daro alone, archaeologists have counted about **700 brick-lined wells**. Many houses had their own well. Others used public wells. This means people had access to clean water without having to walk to a distant river.

### Reservoirs at Dholavira

The city of **Dholavira** in Gujarat is in a dry region — the Rann of Kutch. The Harappans built **at least six large reservoirs** to capture and store rainwater. The biggest reservoir was **73 metres long** — longer than half a football field. Some were cut directly out of solid rock.

Water flowed from the reservoirs into the city through **underground channels**. This careful water harvesting kept Dholavira alive in a desert climate. Modern engineers studying it are amazed.

This tells us something important: the Harappans built cities where they wanted them and **engineered solutions** to local problems. They were not just passive farmers — they were planners and engineers.

## What Did the Harappans Eat?

Archaeologists have found grains, bones, and even chemical traces inside cooking pots. The Harappan diet included:

- **Grains:** wheat, barley, some rice, millets
- **Pulses:** lentils, beans, peas
- **Vegetables** and fruits
- **Meat:** from domesticated cattle, sheep, goats; also fish from rivers and the sea
- **Dairy products:** ghee, curd, butter
- **Spices:** turmeric, ginger have been detected in old pots
- **Bananas** — yes, even these!

They were also the **first people in Eurasia** to grow **cotton**, which they wove into cloth. Indian cotton remained famous for thousands of years afterwards.

They used the **plough** for farming — small clay model ploughs have been found. The basic design of these ancient ploughs is similar to ones still used by farmers in parts of rural India today.

## Crafts and Trade

The Harappans were excellent craftspeople. Some of their specialities:

- **Beads** — especially of **carnelian** (a reddish stone from Gujarat). They invented special drilling techniques to make tiny holes through these very hard beads. Harappan carnelian beads have been found as far away as **Mesopotamia** and **Egypt** — evidence of long-distance trade!
- **Shell bangles** — cut from large sea-shells. Bangles found in Harappan sites cover the entire arm, a tradition still followed in parts of Gujarat and Rajasthan.
- **Pottery** — well-made and often decorated with patterns
- **Metals** — they mastered **copper** and made **bronze** (copper + tin). Bronze tools, mirrors, figurines, and statues have been found.
- **Ivory** — beautifully carved combs, ornaments, and dice

### Long-Distance Trade

The Harappans traded by **land, river, and sea**. Some sites are far inland; others are along the coast. **Lothal** in Gujarat had a huge brick-lined basin — **217 metres long**, longer than two football grounds — which is widely thought to be a **dockyard** where boats came in and out. If true, this is one of the earliest known dockyards in the world.

Harappan goods went to:
- **Mesopotamia** (Iraq) — beads, ornaments, timber, cotton
- **Iran** and Persian Gulf — many trading colonies
- **Oman** — a Harappan ivory comb has been found there!

In return, the Harappans likely imported **copper, gold, tin, and certain types of stone**.

### Seals — A Mystery That Remains

Thousands of small **stone seals** have been found — typically a few centimetres across, made of **steatite** (a soft stone hardened by heating). Each seal usually shows an animal (a bull, unicorn, tiger, elephant) and some symbols above it.

The symbols are clearly part of a **writing system** — but **nobody has been able to read it yet**. The Harappan script is one of the world's great unsolved mysteries. Until it is deciphered, we will not know the language they spoke or the details of their thoughts.

The seals were probably used by traders — like a stamp on a package — to identify ownership or origin.

## Life and Culture

Many small finds give us glimpses of Harappan life:

- **Toys:** wheeled carts, animal figurines, whistles, gameboards
- **The "Dancing Girl"** — a small bronze figurine from Mohenjo-daro, only about 10.8 cm tall, showing a young woman standing in a confident pose with many bangles on her arm. The bangle style is still seen in Rajasthan and Gujarat today!
- **The "Priest King"** — a stone statue of a bearded man with a band on his forehead. We don't actually know who he represents.
- **A "namaste" figurine** — a small terracotta statue showing a seated person with hands joined — exactly like the namaste gesture used in India today.
- **A swastika seal** — the swastika symbol used in Indian traditions for thousands of years was already present then.
- **A "thirsty crow" pot** — the famous Aesop-like story of a crow dropping pebbles to raise water in a pot is depicted on a Harappan pot from Lothal. It is more than 4,000 years old!

These finds show that **many small habits, gestures, and ornaments** we still see in India today — namaste, bangles, the swastika symbol — have roots stretching back 4,000+ years.

## Why Did the Civilisation Decline?

Around **1900 BCE**, the cities began to be abandoned, one by one. By **1300 BCE**, the great Harappan civilisation was no more — at least not in its urban form.

Why?

The old idea — that invaders destroyed the cities — has been **ruled out**. No evidence of warfare or invasion has been found. The Harappans don't seem to have kept armies or even much in the way of weapons.

Two main causes are now generally agreed:

1. **Climate change** — starting around 2200 BCE, the whole region became drier. Rainfall reduced over many decades. With less water, farming declined, and cities could not be fed.

2. **The drying of the Sarasvati River** — the Sarasvati gradually dried up in its central basin. Cities along it, like Kalibangan and Banawali, were abandoned. Some people may have moved east towards the Ganga plains.

The Harappans returned to **rural living** — small farming villages — because villages need less infrastructure and can survive on local water. The grand city life ended, but **the people, their knowledge, and their culture did not disappear**.

Much of what they invented — pottery techniques, the plough, cotton, bronze tools, town planning ideas, even cultural symbols — passed on to later phases of Indian civilisation. The Harappans are not strangers from a vanished world. They are our ancestors.

## Before We Close

- A **civilisation** has cities, government, crafts, trade, writing, and cultural ideas.
- India's first great civilisation was the **Indus-Sarasvati** (also called Harappan) civilisation, from about **2600 to 1900 BCE**.
- Major cities: **Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Dholavira, Rakhigarhi, Lothal, Kalibangan**.
- The Harappans were excellent at **town planning** and especially at **water management** — wells, drains, and reservoirs.
- They had **rich trade** with Mesopotamia, Iran, and beyond. **Lothal** had perhaps the world's first known dockyard.
- Their writing has not yet been deciphered — one of history's great mysteries.
- The civilisation declined due to **climate change** and the **drying of the Sarasvati River**, but its culture passed on to later India.
