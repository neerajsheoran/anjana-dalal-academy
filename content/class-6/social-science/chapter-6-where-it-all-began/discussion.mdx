## How Did Cities Even Become a Thing?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says villages slowly grew into cities. But it's a huge jump from a village of 50 people to a city like Mohenjo-daro with thousands of people. How did that even happen?

**Mother:** Slowly. Over hundreds of years. The change wasn't sudden. Imagine a village near a river with good farming. The farms produce more food than the village needs. Some people start specialising — one family becomes potters, another becomes carpenters, another becomes traders. They exchange food for pots and tools.

**Child:** Like a market?

**Mother:** Exactly. As more people specialise and trade grows, the village attracts more people. It needs more houses, wider streets, places to store extra food. After a few generations, it's not a village anymore — it's a town. After more generations, it's a city.

**Child:** And every city needs water and drains and rules?

**Mother:** Yes. The more people live together, the more they have to **plan**. In a small village, everyone knows everyone and disputes are settled by elders. In a city of 10,000, you need rules, leaders, marketplaces, sewerage. That is where **administration** comes in. Civilisation is what happens when humans figure out how to live together in large numbers.

**Child:** So civilisation is basically humans solving the problem of being together in big groups?

**Mother:** A very good way to put it. Yes.

## Why Are the Harappan Drains So Famous?

**Child:** Maa, every Class 6 textbook in India talks about Harappan drains. Why is a drainage system such a big deal? It is just... pipes.

**Mother:** Because **no other ancient civilisation had drainage this good**. Not Egypt, not Mesopotamia, not Greece, not Rome for many centuries afterwards. The Harappans were thousands of years ahead in city sanitation.

**Child:** Why does sanitation matter so much?

**Mother:** Think of what happens in a city without drains. Wastewater accumulates. Garbage piles up. Diseases spread quickly because the water gets contaminated. In many old cities, **plagues and epidemics** killed huge numbers of people.

The Harappans designed their cities so that **every house** could connect to a drain, and the drain led out of the city in a planned way. Covered drains. Inspection points. Even something like a modern manhole.

**Child:** So they kept their cities cleaner than most cities for the next 3,000 years?

**Mother:** Yes. In the 19th century, when London was the largest city in the world, **cholera killed thousands** because the drinking water was contaminated by sewage. The British city engineers had to learn — at huge cost in lives — what the Harappans seemed to have figured out 4,000 years earlier.

**Child:** That is amazing and tragic.

**Mother:** History often is.

## Were the Harappans Rich and Poor Like Us?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says that big and small houses in Harappan cities had similar quality of construction. Does that mean there were no rich and poor people?

**Mother:** Probably not. Some people clearly lived in larger houses, in the upper town with thicker walls. Some had more gold and beads and bronze items in their homes. So inequality existed.

But — and this is what is interesting — the gap was not extreme. There are **no giant palaces** or **massive royal tombs** in Harappan cities. Compare that with Egypt, where pharaohs built enormous pyramids while peasants lived in mud huts. Or Mesopotamia, with royal cemeteries full of treasure. The Harappans had no such grand displays of wealth.

**Child:** So they were rich and poor, but not super-rich and super-poor?

**Mother:** That seems right. Even **small houses had bathrooms**. Even ordinary people could afford some ornaments. The Harappans seem to have shared the basics — water, sanitation, shelter — fairly widely.

**Child:** Why was that?

**Mother:** We don't know for sure. Maybe their leaders were not as showy as pharaohs and kings elsewhere. Maybe trade and agriculture spread wealth more evenly. Maybe their culture emphasised collective welfare over personal display. Whatever the reason, it is one of the most distinctive things about this civilisation.

**Child:** So India's first civilisation cared about everyone, not just the rich?

**Mother:** That seems to be a fair reading of the evidence. Some scholars say this is one of the most beautiful things about the Harappans.

## Why Can't We Read Their Writing Yet?

**Child:** Maa, this annoys me. We know the writing existed — there are thousands of seals with symbols. Why can't anyone read it after all these years?

**Mother:** Three problems make it very hard:

**First — the texts are too short.** Most Harappan seals have **only 4 or 5 symbols** on them. That is too little to figure out the patterns of a language. When the famous Egyptian hieroglyphs were deciphered, scholars had the Rosetta Stone with hundreds of words of the same text in three languages. We don't have anything like that for the Harappan script.

**Second — we don't know the language.** Even if we knew what each symbol meant, we wouldn't know what language they were writing in. Was it an early form of Sanskrit? An early Dravidian language? Something else entirely? Without knowing the language, the symbols are like keys to a lock we cannot find.

**Third — no bilingual inscriptions.** No "Rosetta Stone" yet exists for the Harappan script. If we found one inscription with both the Harappan script and another known script saying the same thing, we could probably crack it.

**Child:** So we may never know?

**Mother:** Maybe. Or maybe one day, archaeologists will dig up a longer text — say a clay tablet with hundreds of symbols — that will give us enough patterns to work with. Computer techniques are also helping. Some scholars have made partial progress. But for now, the Harappans' words are silent.

**Child:** That feels sad.

**Mother:** It is. They wrote things down for us, but we don't yet know how to listen.

## What Do the Harappan Objects Tell Us?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter shows the "Dancing Girl," the "Priest King," a namaste figurine, swastika seals, even a thirsty crow story on a pot. Some of these feel completely Indian. Are we really that old?

**Mother:** Yes — that is one of the most striking things about Harappan finds. Many of the **gestures, ornaments, and symbols** we see in everyday Indian life today were already present 4,000 years ago.

- The **namaste gesture** — joined hands — exactly as we do today
- **Bangles covering the entire arm** — still seen in Rajasthan and Gujarat
- The **swastika symbol** — still used in many Indian homes
- The story of the **thirsty crow** — told and retold across India
- A **bronze figurine of a confident woman** — already showing that women could hold a strong, expressive presence

**Child:** So Indian culture didn't suddenly appear in some recent era. Some of it is really old.

**Mother:** Very old. Of course, much has changed and grown. But there is a **continuity** that runs through Indian culture from the Harappan times to today. That continuity is part of why India is sometimes called one of the oldest **continuous** civilisations in the world.

**Child:** Were the Harappans Indians the way we are Indians?

**Mother:** They lived in the Indian Subcontinent. Many of them likely spoke languages ancestral to the languages we still speak. Many of their customs continued in some form. Their genes mixed with later populations, and many of us in India today carry some of their ancestry. They were one of the threads from which we are woven.

## Why Did Such a Great Civilisation Decline?

**Child:** Maa, if the Harappans were so advanced — drains, cities, trade, crafts — why did their civilisation fall apart? Egypt's civilisation lasted thousands of years longer. Why did ours collapse?

**Mother:** It is a very important question. The simple answer is **environmental change**.

**1. Climate became drier.** Around 4,200 years ago — about 2200 BCE — the whole region from the Mediterranean to South Asia began to get less rain. This dry phase lasted hundreds of years. With less water, crops failed. Less food meant cities could not survive.

**2. The Sarasvati River dried up.** This was perhaps the biggest blow. The Sarasvati had been a mighty river — mentioned in the Rig Veda as flowing from the mountains to the sea. Around 1900 BCE, it began to shrink. Cities along it — Kalibangan, Banawali — had to be abandoned. Imagine if the Ganga suddenly began drying up today. Cities like Varanasi and Kolkata would face enormous problems.

**Child:** Could they not just dig more wells?

**Mother:** They tried. But cities depend on **enough food** coming in from surrounding farms. When the rains failed and the river died, the farms couldn't produce enough. The cities couldn't be fed. People had no choice but to leave and go back to villages where they could grow their own food.

**Child:** Was it slow or sudden?

**Mother:** Slow — over a few generations. The Harappans probably didn't realise their civilisation was ending. Each year was just a bit worse than the last. By the time they noticed, the trend was unstoppable.

**Child:** That feels scary. Like a warning for us too.

**Mother:** It is. Modern climate change, water scarcity, and ground water depletion are exactly the same kinds of slow problems. The Harappans show us that even a great civilisation can fail if it doesn't respect its environment. Their fate is a lesson we should listen to.

## A Civilisation That Doesn't Brag

**Child:** Maa, I have noticed something. The Egyptians built giant pyramids. The Mesopotamians built tall ziggurats. The Greeks and Romans had huge statues. The Harappans... built drains, wells, and small bronze figurines. They didn't seem to want to show off.

**Mother:** You noticed something many scholars have noticed. The Harappans had a **quiet kind of greatness**. They focused on **everyday life** — water for everyone, sanitation, planned streets, good farming, fair trade. They did not waste enormous resources on monuments for kings or gods.

**Child:** So they were practical rather than showy?

**Mother:** Yes. And in some ways, that is even more impressive. Building a giant pyramid is amazing engineering, but it doesn't improve life for ordinary people. Building city-wide drainage and a network of wells does. The Harappans seemed to care about **the daily life of regular people** in a way few other ancient civilisations did.

**Child:** Maybe that is why we should be proud of them?

**Mother:** I think so. India's oldest civilisation was not a civilisation of pharaohs and emperors who left grand monuments. It was a civilisation of careful planners who left behind clean streets, clever water systems, beautiful crafts, and the quiet message that **everyone deserves a decent life**. That is a wonderful inheritance for any modern country to carry forward.
