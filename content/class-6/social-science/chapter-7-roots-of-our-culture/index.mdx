## A Tree With Many Roots

If Indian culture were a tree, it would be a very, very old one. Its **roots** go thousands of years back. Its **trunk** carries shared concepts that connect every part. Its **branches** include art, literature, science, medicine, schools of thought, philosophy, and music.

This chapter looks at some of the deepest roots that shaped the personality of our country.

The earliest roots trace back to the **Indus-Sarasvati civilisation** we studied in the last chapter. Layer upon layer was added over time. By the **1st millennium BCE** (the 1,000 years before Jesus' birth), many great schools of thought had emerged — the Vedas, Upanishads, Buddhism, Jainism, and others.

Let us walk through them.

## The Vedas — India's Oldest Texts

The word **Veda** comes from the Sanskrit root **vid** meaning "knowledge" — the same root we still hear in **vidya** (learning).

There are **four Vedas**:
1. **Rig Veda** — the oldest
2. **Yajur Veda**
3. **Sama Veda**
4. **Atharva Veda**

The Vedas are **collections of hymns** — prayers in the form of poems and songs. They are some of the **oldest texts in the world**.

### Composed Orally, Not Written

The Vedas were not written down for a very long time. They were composed in an early form of **Sanskrit**, by **rishis** (male seers) and **rishikas** (female seers), in the **Sapta Sindhava** region — the land of the seven rivers in the northwest of the Subcontinent.

Different scholars date the Rig Veda anywhere between the **5th millennium BCE** and the **2nd millennium BCE**. So for **100 to 200 generations**, these hymns were memorised and passed down **orally** — recited word-perfect from teacher to student.

This careful oral transmission is so remarkable that **UNESCO** in 2008 recognised Vedic chanting as a **"masterpiece of the oral and intangible heritage of humanity."**

### What the Vedas Talk About

The hymns address many deities — **Indra** (rains and storms), **Agni** (fire), **Varuna** (cosmic order), **Mitra**, **Sarasvati** (the river goddess and goddess of learning), **Ushas** (dawn), and many more.

But — and this is important — the early seers did **not** see these gods as completely separate beings. A famous verse says:

> **ekam sat vipra bahudha vadanti**
> "The Existent (the supreme reality) is one, but sages give it many names."

This idea — that **the divine is ultimately one**, though we may approach it through many forms — is one of the most fundamental ideas in Indian culture.

The Vedas also strongly value **unity among people**. The last hymns of the Rig Veda say:

> "Come together, speak together; common be your mind, may your thoughts agree...
> United be your purpose, united your heart...
> may your thoughts be united, so all may agree!"

### Vedic Society

Early Vedic society was made up of **janas** or **clans** — like the Bharatas, Purus, Kurus, Yadus. The Rig Veda mentions more than 30 such janas.

They had leaders called **raja** (king or chief) and assemblies called **sabha** and **samiti**. Many professions are mentioned — farmer, weaver, potter, builder, carpenter, healer, dancer, barber, priest. Society was complex even then.

## The Upanishads — Asking the Big Questions

After the Vedas came another group of texts called the **Upanishads**. These took the Vedic ideas further and asked deeper questions:
- What is the **self** (atman)?
- What is the **ultimate reality** (brahman)?
- What happens **after death**?
- Why do good or bad actions affect us (karma)?

### Brahman — The One Reality

The school of thought called **Vedanta** says everything in existence — humans, animals, nature, the universe — is at heart one single divine reality called **brahman**. (Not to be confused with the god **Brahma**.)

Two famous Upanishadic verses express this idea simply:

> **aham brahmasmi** — "I am brahman" (I am divine in my deepest self)
>
> **tat tvam asi** — "You are That"

### Atman — The Self Inside Every Being

The Upanishads taught that within every living being is the **atman** — the divine self. The atman is ultimately the same as brahman. So **everything is connected**, and everything is part of one whole.

This leads naturally to one of the most beautiful Indian prayers:

> **sarve bhavantu sukhinah, sarve santu niramayah**
> "May all beings be happy, may all beings be free from illness..."

### A Story from the Upanishads

In the **Chandogya Upanishad**, a young man named **Shvetaketu** comes home after 12 years of study, full of pride. His father, the rishi **Uddalaka**, asks him about the nature of brahman. Shvetaketu doesn't know.

So his father shows him a banyan seed. "What is inside?" Almost nothing — yet inside that tiny seed is a whole banyan tree. The same essence — brahman — is everywhere, in every being, even though we cannot see it. Uddalaka concludes:

> "Everything consists of this subtle essence... **You are That**, Shvetaketu."

This is a beautiful lesson that wisdom is not about how many texts you memorise but about understanding the deeper reality of life.

### Other Stories

- **Nachiketa**, a young boy, travels to Yama (the god of death) and questions him about what happens after death. Yama eventually teaches him that the atman is immortal — neither born nor dies.

- **Gargi**, a learned rishika, debates with the famous rishi **Yajnavalkya** in the court of King Janaka. She asks him deep questions about the nature of brahman. This story shows that **women too were great philosophers** in ancient India.

## Yoga — Realising the Truth Within

Out of the Vedas came another school called **Yoga**. Today most people think of yoga as **yoga asanas** (physical postures). But the original Yoga was a complete **method for the mind and body** to realise brahman in one's own consciousness.

It includes:
- Physical postures (asanas)
- Breath control (pranayama)
- Concentration (dharana)
- Meditation (dhyana)
- Ethical principles like truthfulness and non-violence

Yoga is one of India's gifts to the world. Today, **International Yoga Day** is celebrated on June 21st across the globe.

The Vedanta and Yoga schools, along with several others, became the foundations of what we call **Hinduism** today.

## Buddhism — The Path of the Awakened One

Around **2,500 years ago**, in a small kingdom in what is today Nepal, a young prince named **Siddhartha Gautama** was born — in a place called **Lumbini** (about 560 BCE, though dates vary by source).

He grew up in great comfort in his palace. But at age 29, on a chariot ride through the city, he saw four sights that changed his life forever:
1. An **old man**
2. A **sick man**
3. A **dead body**
4. An **ascetic** who appeared calm and at peace

Why is there suffering in life? Why must everyone grow old, fall sick, and die? Is there a way out?

### The Search and the Awakening

Siddhartha left his palace, his wife, and his young son. He travelled across the land, meeting other ascetics and scholars, searching for the cause of suffering.

After many days of meditation under a **pipal tree** at **Bodh Gaya** (in modern Bihar), he had a profound realisation. The cause of suffering, he understood, is **avidya** (ignorance) and **attachment** (clinging to what cannot last).

He became known as **The Buddha** — "The Awakened One."

### The Buddha's Teachings

The Buddha taught some powerful ideas:

- **Ahimsa** — non-violence. Originally means "non-hurting" — in body, speech, and even thought.
- **The Middle Path** — neither extreme luxury nor extreme self-denial. Walk the balanced middle.
- **Inner discipline** — self-conquest is greater than victory over others.
- **Compassion** — for all living beings.

A famous saying of the Buddha:

> "Not by water is one made pure, though many people may bathe in sacred rivers. But one is pure in whom truth and dharma reside. Conquering oneself is greater than conquering a thousand men on the battlefield a thousand times."

### The Sangha

The Buddha founded the **Sangha** — a community of **bhikkhus** (monks) and **bhikkhunis** (nuns) who dedicated their lives to practising and spreading his teachings.

Buddhism spread far beyond India — to Sri Lanka, Tibet, China, Korea, Japan, Southeast Asia. Even today, hundreds of millions follow it across the world. Within India, states like Sikkim, Ladakh, parts of Arunachal Pradesh, and Maharashtra have substantial Buddhist communities.

## Jainism — The Path of the Conquerors

At about the same time as the Buddha, another great teacher was born in northern India — **Vardhamana**, born near **Vaishali** in modern Bihar in the **6th century BCE**.

He too came from a royal family. At age 30, he left home to seek spiritual truth. After 12 years of intense asceticism, he attained supreme wisdom and became known as **Mahavira** — "the Great Hero."

### The Word "Jain"

The word **Jain** comes from **jina**, meaning "conqueror" — not a conqueror of land or enemies, but a conqueror of **ignorance** and **attachments**.

### Three Core Ideas of Jainism

1. **Ahimsa** — non-violence in the deepest sense. Mahavira taught:
   > "All breathing, existing, living, sentient creatures should not be slain, nor treated with violence, nor abused, nor tormented, nor driven away."

2. **Anekantavada** — "not just one" perspective. The truth has many aspects and cannot be fully grasped from one viewpoint. A good listener tries to understand all sides.

3. **Aparigraha** — non-possession. Don't cling to material things. Use only what is truly necessary.

Jainism shares much with Buddhism and Vedanta — but each tradition has its own character.

## A Quiet Diversity

There were other schools of thought too. One was the **Charvaka** (or **Lokayata**) school, which believed only this material world exists and there is no life after death. It was unusual but openly discussed. The fact that such different views could coexist tells us that **ancient India was a place of intellectual freedom** — people could choose what suited them.

Even with their differences, the Vedic, Buddhist, and Jain schools shared important concepts:
- **Dharma** — right conduct, duty
- **Karma** — actions and their consequences
- **Rebirth** — the soul takes new bodies
- **Search for liberation** from ignorance and suffering

These shared ideas are the **trunk** of our cultural tree.

## The Folk and Tribal Roots

Not all of India's wisdom was written down. **Oral traditions** — passed from generation to generation by everyday people — carry deep knowledge too. Two important streams are **folk traditions** (common villagers) and **tribal traditions** (communities living in forests, hills, and remote areas).

### India's Tribal Heritage

According to the 2011 Census, India has about **705 tribal communities** with around **10.4 crore (104 million) people** — more than the combined population of Australia and the United Kingdom!

Tribal communities have their own gods, songs, stories, dances, and ways of life. The **Toda** tribe of the Nilgiris worships over 30 mountain peaks. The **Munda** and **Santhal** tribes of eastern India worship **Singbonga**, a creator deity. The **tribes of Arunachal Pradesh** worship **Donyipolo** — a combined form of the Sun and Moon.

### Constant Exchange Between Folk, Tribal, and Mainstream

Indian culture has never been a one-way street. Folk and tribal traditions have given to and taken from the mainstream throughout history.

- **Jagannath of Puri** — one of India's most famous deities — has tribal origins
- Several forms of the **Mother Goddess** worshipped across India come from tribal traditions
- Many tribes have their own versions of the **Ramayana** and **Mahabharata**, from the northeast to Tamil Nadu

The sociologist **Andre Beteille** put it well:

> "Tribal religions have been influenced by Hinduism, but it is equally true that Hinduism... has been influenced by tribal religions."

This **mutual enrichment** is one of the most beautiful features of Indian culture. We are not a single thread but a woven cloth of many fibres.

## Why Not Use the Word "Religion"?

The Vedas, Upanishads, Buddhism, Jainism, and tribal traditions are usually called "religions" in English. But this word is often too narrow for Indian traditions. Indian traditions include **philosophy, spirituality, ethics, art, science, society** — much more than what "religion" usually suggests in other cultures.

That is why we use words like **"schools of thought"** or **"belief systems"** instead. They cover more of what these traditions truly are.

## Before We Close

- **Indian culture has many roots**, going back thousands of years.
- The **Vedas** are India's oldest texts; the **Rig Veda** is the oldest of all. Preserved orally with remarkable care.
- The **Upanishads** introduced ideas like **atman, brahman, karma, rebirth**.
- **Vedanta** teaches that everything is one — brahman.
- **Yoga** developed methods to realise this oneness in one's own life.
- **Buddhism**, founded by **Siddhartha Gautama** (the Buddha), focused on overcoming suffering through wisdom and compassion.
- **Jainism**, taught by **Mahavira**, emphasises **ahimsa, anekantavada, and aparigraha**.
- **Folk and tribal traditions** have enriched Indian culture for thousands of years, exchanging deities and ideas with the mainstream.
- The result is a uniquely **diverse and interconnected** culture — many branches, one trunk.
