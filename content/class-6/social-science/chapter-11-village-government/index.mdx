## Why Local Government Matters

India is huge. We have about **6 lakh villages**, **8,000 towns**, and **over 4,000 cities**. The population is about **1.4 billion**, and roughly **two-thirds** of Indians still live in rural areas.

Imagine if every problem in every village had to be solved by the central government in New Delhi or the state government in the state capital. A broken handpump in a village in Bihar. A torn school roof in a Tamil Nadu hamlet. A village road washed away by rain in Madhya Pradesh. There would be no way to handle them.

So **villages need their own government** for their own problems. India has had such systems for thousands of years. Today, this system is called **Panchayati Raj** — "rule by panchayats."

Let me introduce you to a typical Indian village.

### Lakshmanpur — A Village in the Foothills

In the foothills of the Himalayas lies a small village called **Lakshmanpur**. It has about **200 houses** and **700 people**. Most villagers are farmers. They grow crops, keep cows and goats. Some young people have moved to cities for jobs. A few young men serve in the armed forces.

The village faces real questions every day:
- Will there be enough water for the crops this year?
- The main road got damaged in heavy rains — who will repair it?
- The primary school needs new benches — where will the money come from?
- A buffalo has gone missing — was it stolen, or did it wander off?

These are everyday concerns. The villagers cannot run to the state capital for every issue. So they have their own system to handle local matters. That system is the **Panchayat**.

## The Panchayati Raj System

The word **panchayat** comes from **panch** (five) + **ayat** (committee) — historically, a small council, often of five elders. Such councils have existed in Indian villages for thousands of years.

Today's modern Panchayati Raj system works at **three levels** — village, block, and district. This is called a **three-tier system**.

```
        District Level: Zila Parishad
                    |
        Block Level: Panchayat Samiti
                    |
        Village Level: Gram Panchayat
                    |
             Gram Sabha (villagers)
```

Together, these institutions handle almost every aspect of rural life — agriculture, housing, roads, water, education, healthcare, social welfare, cultural activities.

Let's look at each level.

## The Gram Panchayat — Closest to the People

The **Gram Panchayat** is the **village-level government**. It is closest to the people and handles the most basic local matters.

### The Gram Sabha — All Villagers Together

Underneath the Gram Panchayat is the **Gram Sabha** — the assembly of **all adult villagers** who are registered as voters. Women and men come together to discuss village matters, raise concerns, and take decisions.

The Gram Sabha **elects** the members of the Gram Panchayat. So the Gram Panchayat is **directly accountable** to the villagers — if they do a bad job, the villagers can elect different members next time.

### The Sarpanch (or Pradhan)

Each Gram Panchayat is led by a **Sarpanch** (in many states) or **Pradhan** (in others). This is the head of the village council. The Sarpanch:
- Calls meetings
- Speaks on behalf of the village to higher authorities
- Coordinates the work of the panchayat members
- Helps resolve disputes

In recent decades, more and more **women** have been elected as Sarpanchs. This has changed many villages for the better.

### Who Helps the Gram Panchayat?

- **Panchayat Secretary** — performs administrative work: calling meetings, keeping records.
- **Patwari** — keeps the village's **land records**. In many parts of India, a Patwari has maps that go back generations. These old maps tell us a lot about how the village has changed over time.

## Three Inspiring Sarpanchs

The Panchayati Raj system gives ordinary citizens a chance to lead and transform their villages. Here are three real examples.

### Dnyaneshwar Kamble — A Transgender Sarpanch

**Dnyaneshwar Kamble** is a transgender person from **Tarangfal village** in **Solapur district, Maharashtra**. In 2017, Kamble was elected Sarpanch — defeating six other candidates.

Kamble's motto: **lok seva, gram seva** — *"Service to the village is service to the public."*

This shows that the panchayat system gives a voice to all kinds of people — not just the rich, not just men, not just upper castes. Anyone whom the villagers trust can lead.

### Vandana Bahadur Maida — A Tribal Woman Sarpanch

**Vandana Bahadur Maida** is from the **Bhil tribal community** in the village of **Khankhandvi, Madhya Pradesh**. Going against traditional norms, she became the **first female Sarpanch** of her village.

She did not just take office — she **transformed it**. She:
- Encouraged other women in the village to attend Gram Sabha meetings
- Addressed critical issues like **education and sanitation**
- Earned recognition across the region for her work

Her story shows how women — even from communities that have been historically excluded — can lead rural India forward.

### Popatrao Pawar — Reviving Hiware Bazar

**Hiware Bazar** is a village in **Ahmednagar district, Maharashtra**. Once it was a poor, drought-prone village. Crops failed. People migrated away.

Then **Popatrao Baguji Pawar** became Sarpanch. Inspired by the rainwater-harvesting work of social reformer **Anna Hazare**, he led the village in:
- **Rainwater harvesting** — capturing every drop of rain
- **Watershed conservation** — protecting streams and small rivers
- **Massive tree planting** — lakhs of trees, reviving groundwater

Within a few years, Hiware Bazar transformed from a dry, poor village into a **green, prosperous one**. Migrants returned. Crops thrived. Other villages came to learn from it.

In 2020, **Popatrao Pawar was awarded the Padma Shri** — one of India's highest civilian honours — for transforming his village.

## Child-Friendly Panchayats

The voice of children matters too. Many Gram Panchayats have started **Bal Sabhas (children's assemblies)** and **Bal Panchayats (children's panchayats)** where children discuss issues that affect them.

### Children's Issues, Children's Voice

In Maharashtra, some Bal Panchayats have worked to:
- **End child labour** — bring children working in fields or shops back to school
- **Stop child marriage** — convince parents not to marry off young daughters
- **Improve schools** — speak up about leaky roofs, broken benches, missing teachers

The Bal Panchayat members visit homes, talk to parents, and explain why education matters. Children speaking up for children — and adults listening — is a beautiful form of grassroots democracy.

### Sangkhu Radhu Khandu — A Child-Friendly Village

The **Sangkhu Radhu Khandu Gram Panchayat** in **West Sikkim** has been declared a **"child-friendly Gram Panchayat"**. They have:
- Built compound walls for schools to make them safer
- Built proper kitchens in schools so that midday meals are cooked hygienically
- Given children's needs special attention

### The Children's Parliament of Rajasthan

In the 1990s, the social reformer **Bunker Roy** started a **"Barefoot College"** in Rajasthan. As part of this, he created a **Children's Parliament** for underprivileged village children aged 8 to 14.

The Children's Parliament had:
- Real elections — with voter ID cards, campaigning, ballots
- A "Cabinet" of elected children overseeing school management
- Discussions about real issues — access to education, sanitation, social equality

This initiative empowered children to lead and grow into responsible citizens. In 2001, the Children's Parliament received the **World's Children's Honorary Award**.

## The Panchayat Samiti — The Block Level

Above the Gram Panchayat is the **Panchayat Samiti** (sometimes called Mandal Parishad or Block Panchayat). It works at the **block level** — a group of several villages.

The Panchayat Samiti:
- **Coordinates** between several Gram Panchayats
- **Collects development plans** from all the villages in the block
- **Sends them up** to the District level for funding
- Implements **state and central government schemes** at the block level

A famous national scheme handled at this level is the **Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana** — the all-weather road-building scheme that connects villages by good roads even in monsoon.

## The Zila Parishad — The District Level

At the top of the three-tier system is the **Zila Parishad** — the **District Panchayat**. It coordinates panchayat work across the entire district.

Members of the Zila Parishad include:
- Elected representatives from across the district
- Sarpanchs of villages in the district
- Local Members of the State Legislative Assembly (MLAs)

The Zila Parishad:
- Plans and oversees rural development at the district level
- Allocates funds to Panchayat Samitis and Gram Panchayats
- Coordinates major development projects

## Reservation — Empowering the Excluded

To ensure that **everyone** has a voice — not just the rich or upper-caste men — the law has special **reservation** rules in panchayat elections.

- **One-third of seats are reserved for women.** Some states have increased this to **50%**.
- **Seats are also reserved for Scheduled Castes (SCs)** and **Scheduled Tribes (STs)** in proportion to their population.

These reservations have made an enormous difference. Lakhs of women have been elected to panchayats. Many from tribal, Dalit, and other historically excluded communities have become leaders for the first time. India has experimented with grassroots inclusion on a scale unmatched in the world.

## An Ancient Indian Tradition

The idea of local self-government is not new in India. It has been there for thousands of years.

The **Arthashastra**, written by **Kautilya** (Chanakya) about **2,300 years ago**, describes how an entire administrative structure should be set up — from a single village up to the regional capital:

> "The king shall establish a *sangrahana* (sub-district headquarters) for every 10 villages; a *kharvatika* (district headquarters) for every 100 villages; a *dronamukha* for every 400 villages; and a *sthaniya* (provincial headquarters) for every 800 villages."

If you read this carefully, you'll see it sounds exactly like our modern three-tier system — village → block → district → state. The Arthashastra was thinking about the same problem we still face today: **how to administer a vast country efficiently** by dividing it into manageable units.

Mahatma Gandhi believed deeply in local self-government. He famously said:

> **"The real India lives in its villages."**

He envisioned India as a country of **self-reliant villages** — what he called **Gram Swaraj** ("village self-rule"). Our modern Panchayati Raj system is one way of realising that vision.

## Before We Close

- India has about **6 lakh villages** with about two-thirds of its 1.4 billion people in rural areas.
- The **Panchayati Raj** system gives villages their own local government.
- It has **three tiers**: **Gram Panchayat** (village), **Panchayat Samiti** (block), **Zila Parishad** (district).
- The **Gram Sabha** (all adult villagers) elects the **Gram Panchayat**.
- The village head is called **Sarpanch** or **Pradhan**.
- Real examples — like Dnyaneshwar Kamble, Vandana Maida, and Popatrao Pawar — show how local leaders can transform villages.
- **Bal Panchayats** give children a voice in their villages.
- **Reservations** ensure women, SCs, and STs are represented in local government.
- India's tradition of local self-government goes back **more than 2,000 years** to Kautilya's Arthashastra.
- Panchayati Raj is the closest example of **grassroots democracy** anywhere in the world.
