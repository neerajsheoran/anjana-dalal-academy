## Is My Mother's Work Less Important Because She's Not Paid?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says work that isn't paid is still valuable. But society seems to give more respect to people who earn salaries. Is my mother's housework really as important as my father's job?

**Mother:** It is one of the most important questions you can ask. Let me answer carefully.

In **economic terms**, paid work is measured in money. Your father earns a salary; we can count it. A homemaker's work isn't paid, so we can't count it the same way. This makes paid work **visible** and unpaid work **invisible** — even though both are happening every day.

But just because something isn't measured in money doesn't mean it isn't valuable.

**Child:** What does my mother actually do that creates value?

**Mother:** Think about it. A homemaker:
- Plans and shops for groceries (a small CEO running the household budget)
- Cooks meals (a private chef)
- Cleans and maintains the home (housekeeping)
- Takes care of children — feeding, bathing, teaching values (childcare professional + teacher)
- Helps children with homework (tutor)
- Cares for elderly parents (nurse)
- Manages household repairs (project manager)
- Handles emergencies (first responder)
- Maintains family relationships (social manager)
- Listens patiently when family members are stressed (counsellor)

If you hired separate professionals for all these jobs, it would cost a huge amount of money every month. Studies have estimated this would amount to **lakhs of rupees per year** of value.

**Child:** So if all this work were paid, homemakers would be among the highest earners?

**Mother:** Yes. Many economists now believe that GDP — the way we measure a country's economy — should include unpaid household work. India would suddenly be measured as a much richer country if all this unpaid work were counted.

**Child:** Then why don't we pay homemakers?

**Mother:** Because traditionally, household work has been seen as a **duty**, not a **job**. Both ideas have value, but only one is paid. Some countries are starting to think differently — paying women for raising children, or counting unpaid household work in national statistics.

**Child:** What can I do about it?

**Mother:** **Respect** the unpaid work in your home. Help with it. Recognise it. Thank the people who do it. Don't assume that "Mom will cook" or "Grandma will care for the kids" without thinking about how much that costs them in time and energy.

When you grow up, share the household work fairly. Don't leave it all to women just because that's how it was in the past. Equality begins at home.

**Child:** I will help more from now.

**Mother:** That is the most important thing.

## How Does a Carpenter "Add Value"? Isn't He Just Working?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says Rajesh the carpenter adds ₹400 of value when he turns ₹600 of wood into a ₹1,000 chair. But isn't that just his fee for working? Why call it "value addition"?

**Mother:** Great question. Let me explain why economists use this special term.

Imagine you bought ₹600 of wood from the market. Could you turn it into a chair by yourself?

**Child:** Probably not. I don't have the tools or skills.

**Mother:** Exactly. Even if I bought the wood for the same ₹600, the wood would still be just wood in my house. It wouldn't be a chair until someone with skill, tools, time, and effort transformed it. **Rajesh transforms wood into a chair.** That transformation has real value — the chair is something a customer wants, while raw wood often is not.

The ₹400 difference is **the value of the transformation itself**. It is the value of his skill (learned over years), his tools (which cost money to buy and maintain), his time (he spent several hours on the chair), and his effort.

**Child:** So "adding value" means actually making something more useful than it was before?

**Mother:** Yes. Every economic activity adds value at some step:
- A farmer plants a seed and adds value by growing wheat
- A miller adds value by separating grain from chaff
- A baker adds value by turning grain into bread
- A trader adds value by bringing bread to the market

If you trace it backwards, the bread you ate this morning had **value added by many people** — farmer, miller, baker, trader. Each one earned some money for their share of the value they added.

**Child:** So when I pay for something, I'm paying for the value added by many people I've never met?

**Mother:** Exactly. This is the magic of an economy. You give some money, and you receive the work of many hands. Those people, in turn, take that money and use it to buy what they need — which was made by yet other hands. Everyone serves each other through this exchange.

**Child:** That's beautiful.

**Mother:** It is. The market may seem cold and impersonal, but at its best, it is a giant web of cooperation between strangers — each adding value to the others' lives.

## What's the Difference Between Doing Work for Money and Doing Work for Love?

**Child:** Maa, if doing things for money is "economic" and doing things for love is "non-economic," is one better than the other? Should I prefer one over the other?

**Mother:** Neither is better. Both are needed. Both are valuable. But they serve different purposes.

**Work for money** allows you to:
- Buy the things you need (food, clothes, shelter)
- Support your family
- Save for the future
- Plan for big goals (education, a home, retirement)
- Contribute to the economy

**Work for love** allows you to:
- Care for the people you love
- Build deep relationships
- Pass on values and traditions
- Serve your community
- Find personal meaning beyond material things

Both are essential parts of a full life. **The wisest people do both.**

**Child:** But what if I have to choose? Like, what if a job pays a lot but I don't enjoy it?

**Mother:** That is a real choice many adults face. There is no single right answer.

Some choose **high-paying jobs they don't love** because they need the money for their family. There is dignity in this — providing for those you love is itself a form of love.

Others choose **less-paid work that they find meaningful** — like teaching, social work, art, public service. There is dignity in this too — making the world a better place is valuable.

Many find a **middle path** — they take a job that pays reasonably and find meaning in volunteer work or hobbies on the side.

There is no universal "right" answer. The question is — **does your work give you enough money, AND enough meaning, AND enough time for the people and activities you love?**

**Child:** What about Geeta Aunty? She works as an Air Force pilot. Is that for money or love?

**Mother:** Probably both. She earns a salary — so it is economic. But she also serves the country — so there is meaning beyond money. Many people in the armed forces, medicine, teaching, government service combine income with service. They get a salary but also feel they are doing something important. That is a wonderful combination.

## Why Does Indian Culture Value Seva So Much?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter mentions seva — selfless service. Why is this such a big deal in Indian culture?

**Mother:** Because Indian thought has always recognised that **happiness from giving is different from happiness from receiving** — and often deeper.

**Child:** What do you mean?

**Mother:** When you buy something nice for yourself, you feel happy for a while. Then the happiness fades. You want more.

But when you do something for someone else — really help them, see their gratitude, see the difference your help made — that feeling lasts longer. It changes you. You feel **connected** to others, to humanity. You feel **purposeful**.

Indian traditions discovered this thousands of years ago. They built it into many practices:

- **Langars at gurudwaras** — Sikhs serving free meals to everyone who comes, regardless of caste, religion, status. The biggest is at the **Golden Temple in Amritsar**, where about **50,000 people are fed every day**.

- **Prasad at temples** — food blessed and distributed to all visitors

- **Dana** — the practice of giving (food, money, clothes, knowledge) to those who have less. Considered one of the highest spiritual practices.

- **Hospitality (atithi devo bhava)** — guests treated as God

- **Volunteer work at religious institutions** — devotees doing seva by cleaning temples, organising festivals, helping pilgrims

**Child:** Why do people do this?

**Mother:** Because they feel **enriched, not depleted, by giving**. It is a paradox — the more you give, the more you feel you have. The more you serve, the more meaningful your life feels.

**Child:** Is this only Indian?

**Mother:** No, every culture has forms of service and giving. But Indian culture has particularly developed it. Almost every Indian religious tradition emphasises seva. The idea is woven into our daily life — even in homes where guests are honoured, where neighbours are helped, where elders are served.

**Child:** Can I do seva now?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Help a younger sibling with homework. Help an old neighbour carry groceries. Donate books or clothes you've outgrown. Visit a children's home or an old-age home and spend time talking. Plant a tree. Pick up litter from your street. Teach someone something you know.

Each act is small. But each one is a tiny piece of seva. Practice it as a child, and it becomes a habit for life.

## Why Did Swami Vivekananda Call Work a Form of Worship?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter quotes Swami Vivekananda saying "do any work as worship, as the highest worship." How can ordinary work be worship?

**Mother:** Because Vivekananda was teaching a profound idea — that **everything we do can be done with deep attention and care**, and when it is, it becomes sacred.

Imagine cooking dinner. You can do it carelessly — half your mind on your phone, food getting burnt, plates clattering. Or you can do it with **full presence** — every chop of the knife mindful, every taste considered, the meal prepared with love. Same activity. Two different experiences.

Vivekananda was saying — **don't divide your life into "important" sacred work and "unimportant" daily work.** Every action, if done with care, can be lifted to the highest level.

**Child:** Even small things?

**Mother:** Especially small things. A teacher giving full attention to one student's question. A doctor listening carefully to a patient. A homemaker cooking a meal with love. A janitor cleaning a floor thoroughly. A driver carrying passengers safely. **Each of these, done with attention, is a kind of worship.**

**Child:** That changes how I look at my homework.

**Mother:** Yes. Even homework can be approached with full attention rather than just "getting it done." When you do it carefully, you actually learn — and you also practice the habit of giving your best to whatever you do.

This becomes one of the most powerful skills in life. The person who gives their best to every task, big or small, builds a deeply satisfying life. The person who only puts in effort when they think it's "important" misses many opportunities to grow.

**Child:** So work is not just about earning money. It is about how I do it.

**Mother:** Exactly. **Work, done with attention and care, is one of the most beautiful things a human can do.** Whether it pays or not. Whether it is famous or not. Whether anyone is watching or not.

That is Vivekananda's teaching — and it is one of India's gifts to the world.
