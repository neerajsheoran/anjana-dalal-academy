## Why Do We Need Rules?

Imagine a busy market. Hundreds of people are buying and selling. Children are playing. Vehicles are passing through. Now imagine the same scene with **no rules** — no traffic signals, no agreed prices, no rules about cleanliness, no protection against thieves.

It would be chaos.

That is why every group of humans — from families to schools to nations — has **rules**. Some rules are spoken; some are silent. But all rules exist for the same reason: to **help people live together peacefully**.

At home, there are rules — like helping with chores, or returning home before dark.
At school, there are rules — for students, for teachers, for exams.
On the road, there are traffic rules.
At workplaces, there are rules for employees and employers.

What would happen if **nobody followed any rules**? Society could not function. People would harm each other. Disputes would never get settled. The strong would oppress the weak.

So we need rules. And to make rules, follow them, and check that they are followed, we need **governance** and a **government**.

## Some Key Words

Let us define a few terms:

- **Governance** — the process of making decisions, organising society's life, and ensuring rules are followed
- **Government** — the group of people or system that does this
- **Laws** — the most important and binding rules, enforced by the government

Rules and laws are not set in stone. They can be changed — through proper discussion. Just like you might ask your parents to change a rule at home if you have a good reason, citizens can ask the government to change a law if they have good reasons.

## The Three Organs of Government

To stop power from going to one person's head, modern governments are split into **three separate parts** — each with its own job. These three are called the **three organs** of government.

### 1. The Legislature

The **legislature** is the group that **makes laws**. It is made up of representatives elected by the people.

- At the national level in India: **Parliament**, with two houses — the **Lok Sabha** and the **Rajya Sabha**
- At the state level: the **Vidhan Sabha** (and a Vidhan Parishad in some states)

When India needs a new law — say, a law about cybercrime — it is the legislature that discusses it, debates it, and passes it.

### 2. The Executive

The **executive** is the group that **carries out the laws**.

- At the national level: led by the **President** (nominal head — title-only) and the **Prime Minister** (real head)
- At the state level: led by the **Governor** (nominal head) and the **Chief Minister** (real head)

The executive includes ministers (Finance Minister, Defence Minister, etc.) and the various agencies that enforce law and order — like the police, customs, and other government departments.

### 3. The Judiciary

The **judiciary** is the **court system** that decides whether someone has broken the law and what should be done if they have.

- At the national level: the **Supreme Court of India** (the highest court)
- At the state level: the **High Court** in each state
- Below them: district courts and local courts

The judiciary also checks whether laws made by the legislature are **fair** and whether actions of the executive follow the Constitution.

### Separation of Powers

In a well-designed government, these **three organs are kept separate** but must **work together**. This is called **separation of powers**.

Why separate? Because if one group controls everything — making laws, enforcing them, and deciding court cases — that group can become a dictator. Power tends to corrupt.

By keeping the three organs separate:
- The legislature watches the executive (asking questions about what the government is doing)
- The judiciary checks both — striking down laws or actions that violate the Constitution
- The executive cannot rule without the legislature's approval

This is called the system of **checks and balances**. Each organ can "check" the others, keeping them within their proper roles.

### An Example: Catching Cybercriminals

Suppose a new kind of criminal — a hacker — is stealing money from people's bank accounts using the internet.

- The **legislature** passes a new law that defines cybercrime and sets punishments.
- The **executive** (the police, including a special cyber cell) investigates the case, finds the criminal, and arrests them.
- The **judiciary** (the courts) hears the case, examines evidence, decides whether the person is guilty, and gives the punishment.

All three organs play different roles. Together, justice is done.

## The Three Levels of Government

Indian government is also divided **vertically** — into three levels. Each level handles different things.

### 1. Central (Union) Government

This is the **national level**, run from New Delhi. It handles things that affect the whole country:
- Defence — armed forces protect the country
- Foreign affairs — relations with other countries
- Atomic energy
- Currency — the rupee, banks
- Inter-state trade
- National policies in many areas

### 2. State Government

Each Indian state has its own government. It handles things specific to that state:
- Police, law and order
- Public health
- Education (with the centre)
- Agriculture and irrigation
- Local government

### 3. Local Government

At the village, town, or city level, **local government** handles day-to-day matters in your area:
- Water supply
- Garbage collection
- Local roads
- Primary schools
- Public parks

We will study local government in detail in the next two chapters.

### A Flood Example

Suppose heavy rain causes flooding:
- A **small flood** in one village — the **local government** handles it
- A flood affecting many villages and towns — the **state government** steps in with rescue teams
- A massive flood across multiple districts or states — the **central government** sends the army, relief supplies, and money

Different levels for different scales of problems.

## What is Democracy?

The word **democracy** comes from two Greek words:
- **demos** = "people"
- **kratos** = "rule" or "power"

So democracy literally means **"rule of the people."**

But can all people actually rule? Imagine 140 crore Indians all trying to make every decision together. Impossible.

So in practice, **the people elect representatives**, and the representatives govern on their behalf. This is called **representative democracy**.

- People vote in elections
- They choose representatives — **Members of Parliament (MPs)** at the national level, **Members of Legislative Assembly (MLAs)** at the state level
- These representatives discuss laws, debate problems, and make decisions on behalf of the people who elected them

### Direct Democracy

Sometimes, the people themselves decide an issue directly, without representatives. For example, in your class, when you vote with a show of hands on where to go for the picnic — that is **direct democracy**.

In small communities (like village gram sabhas), direct democracy is possible. At the national level, only representative democracy works.

### India — The World's Largest Democracy

India has about **970 million voters** (as of 2024). Every Indian citizen aged **18 or older** has the right to vote. This makes India the **largest democracy in the world** — more voters than the entire population of Europe!

### Grassroots Democracy

The phrase **"grassroots democracy"** means democracy that works at the **lowest level** — among ordinary citizens, in villages and neighbourhoods. It is democracy that involves regular people, not just leaders at the top.

This is why local governments (gram panchayats in villages, municipal bodies in cities) are so important. They are democracy at the grassroots.

## Inspiring Leaders

India has been blessed with many leaders who showed how to serve people. **Dr. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam** (1931–2015) is one beautiful example.

Born in a humble family in Rameswaram, Tamil Nadu, Dr. Kalam became:
- A great scientist — nicknamed the **"Missile Man of India"** for his role in India's space and defence programmes
- The **11th President of India** (2002 to 2007)

Despite his high office, he remained connected to the people, especially **young Indians**. He wrote books for children. He visited schools. He encouraged students to **"dream big"**.

Some of his most-quoted thoughts:

> "Dream is not that which you see while sleeping; it is something that does not let you sleep."

> "If you fail, never give up because F.A.I.L. means 'First Attempt In Learning.'"

> "To succeed in your mission, you must have a single-minded devotion to your goal."

Though the role of President is nominal, Dr. Kalam showed that **how you use your role matters more than the role itself**.

## A Few Special Mottos

Many Indian institutions have mottos drawn from ancient wisdom:

- **Government of India motto**: **Satyameva Jayate** — "Truth alone triumphs"
- **Supreme Court of India motto**: **Yato Dharmastato Jayah** — "Where there is dharma, there is victory"

These mottos remind us that government is not just about power and rules — it is also about **truth and dharma**.

## Before We Close

- Rules and laws are needed to **maintain order** in any society.
- **Governance** is the process of making rules and ensuring they are followed.
- **Government** is the system or group that does this.
- A modern government has **three organs**:
  - **Legislature** — makes laws (Parliament, Vidhan Sabha)
  - **Executive** — implements laws (PM, ministers, police)
  - **Judiciary** — interprets laws and delivers justice (Supreme Court, High Courts)
- These three are kept separate to provide **checks and balances**.
- The Indian government works at **three levels**: Central, State, Local.
- **Democracy** means "rule of the people" — in practice, through elected representatives.
- India is the **largest democracy in the world**, with about 970 million voters.
- **Grassroots democracy** is democracy that empowers ordinary citizens.
