## Why Study the Past?

A famous historian once said history is "an unending conversation between today and yesterday." That is a beautiful way to think about it. The past explains why our world is the way it is — why we speak the languages we speak, eat the food we eat, follow the customs we follow.

**History** is the study of the **human past**. It is not just kings and battles. It is also farmers, traders, mothers, children, artists — everyone who lived before us and shaped the world we inherited.

But how do we actually **know** what happened thousands of years ago, when no one alive today was there to see it? That is the central question of this chapter.

## Who Studies the Past?

Different kinds of experts uncover different kinds of clues:

- **Geologists** study the **Earth itself** — soil, stones, hills, rivers. They can tell us how a region looked millions of years ago.
- **Palaeontologists** study **fossils** — the preserved remains of ancient plants and animals — to learn about life from before humans existed.
- **Anthropologists** study **human societies and cultures**, both old and modern, to understand how groups of people live and change.
- **Archaeologists** literally **dig up the past** — old pots, broken tools, jewellery, bricks, bones, grains. Each object is a piece of a giant jigsaw puzzle.
- **Historians** put all the clues together and write the story of what probably happened.
- **Epigraphists** specialise in reading **old inscriptions** carved on stone, copper plates, or coins.

A historian is like a **detective**. They follow clues, weigh evidence, and try to reach the most likely conclusion. Sometimes the clues match. Sometimes they contradict. The historian has to judge which sources are more trustworthy.

## How Do We Measure Time in History?

### The Gregorian Calendar (BCE and CE)

The calendar used worldwide today is the **Gregorian calendar**. It has 12 months totalling 365 days, with a leap year every 4 years (with a small adjustment for century years).

In this calendar, years are counted starting from a fixed point — the **conventional year of Jesus Christ's birth**.

- Years **after** this point: marked **CE** (Common Era). Earlier called AD. So 1947 = 1947 CE.
- Years **before** this point: marked **BCE** (Before Common Era). Earlier called BC. So Gautama Buddha was born around 560 BCE.

There is **no Year Zero**. The year 1 BCE is immediately followed by 1 CE. So to calculate the gap between a BCE date and a CE date, you **add and subtract 1**. For example, from 560 BCE to 2024 CE = 560 + 2024 − 1 = 2,583 years.

### Decade, Century, Millennium

- **Decade** = 10 years
- **Century** = 100 years
- **Millennium** = 1,000 years

We are currently in the **21st century CE** (2001 to 2100) and the **3rd millennium CE** (2001 to 3000).

For BCE, centuries and millenniums are counted backwards. So the **3rd century BCE** = 300 BCE to 201 BCE.

### Other Calendars Still in Use

Many other calendars are followed in India alongside the Gregorian:
- **Vikram Samvat** and **Shaka Samvat** — old Indian calendars
- **Hijri calendar** — the Islamic calendar
- **Hebrew calendar** — the Jewish calendar
- **Chinese calendar**

Most are based on the movements of the **sun**, **moon**, or both. The Indian **panchanga** is a traditional almanac that lists daily astronomical data — sunrise, sunset, eclipses, festivals — and is still widely used to decide auspicious times for weddings, festivals, and other ceremonies.

## A Timeline of Human History

A **timeline** is a line of dates with important events marked along it. It helps us see the **order** in which things happened. Some major points on the timeline of human history:

- **300,000 BCE** — First modern humans (Homo sapiens) appear
- **40,000 BCE** — First known rock art and ornaments
- **14,000 BCE** — End of the last Ice Age starts
- **10,000 BCE** — First settlements, beginning of agriculture
- **6,000 BCE** — Beginning of pottery in the Indian Subcontinent
- **4,000 BCE** — Beginning of copper metallurgy
- **2,500 BCE** — Indus-Sarasvati civilisation
- **560 BCE** — Birth of the Buddha
- **268 BCE** — Ashoka becomes emperor of the Mauryan kingdom
- **0 (approx)** — Birth of Jesus, start of CE
- **2025 CE** — Today

Looking at this list, even without remembering exact dates, you can see that humans have been around far longer than most cities, kingdoms, or empires. Civilisations are very recent in our story.

## Sources of History

A **source** is anywhere we get information about the past. There are many kinds:

### 1. Written Sources

- **Manuscripts** — old hand-written documents on palm leaves, paper, or cloth
- **Inscriptions** — text carved into stone, metal, or pottery (royal edicts of Ashoka are a famous example)
- **Copper plates** — old land grants written on metal sheets
- **Coins** — show kings, dates, symbols, languages used

### 2. Literary Sources

- **Religious texts** — Vedas, Upanishads, the Tripitaka, the Bible, the Quran
- **Epics** — Ramayana, Mahabharata
- **Plays and poetry** — works of poets like Kalidasa
- **Scientific and mathematical texts** — works of Aryabhata, Bhaskara
- **Historical chronicles** — like the *Rajatarangini* of Kashmir

### 3. Oral Sources

- **Folk songs and stories** passed down by word of mouth for generations
- **Family genealogies** — records of who descends from whom
- **Local legends** about saints, kings, heroes

Some oral traditions are thousands of years old. Many were written down only much later.

### 4. Archaeological Sources

- **Excavated objects** — tools, weapons, pottery, toys, beads, figurines
- **Habitations** — old houses, streets, drainage systems
- **Burial sites** — what was buried with the dead tells us a lot about beliefs
- **Bones and grains** — what people and animals ate, and what they farmed

### 5. Structures

- **Monuments and temples** — like the Brihadeeswarar Temple in Tamil Nadu
- **Forts and palaces** — like the Red Fort or Mehrangarh
- **Mounds** — old hills that turn out to be the buried remains of ancient cities

### 6. Artistic Sources

- **Sculptures**
- **Paintings on walls and caves** — like Ajanta and Bhimbetka
- **Panels carved on temples** showing daily life, mythology, kings

### 7. Foreign Accounts

- **Travelogues** of foreign visitors who wrote about India — like the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang in the 7th century CE, or Ibn Battuta in the 14th century CE
- **Trade records** of foreign merchants

### 8. Modern Scientific Sources

In the last 50 years, science has added new tools:
- **Carbon dating** to determine the age of organic remains
- **Genetic studies** of ancient bones to track how populations moved
- **Climate science** to know what the weather was like in past eras
- **Chemical analysis** of pottery to know what people ate or where the clay came from

## How Did Early Humans Live?

For the first 290,000 years of our species, humans lived as **hunter-gatherers**. They:

- Lived in small groups (called **bands**)
- Hunted animals and gathered plants, fruits, roots, and nuts
- Used tools made of stone, bone, and wood
- Lived in **rock shelters**, **caves**, or temporary camps
- Used **fire** for warmth, cooking, and protection
- Buried their dead — suggesting they had ideas about life after death
- Painted scenes on cave walls — like the famous **Bhimbetka rock paintings** in Madhya Pradesh, India

These early humans communicated using languages that are now lost. They made simple ornaments from shells, stones, and animal teeth. They sometimes traded with other groups.

### From Wandering to Farming

The last **Ice Age** ended about **12,000 years ago**. The climate became warmer and steadier. This changed everything.

People began to:
- **Settle down** in one place
- **Plant crops** instead of just gathering
- **Domesticate animals** — cattle, goats, sheep
- Build **permanent houses** near rivers (because rivers gave water and the soil nearby was fertile)
- Make **pottery** to store food and water
- Use **metal** — first copper, then much later iron — to make stronger tools

Settlements grew into **villages**. Villages traded goods with each other and grew into **towns**. Towns grew into **cities**. Some of those first cities — in Mesopotamia and along the Indus and Sarasvati rivers in India — were the beginning of what we now call **civilisation**.

This is the story we will pick up in the next chapter.

## Before We Close

- **History** is the study of the human past.
- We measure time in **years, decades, centuries, millenniums** — counting backwards as BCE and forwards as CE.
- Many people work to uncover the past: **archaeologists, historians, anthropologists, palaeontologists, epigraphists, geologists**.
- **Sources of history** include written documents, inscriptions, literature, oral traditions, excavated objects, monuments, artwork, foreign accounts, and modern science.
- **Early humans** lived as hunter-gatherers for hundreds of thousands of years. Only about 12,000 years ago did farming begin — leading to villages, towns, and eventually civilisations.
