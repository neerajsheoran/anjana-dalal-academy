## What is a Map?

A map is a small picture of a place drawn on paper. It shows where things are and how to reach them. When you visit a new city, a map is like a friendly guide that fits in your hand.

Think about it this way. If you were going from your house to the railway station for the first time, would you walk in any random direction? Of course not. Either you would ask someone, or you would look at a map on your phone. A map helps because it shows the area as if you are looking down on it from the sky.

A book full of maps is called an **atlas**.

## Different Kinds of Maps

Not all maps look the same. Different maps are made for different purposes.

### Physical Maps
These show **natural features** — mountains, rivers, oceans, deserts, forests. If you want to know where the Himalayas are or which way the Ganga flows, a physical map is what you need.

### Political Maps
These show **countries, states, cities and borders**. India's political map shows all 28 states, 8 Union Territories and their capitals. The borders are drawn in clear lines.

### Thematic Maps
These show **one specific kind of information**. A rainfall map shows where it rains the most. A population map shows where more people live. A wildlife map shows where tigers and elephants are found.

## Three Important Parts of Every Map

Every good map has three things working together: **distance**, **direction**, and **symbols**.

### Distance and Scale

A huge country like India cannot fit on a small piece of paper at its real size. So we shrink it. The **scale** of a map tells you how much it has been shrunk.

For example, if a map says **1 cm = 100 km**, it means every 1 centimetre on the paper stands for 100 kilometres on the ground. So if Delhi and Agra are 2 cm apart on the map, the real distance is about 200 km.

Without a scale, a map is just a drawing. With a scale, you can actually measure distances.

### Direction

Most maps have a small arrow marked with the letter **N** in a corner. It points to the **north**. Once you know which way is north, you automatically know the other three main directions:

- **North** at the top
- **East** to the right
- **South** at the bottom
- **West** to the left

These four are called **cardinal directions**. Between them lie the **intermediate directions** — northeast (NE), southeast (SE), southwest (SW) and northwest (NW). So we get eight directions in all.

### Symbols

Maps cannot show real buildings, trees and rivers as full drawings. There is no space. Instead, mapmakers use small picture symbols. A train symbol means a railway station, a small cross might mean a hospital or a temple, wavy blue lines mean a river.

The Survey of India is a government body that has fixed standard symbols for our country's maps. Once you learn these symbols, you can read any map of India quickly.

## Why a Map of the Whole Earth is Tricky

Our planet is shaped almost like a ball — what we call a **sphere**. (It is slightly flattened at the top and bottom, but we can imagine it as round.)

The problem is, you cannot perfectly flatten a ball onto paper without tearing it. Try this at home: peel an orange in three or four big pieces, then press the pieces flat on a table. They crack at the edges. The same thing happens when we try to draw the round Earth on a flat sheet.

This is why we have **globes** — small models of the Earth that are also round. A globe shows the shape of our planet correctly, even if it is harder to carry around than a flat map.

## Finding Any Place on Earth: Coordinates

How do you tell someone exactly where to find you in a crowded market? You might say "third shop in the fifth row from the entrance." That gives **two numbers** — row and shop — and together they pin you down precisely.

The Earth uses a similar idea. Two numbers — called **latitude** and **longitude** — can locate any place on our planet exactly.

### Latitude: How Far From the Equator

Imagine a giant imaginary belt going around the middle of the Earth, equally far from the top and bottom. This belt is called the **Equator**.

The top point of the Earth is the **North Pole** and the bottom point is the **South Pole**.

Latitude tells you how far north or south of the Equator a place is. Some basics:

- The Equator is at **0° latitude**
- The North Pole is at **90° North** (written 90°N)
- The South Pole is at **90° South** (written 90°S)
- Delhi is at about **29°N** — meaning it is 29 degrees north of the Equator

There is also a link between latitude and weather. Places near the Equator are usually hot, places far from it (near the poles) are usually cold, and places in between have moderate weather. That is why Kanyakumari is warm year-round while Ladakh has snow.

### Longitude: How Far From the Prime Meridian

If latitudes go around the Earth like belts (parallel to the Equator), longitudes run from the North Pole to the South Pole like the lines on a peeled orange.

But we need a starting line — a "first" longitude. In 1884, countries around the world agreed that the line passing through **Greenwich**, a part of London, would be the starting line. It is called the **Prime Meridian** or **Greenwich Meridian** and is marked as **0° longitude**.

Longitudes are measured east or west of this Prime Meridian:

- Delhi is at about **77°E** (77 degrees east of Greenwich)
- Mumbai is at about **73°E**
- New York is at about **74°W** (west of Greenwich)
- Tokyo is at about **140°E**

Together, **latitude + longitude** = one exact spot on Earth. With just these two numbers, anyone can find you.

### India's Own Ancient Prime Meridian

Long before Greenwich was chosen, India had its own prime meridian. It was called **madhya rekha** (middle line) and passed through the city of **Ujjayini** — today's Ujjain in Madhya Pradesh.

Ujjain was a famous centre of Indian astronomy for many centuries. The astronomer **Varahamihira** lived and worked there around 1,500 years ago. Indian scholars knew about latitudes and longitudes and used the Ujjayini meridian for their calculations.

## Time Zones: Why Mumbai and London Show Different Times

The Earth spins on its own axis once every **24 hours**. A full spin is 360 degrees, so this means the Earth turns by **15° every hour** (because 15 × 24 = 360).

What does that mean for us? When it is noon at the Prime Meridian (Greenwich), the Sun is overhead there. But India is east of Greenwich, so the Sun reached India earlier in the day. By the time it is noon in London, it is already evening in India.

If you walk 15° east from Greenwich, the local time goes up by 1 hour. So:
- Greenwich: 12 noon
- 15°E: 1 pm
- 30°E: 2 pm

Going west, the local time goes down by 1 hour:
- 15°W: 11 am
- 30°W: 10 am

### Indian Standard Time (IST)

If every Indian city used its own local time, life would be confusing. Mumbai's local time is different from Kolkata's by almost two hours. So India follows one common time across the whole country, called **Indian Standard Time (IST)**.

IST is **5 hours 30 minutes** ahead of Greenwich. When it is noon in London, it is **5:30 pm** in India.

### The International Date Line

If you keep travelling east from Greenwich, you eventually reach a point exactly opposite — at about **180° longitude**. This line is called the **International Date Line**.

It is special because crossing it changes your date by one whole day. If you fly east across it, you go back a day; if you fly west across it, you jump forward a day. This zigzag line in the middle of the Pacific Ocean keeps countries from being split into two different days.

## Before We Close

- A map is a picture of an area showing where things are — using distance, direction and symbols.
- The Earth is round, so we use a globe or special techniques to draw it on flat paper.
- **Latitudes** (east-west lines) and **longitudes** (north-south lines) together can locate any place on Earth.
- The Equator is at 0° latitude; Greenwich is at 0° longitude.
- Longitude also decides time. India uses one common Indian Standard Time, 5.5 hours ahead of Greenwich.
- The International Date Line is at about 180° longitude.
