## A Country With Many Names

If you look at the very first words of the **Indian Constitution**, you will see this beautiful phrase:

> "India, that is Bharat..."

Two names side by side. Both refer to the same country. But why does India have **two official names**? And were there others before them?

The answer takes us thousands of years back. The land we now call India has had **many names** across history. Some were given by people who lived here. Some were given by visitors and traders from other lands. Each name carries a story.

## The Indian Subcontinent

Before we dive into names, it helps to know what we mean by "India" in this chapter. The land area today divided into India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, and parts of Myanmar and Afghanistan is together called the **Indian Subcontinent**.

It is called a *sub-continent* because it sits on its own piece of Earth's crust — pushed up against Asia by tectonic plates, but geographically a separate region. Its natural boundaries are:

- **Himalayas** to the north — a giant wall of mountains
- **Arabian Sea** to the west
- **Bay of Bengal** to the east
- **Indian Ocean** to the south

These boundaries have stayed roughly the same for thousands of years, even as the names and political borders kept changing.

## Names Given by Indians Themselves

### Sapta Sindhava — "Land of Seven Rivers"

The **Rig Veda** is India's oldest known text, composed thousands of years ago. It calls the northwest region of the Subcontinent **Sapta Sindhava** — the **land of seven rivers**.

The word **Sindhava** comes from **Sindhu**, which originally meant the **Indus River** (or any large river). The "seven rivers" included the Indus and several of its tributaries.

This name covered only a part of the Subcontinent — the area around what is today Punjab, Haryana, parts of Pakistan, and the Indus River system.

### Bharatavarsha and Jambudvipa — Two Names for the Whole Subcontinent

A later, much grander text — the **Mahabharata** — uses two important names for the entire region:

- **Bharatavarsha** — "the country of the Bharatas." The word "Bharata" first appears in the Rig Veda as the name of a Vedic people. Later, it also became a personal name used by several kings. So Bharatavarsha = the land of the Bharata people / kings.

- **Jambudvipa** — "the island of the jamun tree." The jamun (also called jambul or Indian blackberry) is a fruit-bearing tree native to India. So Jambudvipa is a poetic way of saying "the island where the jamun grows."

Both names refer to the whole Subcontinent.

### Emperor Ashoka Uses "Jambudvipa"

Around **250 BCE**, the great emperor **Ashoka** ruled a vast empire that covered most of the Indian Subcontinent. He left behind many **stone inscriptions** across his territory.

In one of these inscriptions, Ashoka uses the name **Jambudvipa** to describe his entire empire — which included not just today's India, but also today's Bangladesh, Pakistan, and parts of Afghanistan.

This gives us solid evidence that **2,300 years ago**, Indians were already thinking of the Subcontinent as one named, unified region.

### Bharata — The Name That Stayed

Over time, **Bharata** became the most widely used name in Indian languages. An ancient text called the **Vishnu Purana** describes it in a famous verse:

> "The country that lies north of the ocean and south of the snowy mountains is called Bharata."

Can you identify these natural boundaries? The "snowy mountains" are the **Himalayas**. The "ocean" is the **Indian Ocean** to the south. So Bharata is the land between them — exactly the geography we still call India today.

Even in **ancient Tamil poetry** from southern India, written about 2,000 years ago, a king is praised by saying his fame extends:

> "From Cape Kumari in the south, from the great mountain in the north, from the oceans on the east and on the west..."

"Cape Kumari" is **Kanyakumari** at India's southern tip. "The great mountain" is the Himalayas. Ancient Indians clearly knew their geography very well — and they thought of the entire Subcontinent as one country, no matter which region they came from.

In modern Indian languages:
- In Hindi and many northern languages: **Bharat**
- In Tamil and some southern languages: **Bharatam** or **Bharatadesam**
- Many Indian languages: **Bharata Mata** (Mother Bharat / Mother India)

## Names Given by Foreigners

### Persian: Hind, Hindu

About **2,500 years ago** (6th century BCE), a **Persian emperor** sent his army into the northwest of India and gained control of the Indus region — what was called **Sindhu** in Sanskrit.

In Persian (the language of ancient Iran), the sound "S" at the start of the word became an "H" sound. So **Sindhu** became **Hindu** (or **Hind**, **Hindhu**).

This was originally just a **geographical** word — meaning "the land around the Indus River." It did **not** refer to the religion. The use of "Hindu" as a religious name came much, much later.

### Greek: Indoi, Indike

The ancient Greeks, who had close contact with the Persians, learned the name from them. But the Greek language had no "H" sound at the start of a word, so they dropped the H.

- Sanskrit **Sindhu** → Persian **Hindu** → Greek **Indoi / Indike**

This is how the word **India** itself was born. The English name "India" comes through Latin, which got it from Greek. So the name "India" in English is actually a very long journey: Sanskrit → Persian → Greek → Latin → English.

### Chinese: Yintu, Tianzhu

The ancient Chinese also called India by names derived from "Sindhu":

- **Yintu** or **Yindu** (similar journey: Sindhu → Hindhu → Indu → Yindu)
- **Tianzhu** — this had a beautiful double meaning. It could be heard as a version of "Sindhu," but it also meant "**Heavenly Master**" in Chinese. The ancient Chinese held India in great respect because it was the **land of the Buddha**, and Buddhism had spread from India to China.

The famous Chinese pilgrim **Xuanzang** travelled from China to India in the **7th century CE**. He spent **17 years** here, visited many parts of the country, met scholars, studied at the great Buddhist university of Nalanda, and returned to China carrying hundreds of Buddhist manuscripts. He translated many of them from Sanskrit into Chinese. His travel records are one of the most important foreign sources for ancient Indian history.

### Hindustan

You have probably heard the word **Hindustan**. It first appeared in a **Persian inscription about 1,800 years ago**, meaning "the land of the Hindus" — that is, the land beyond the Indus River.

In later centuries, especially after various invasions from Central Asia, "Hindustan" became the most commonly used name for India in the Persian and Arabic-speaking worlds. Even today, the word is heard in our national songs ("Sare Jahan Se Achha Hindostan Hamara") and in everyday speech.

## Many Names, One Land

Let us put all the names together:

| Source | Name |
|---|---|
| Rig Veda (oldest Indian text) | Sapta Sindhava (NW region only) |
| Mahabharata, Vishnu Purana | Bharatavarsha / Jambudvipa |
| Emperor Ashoka's inscriptions | Jambudvipa |
| Most modern Indian languages | Bharat / Bharatam |
| Ancient Persian | Hind / Hindu |
| Ancient Greek | Indoi / Indike |
| Latin and English | India |
| Ancient Chinese | Yintu / Tianzhu |
| Medieval Persian and Arabic | Hindustan |
| Modern Constitution | "India, that is Bharat" |

Every one of these names points to the same land — the same rivers, the same mountains, the same coastlines. But each name carries a slightly different story:

- **Bharata** comes from the people who lived here.
- **Jambudvipa** comes from a tree that grows here.
- **Sindhu / Hind / India** comes from the great river in the northwest.
- **Tianzhu** comes from Buddhist pilgrimage and respect.
- **Hindustan** comes from medieval trade and invasion.

A country that has been named so many times must be a country that **many people have loved, traded with, fought for, and respected**.

## Why So Many Names?

A country doesn't get many names by accident. India has had many because:

1. **It is geographically distinctive** — bounded by huge mountains and oceans, it stood out from neighbouring lands.
2. **It was rich in trade** — spices, textiles, gemstones, and ideas drew traders from Europe, the Middle East, Africa, and East Asia for over two thousand years.
3. **It was a centre of learning** — Buddhism, Jainism, Hinduism, astronomy, mathematics, medicine all flourished here. Foreign students and pilgrims came to learn.
4. **It was diverse** — different parts of the Subcontinent had their own kingdoms, languages, and cultures. Each region had its own name in its own language.

Every time another civilisation encountered India, they gave it a name in their own language. Today, the Indian Constitution honours this rich history by accepting two of its most enduring names side by side: **India and Bharat**.

## Before We Close

- The Indian Subcontinent has had **many names** across history.
- **Sapta Sindhava** in the Rig Veda referred to the seven-river region in the northwest.
- **Bharatavarsha** and **Jambudvipa** were used in the Mahabharata, the Vishnu Purana, and by **Emperor Ashoka** for the entire Subcontinent.
- Foreign names — **Hind, Indoi, India, Yintu, Hindustan** — all trace back to the **Sindhu (Indus) River**.
- The Indian Constitution uses both **India** and **Bharat** — honouring our many traditions.
- A country with many names is a country known and loved by many.
