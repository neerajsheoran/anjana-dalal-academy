## Why Should We Care About the Past?

**Child:** Maa, history feels like just memorising old dates. Why does it matter?

**Mother:** Imagine you woke up one morning and you couldn't remember anything from yesterday. Not who your friends are, not what subjects you study, not where your school is. Would you be able to live a normal day?

**Child:** No way. Everything would feel strange.

**Mother:** Now imagine an entire society forgetting its past. Why we have certain laws, why we celebrate certain festivals, why some communities live where they do. Without that memory, we would have no idea how to make sense of today, or how to plan tomorrow. **History is the memory of a society.**

**Child:** So if I want to understand why India is the way it is, I need to know its history?

**Mother:** Exactly. Why is Hindi spoken in some states and Tamil in others? Why is the Taj Mahal in Agra and not in Mumbai? Why did India fight for independence? You can't answer any of these without history.

**Child:** OK, that makes sense. It is not just dates.

**Mother:** Dates are just the calendar of history. The real meat is in why things happened, how people lived, and what changed over time.

## How Can We Know What Happened 2,000 Years Ago?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says we can know about people who lived thousands of years ago. But everyone from that time is dead. There were no cameras, no phones, no newspapers. How can we know?

**Mother:** Good question. We piece it together from many sources. Let me give you an example.

Suppose 2,000 years ago, a king ruled over a small kingdom in central India. How would we know he existed?

- He might have **inscribed his name on stone pillars**, declaring his laws.
- He might have **issued coins** with his face and name.
- A **temple** he built might still stand, with carvings of his deeds.
- A **poet** of that period might have written verses praising him.
- A **foreign traveller** might have passed through and described the kingdom in his diary.
- **Archaeologists** might have dug up his palace and found royal seals, jewellery, weapons.

Each of these is a clue. None of them alone tells the whole story. But put together, they let historians make educated guesses.

**Child:** So historians are like detectives who solve very old cases?

**Mother:** Yes. Just like a detective, they sometimes get conflicting clues. One source says the king was just; another calls him cruel. Then they have to think — who wrote each source, what was their reason, and what does the rest of the evidence say? It is not always easy.

**Child:** What if some clues are missing forever?

**Mother:** Then history has gaps. And those gaps are real. We will never know everything about our ancestors. That is just the truth.

## What's the Big Deal About BCE and CE?

**Child:** Maa, why did they suddenly start writing BCE and CE in textbooks? My uncle's old books say BC and AD.

**Mother:** Because BC and AD are short for Latin phrases — "Before Christ" and "Anno Domini" (in the year of the Lord). These were tied to **Christianity**. But the calendar is now used worldwide — by Hindus, Muslims, Jews, Buddhists, atheists, everyone. Using a religious label felt unfair to people of other faiths.

**Child:** So they changed the names but kept the dates?

**Mother:** Exactly. CE means **Common Era** and BCE means **Before Common Era**. The numbers are identical to AD and BC — only the labels are different. 1947 AD is the same as 1947 CE. 500 BC is the same as 500 BCE.

**Child:** That makes sense. The years are the same; only the names became more neutral.

**Mother:** Yes. Many countries — including India — now use CE/BCE in their textbooks. It is a small change that respects everyone's beliefs.

## What if There Were No Year Zero — Wait, There Isn't One?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says there is no year zero. The year 1 BCE is immediately followed by 1 CE. That feels weird. Why?

**Mother:** When the Gregorian calendar was designed centuries ago, the number "zero" wasn't widely used in Europe. The concept of zero came from **India** but spread slowly. By the time it reached Europe, the calendar was already in place. So the calendar simply jumped from 1 BCE to 1 CE, skipping zero.

**Child:** Ironic — zero was Indian, but the world's calendar doesn't use it.

**Mother:** Right. And it makes calculations a little tricky. If someone was born in 100 BCE and died in 100 CE, you might say they lived 200 years. But actually they lived **199 years** because there is no year zero in between.

**Child:** So we always subtract one when crossing from BCE to CE?

**Mother:** Yes. From 560 BCE (Buddha) to 2024 CE, the calculation is 560 + 2024 − 1 = 2,583 years. That little −1 is the missing year zero.

**Child:** I will remember that. Indian invention, calendar bug.

**Mother:** A nice irony.

## How Did Early Humans Live Without Phones or Houses?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says humans have existed for 300,000 years. But cities are only a few thousand years old. What were people doing for the other 290,000 years?

**Mother:** Living simpler lives. They moved around in small groups, looking for food. Hunters chased animals. Gatherers picked fruits, nuts, leaves and roots. They didn't grow crops because they didn't yet know how. When food in one area ran out, they moved.

**Child:** So they were always on the move?

**Mother:** Mostly. They lived in **caves**, **rock shelters**, or built temporary camps. They learned to control **fire** — that was a huge invention. Fire kept them warm, cooked their food (which made it safer and easier to digest), and scared away wild animals.

**Child:** Did they have any culture, or were they just surviving?

**Mother:** They had quite rich culture. They made simple **ornaments** from shells, animal teeth, and stones. They painted on **cave walls** — some of the most beautiful paintings are at **Bhimbetka** in Madhya Pradesh, with images of humans, animals, hunting scenes painted over thousands of years.

They also **buried their dead** with care — sometimes with food, tools, or ornaments. That suggests they had ideas about what happens after death. They told stories, sang songs, danced, and passed knowledge from generation to generation.

**Child:** So they were people just like us, just with simpler technology?

**Mother:** Exactly. Their brains and feelings were the same as ours. What changed over time was their tools and knowledge, not their humanity.

## Why Did Farming Change Everything?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says farming began about 10,000 years ago. Why was that such a big deal?

**Mother:** Because for the first time, humans could control their food supply. Instead of chasing animals or hoping for fruits, they could plant a seed and watch it grow into food. That changed everything.

**Child:** What changed?

**Mother:** Several things, all interconnected:

- **People stopped moving**. They settled in one place, near rivers and fertile land.
- **Houses became permanent**. Mud huts, stone walls — not just shelters but actual homes.
- **Families grew larger**, because there was more reliable food.
- **Animals were domesticated** — cattle for milk and ploughing, goats and sheep for milk, wool, meat.
- **Surplus food** could be stored. If you grew more than you needed, you could keep it for hard times — or trade it.
- **Trade began**. One village had grain; another had pottery; a third had metal. They started exchanging.
- **Pottery, weaving, metalwork** developed because settled people had time and tools.
- **Society grew complex**. Some people farmed, some made tools, some traded, some led. Roles emerged.

**Child:** So farming led to almost everything we now think of as civilisation?

**Mother:** Yes. Cities, kingdoms, writing, mathematics, religion, science — all of it became possible once humans stopped moving and started growing their food.

**Child:** What if farming had never been invented?

**Mother:** We might still be hunter-gatherers, in small bands, in caves. Life would be simpler — perhaps in some ways healthier — but there would be no cities, no books, no internet. Almost everything you take for granted came from the day someone planted the first seed and waited.

## Why Are Some Indian Calendars Still Used?

**Child:** Maa, Papa uses Vikram Samvat on Diwali wishes. And he checks the panchanga before any big event. Why does India still use these old calendars?

**Mother:** Because they connect us to thousands of years of culture. The **panchanga** is not just a religious thing — it is a very old **science of timekeeping**. It uses careful observations of the sun, moon, planets, and stars to mark days, seasons, eclipses, and good times for important events.

**Child:** So it's astronomy plus tradition?

**Mother:** Yes. The panchanga predicts solar and lunar eclipses years in advance. Ancient Indian astronomers like **Aryabhata** and **Varahamihira** built this system. It still works today because the sun and moon haven't changed.

**Child:** And the Vikram Samvat?

**Mother:** It's an Indian calendar starting from about 57 BCE. So if a card says "Vikram Samvat 2082", it means it's 2082 years since this calendar started. Different Indian regions use different traditional calendars — some go by Vikram Samvat, others by Shaka Samvat, others by their own regional ones. Tamil New Year, Bengali Pohela Boishakh, Malayali Onam — each follows its own calendar.

**Child:** So while the world uses one common calendar, India still keeps its rich variety?

**Mother:** Yes. The Gregorian calendar runs our offices and schools. The Indian calendars run our festivals, weddings, and family rituals. Both live together peacefully — and that is very Indian.
