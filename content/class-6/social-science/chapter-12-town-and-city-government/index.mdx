## Urban India

In the previous chapter, we looked at how Indian villages govern themselves through Panchayati Raj. Now let us look at the other side — how do **cities and towns** govern themselves?

India has about **8,000 towns** and **over 4,000 cities**. About a third of Indians live in urban areas — that is roughly **47 crore people** in cities and towns.

City life is **complex**. A city like Mumbai has nearly 2 crore people living together. Roads, water supply, garbage, schools, hospitals, parks, police, electricity, public transport — managing all of this for so many people every day is an enormous job.

To handle this, cities and towns have their own form of local government, called **Urban Local Bodies (ULBs)**.

## Three Types of Urban Local Bodies

The kind of urban local body in a city depends on its **population**:

| Population | Local Body | Also called |
|---|---|---|
| **Above 10 lakhs** | **Municipal Corporation** | Mahanagar Nigam |
| **1 to 10 lakhs** | **Municipal Council** | Nagar Palika |
| **Smaller towns** | **Nagar Panchayat** | — |

So Mumbai, Delhi, Bengaluru, and Chennai have **Municipal Corporations**. Smaller cities like Vellore or Bilaspur may have **Municipal Councils**. Small towns may have **Nagar Panchayats**.

## A Quick Look at the Whole Indian Government System

Putting together what we learned in Chapters 10 and 11 and this one, here is the full structure:

```
                  Central (Union) Government

            Rural Local              Urban Local
```

The left side is rural local government (panchayats). The right side is urban local government (municipal bodies). At the bottom of each side are the **everyday people** — the source of all democratic authority.

## How Cities Are Organised

Cities are big. To manage them, they are **divided into smaller units called "wards."**

A ward is like a neighbourhood — a small part of the city with its own residents, shops, schools, and parks. Each ward elects its own representative — called a **Councillor** or **Corporator**. The councillors of all the wards together form the **Municipal Corporation** or **Municipal Council**.

So in a large city like Mumbai:
- The city is divided into **many wards** (Mumbai has about 227 ward areas)
- Each ward elects a **corporator**
- All corporators together form the **Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai**
- The corporation has a head — called the **Mayor**
- The Mayor leads the city's local government

## The First Indian Municipal Corporation

India has had urban local bodies for centuries. The **oldest municipal institution** in modern India is the **Madras Corporation** (today called the **Greater Chennai Corporation**), established way back on **29 September 1688** — over **335 years ago**.

In 1792, a Parliamentary Act gave the Madras Corporation the power to **levy municipal taxes** in the city. That is when modern Indian municipal administration properly began.

The **Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai** (originally Bombay Municipal Corporation), established in 1865, is also one of India's oldest.

These corporations have evolved over centuries to handle the growing complexity of city life.

## What Do Urban Local Bodies Do?

A modern Indian municipal body handles many services. Look at this list of services typically provided by a major city corporation like the **Indore Municipal Corporation**:

- **Property tax** — assessing and collecting tax on houses and land
- **Water supply** — providing clean water, charging water bills
- **Solid waste management** — garbage collection, segregation, disposal
- **Business licences** — for shops, restaurants, hoardings, trade
- **Marriage certificates** — for legal registration
- **Fire services** — fire brigade
- **Various licences** — for street vendors, hawkers, etc.
- **Water tanker services** — emergency water delivery
- **Debris clearance** — after demolition
- **Tree cutting and transportation** — managing trees in city areas
- **Citizen grievances** — handling complaints
- **Various services on request** — ambulance, funeral van, mobile toilets, auditorium bookings

And many more. Each of these matters in the daily life of city residents.

## The Cleanest City — Indore's Success Story

For **seven years in a row**, **Indore in Madhya Pradesh** has been named **India's cleanest city** under the **Swachh Survekshan** government scheme. How did Indore do it?

The Municipal Corporation deserves credit, but **so do the citizens of Indore**. They have actively cooperated by:
- **Segregating garbage** at home — wet (food) waste in one bin, dry (paper, plastic) in another
- **Handing it over to garbage trucks** that come at set times each day
- **Not littering** in public places
- **Maintaining their own neighbourhoods**

The lesson: **urban governance works best when citizens cooperate**. A municipal corporation alone cannot keep a city clean. Citizens have to do their part.

## The Ward Committee — Closest to Citizens

Just like the Gram Sabha is the bottom layer of rural governance, the **Ward Committee** is the bottom layer of urban governance.

A ward committee includes the elected councillor of that ward and sometimes other respected members of the community. The committee:

- Conducts **health camps** in the ward
- Organises **awareness campaigns** (against single-use plastics, on health, etc.)
- Monitors local issues — water leaks, blocked drains, damaged roads, garbage piles
- Reports problems to the higher municipal authorities
- Listens to local citizens' concerns

The exact functions of ward committees differ from state to state because each state makes its own rules. But the principle is the same — **bring governance close to the people**.

## Urban vs Rural — A Conversation

Sameer (a village boy) and Anita (a city girl) once compared notes about their lives.

**Anita:** "Cities are busy and crowded. Tall buildings, lots of noise. People are more independent. We often don't even know our neighbours."

**Sameer:** "Here, everyone knows everyone. We work together in the fields, celebrate festivals together, even make decisions as a community."

**Anita:** "There's still some community feeling in the city. Recently, after heavy rains, a house collapsed two streets away. Dozens of people gathered to help — clearing rubble, making sure no one was trapped."

**Sameer:** "Doesn't the local government help in such cases?"

**Anita:** "Yes. We have local bodies and elected representatives. They handle disasters, water, garbage, schools."

**Sameer:** "Sounds like our village Panchayat, only bigger. Our Panchayat members are elected too. And because everyone knows everyone, lots of people can participate. Sometimes even kids can get heard. Last week, some kids noticed an electricity wire hanging dangerously low. We reported it and an electric post was shifted. It actually worked!"

**Anita:** "That is how democracy should work. It seems more complicated in the city, but the idea is the same — **everyone's voice matters.**"

This little conversation captures the heart of local government in both urban and rural India.

## Citizens' Duties

Cities have many problems — traffic, pollution, water shortage, overcrowded buses, garbage. These cannot be solved by the local government alone. Each citizen has duties:

- **Don't litter.** Put garbage in bins, not on streets.
- **Save water.** Don't let taps run unnecessarily.
- **Save electricity.** Turn off lights and fans when not needed.
- **Use public transport** when possible. Reduces traffic and pollution.
- **Report problems.** A water leak you ignore today wastes thousands of litres tomorrow.
- **Pay taxes honestly.** Municipal bodies need money to provide services.
- **Vote in local elections.** Choose councillors who care.
- **Attend ward meetings.** Make your voice heard.
- **Care for public spaces.** Parks, walls, benches — they belong to everyone.
- **Respect neighbours.** A city of neighbours is a kinder city.

## Why Decentralisation Matters

You may have heard the word **decentralisation**. It means **giving power to local levels** rather than keeping it all at the top.

A decentralised system has several benefits:
- **Faster responses** to local problems
- **Better fit** with local needs
- **Greater citizen participation** — democracy lives in everyday decisions
- **More accountability** — citizens can directly see how their councillors perform
- **Innovation** — different cities can try different approaches and learn from each other

This is why India's Constitution (specifically the **74th Amendment in 1992**) created a clear constitutional structure for urban local bodies — to strengthen this decentralised system.

## A Voice from History

When India was writing its Constitution between 1946 and 1949, the question of local government was actively debated. **Rustom K. Sidhwa**, a member of the Constituent Assembly, said in 1949:

> "I desire that... a full-fledged local body should be immediately formed, so that people may know really what is an administration, what are the franchise, what are the powers, what are the rights and what are the privileges in a small sphere, in their own town, in their own villages."

He saw local government as the school where ordinary citizens learn democracy. You learn how to talk to other citizens, how to negotiate, how to choose leaders, how to follow rules, how to demand accountability. By participating in local government, you become a better citizen — and a better human being.

That hope from 1949 is being fulfilled today, in every Indian village and every Indian city, every day, in big and small ways.

- **Cities and towns** have their own local government called **Urban Local Bodies**.

  - **Municipal Corporation** (above 10 lakhs)

  - **Nagar Panchayat** (smaller towns)

- All councillors together form the corporation/council, led by a **Mayor**.

- **Decentralisation** brings democracy to the grassroots, where it really lives.
