## "All Parties Are the Same" — Is That True?

**Student:** Many people say "all parties are the same — they're all corrupt." Is there truth in this?

**Mentor:** This is one of the most common opinions about Indian politics. Let me unpack it.

**The Grain of Truth.**

There IS truth in the criticism:
- Many parties share certain bad practices (dynasties, dependence on money, candidates with criminal cases)
- Once in power, parties often disappoint
- Promises are made and broken across the spectrum
- Corruption exists in many parties
- Major scams have occurred under both BJP and Congress governments
- Most parties prioritise winning over good policy

So saying "all parties have problems" is fair.

**The Bigger Falsehood.**

But "all parties are the SAME" is false. Parties differ on:

(1) **Ideology**. Congress (broadly secular-welfare), BJP (broadly Hindutva-economic liberalism), CPI/CPI(M) (left), AAP (centrist-welfare). These ideological differences shape policy.

(2) **Policies in government**. Different parties enact different policies. UPA (Congress-led) emphasised welfare (NREGA, Right to Education, Food Security). NDA (BJP-led) has emphasised digital infrastructure, infrastructure, religious-nationalist agenda. CPM-led Kerala has emphasised education, health, equality. These are real differences with real impacts.

(3) **Leadership style**. Different parties have different cultures — top-down vs more participatory, ideological vs pragmatic, etc.

(4) **Constituencies**. Different parties draw support from different groups — caste, class, region, religion.

(5) **Performance**. Different parties run governments with different effectiveness. Some states are well-governed; others poorly. Parties matter.

(6) **Corruption levels**. Yes, all parties have corruption — but levels differ. Some are dramatically more corrupt than others.

(7) **Internal democracy**. AAP has more internal democracy than dynastic parties. Different cultures.

(8) **Specific issues**. Parties take different positions on specific issues — secularism, federalism, economic reforms, foreign policy, environment.

So the saying "all parties are the same" is often used by:
- People too lazy to study differences
- People deeply cynical (sometimes justifiably)
- People who don't want to make a choice
- Sometimes by people defending bad parties ("everyone is bad, so we're fine")

But for someone who looks carefully, parties ARE different.

**Why People Say It Anyway.**

(1) **Disappointment**. Voters elect a party hoping for change. The party falls short. The voter feels betrayed and concludes all are the same.

(2) **Personal corruption experience**. If you've personally been asked for a bribe by a corrupt official, you may generalise to all parties.

(3) **Media noise**. Constant scandals make all parties look bad.

(4) **Convenience**. If all parties are the same, you don't need to vote, don't need to engage, don't need to think.

(5) **Cynicism as protection**. If you expect nothing, you can't be disappointed.

**Why "All Parties Are the Same" Is Dangerous.**

(1) **It excuses voter passivity**. If all are the same, why vote? Why engage? Why pressure for reform? This benefits the worst politicians.

(2) **It rewards the corrupt**. If voters don't distinguish, the corrupt and the relatively cleaner are treated equally. Corruption then continues.

(3) **It hurts reformers**. The few politicians who try to be different get no reward — voters don't notice or don't care.

(4) **It weakens democracy**. Democracy requires citizens to make informed choices. "All parties are the same" abandons that responsibility.

(5) **It plays into authoritarian narratives**. Authoritarians often say "all democratic parties are the same; only strong leader can fix things." This justifies abandoning democracy.

**How to Vote Wisely.**

(1) **Study what parties have actually done**, not just what they say. Read their performance over time.

(2) **Look at the candidate**, not just the party. Even within bad parties, some candidates are decent.

(3) **Use the NOTA option** if you genuinely think all are bad — at least register your discontent.

(4) **Vote for cleaner alternatives** when available — even if they have less chance, you signal what you want.

(5) **Engage between elections**. Pressure parties. Demand transparency. Push for reform.

(6) **Don't generalise from individual experience**. The corrupt clerk in your area doesn't mean ALL politicians are corrupt.

(7) **Reward improvement**. If a party reforms, give it a chance.

(8) **Punish wrongdoing**. If a party becomes worse, vote it out.

**The Bigger Lesson.**

Cynicism is easy. Engagement is hard. But cynicism leads to bad democracy; engagement makes democracy better.

Indian parties have many problems. They are also responsible for delivering food, water, education, jobs, security to 1.4 billion people. Most of the time, most of the country functions.

The path forward: not abandoning parties (which would mean abandoning democracy), but making parties better through:
- Informed voting
- Civic engagement
- Demanding transparency
- Supporting reforms
- Calling out wrongdoing
- Celebrating improvements

When you turn 18, please vote. And please vote thoughtfully — not from cynicism, not from family loyalty, not from caste/religion alone. From your understanding of what each party stands for, what they've done, what they promise.

This is the kind of voting that gradually transforms parties — and democracy.

## Why Are Dynastic Parties So Common in India?

**Student:** The chapter mentioned many Indian parties are run by families. Why does this happen so much?

**Mentor:** Excellent question. India has more dynastic parties than most democracies. Several reasons explain this.

**1. Founding Family Phenomenon.**

Many Indian parties were founded by individuals whose family then takes over:
- Congress originally was a broad organisation, but the Nehru family became central
- DMK was founded by Annadurai, then Karunanidhi (whose family controls it now)
- Trinamool Congress was founded by Mamata Banerjee, who is grooming her nephew
- Akali Dal has been a Badal family party for decades
- Many regional parties — TDP (Naidu family), YSRCP (YSR family), Shiv Sena (Thackeray family), SP (Yadav family), RJD (Yadav family)

When the founder is charismatic, family members often inherit the party.

**2. Voters Often Support Dynasties.**

This is uncomfortable but true. Indian voters often support dynasties:
- Familiarity (the family is known)
- Loyalty to the founder
- Belief that the heir inherits the founder's qualities
- Family disputes are seen as continuing the legacy
- Some communities (especially in patriarchal cultures) accept family succession

Voters could reject dynasties at any time. They sometimes do (Rahul Gandhi has had electoral failures). But often, they don't.

**3. Money and Networks.**

Politics requires money, networks, organisational support. Families that have ruled have accumulated all of these. Outsiders find it hard to break in.

**4. Lack of Internal Democracy.**

In most Indian parties, leaders are not elected by members. They are anointed by previous leaders. Family heirs are obvious choices. This creates a circular problem — lack of internal democracy enables dynasty, dynasty prevents internal democracy.

**5. Weak Institutions.**

In countries with strong institutions, leaders can be replaced fairly. In countries with weaker institutions, personal loyalty matters more. India's institutions are evolving but still leave room for personality and family-based leadership.

**6. Caste-Family Politics.**

In some parties, dynasty also represents caste politics. The Yadav family represents Yadav community in UP/Bihar. The Naidu family represents Kammas in Andhra. Maintaining the family means maintaining caste representation. This is hard to break.

**7. Voters' Distrust of Outsiders.**

Indian voters often distrust new political entrants. They prefer "known evils" (existing families) over "unknown candidates" who might be even worse. This bias favours established dynasties.

**8. Media and Personality Focus.**

Indian media often focuses on personalities — Indira Gandhi, Rajiv Gandhi, Sonia Gandhi, Rahul Gandhi; Karunanidhi, Stalin; Lalu Prasad, Tejashwi Yadav. This personalisation strengthens dynasty.

**Why Dynastic Politics Is Problematic.**

(1) **Merit not rewarded**. Children of leaders get positions regardless of ability. Outsiders with talent are blocked.

(2) **Internal democracy stifled**. Party members can't challenge leaders effectively.

(3) **Policy not the focus**. Personality and family drama dominate over substance.

(4) **Concentration of power**. Few families control vast political resources.

(5) **Corruption ecosystems**. Family-run parties often become networks of corruption (relatives placed in lucrative posts).

(6) **Reduces accountability**. Leaders are accountable mainly to family, not to voters.

(7) **Discourages talent**. Talented people may avoid politics, knowing dynasty controls it.

**Counter-Examples — Non-Dynastic Parties.**

Not all Indian parties are dynastic:

(1) **BJP**: Has had multiple leaders from different families. PM Narendra Modi is not from a political family. Atal Bihari Vajpayee, L.K. Advani — also not dynastic. Internal succession through party processes (though tightly controlled).

(2) **AAP**: Founded by Arvind Kejriwal (not from political family). Has had power-sharing within party, though Kejriwal dominates.

(3) **CPM, CPI**: Communist parties don't have dynasties — leadership from party cadre.

(4) **Several BJP CMs**: Yogi Adityanath, Manohar Lal Khattar, Bhajan Lal Sharma, Mohan Yadav — none from political dynasties (though some had connections).

So dynastic politics is COMMON but NOT UNIVERSAL.

**Can Dynastic Politics Decline?**

Yes — through several paths:

(1) **Voters rejecting dynasties**. When voters punish dynastic candidates, parties respond. Some Congress losses have been blamed on Gandhi family leadership.

(2) **Internal party democracy**. If parties hold genuine internal elections, non-family members can rise.

(3) **Strong civil society**. Movements demanding clean politics put pressure on parties.

(4) **Educated electorate**. As voters become more informed, family appeals lose strength.

(5) **Successful non-dynastic parties** show alternatives. BJP's success has put pressure on dynastic rivals.

(6) **Media scrutiny**. Independent journalism exposing dynastic privileges.

(7) **Generational change**. Younger voters may be less attached to old loyalties.

**The Other Side.**

Some defend dynasties:

(1) **Stability**. Families provide continuity. Rapid leadership change creates uncertainty.

(2) **Voters' choice**. If voters want them, who are we to say no?

(3) **Some dynastic leaders are competent**. Indira Gandhi, Sheikh Hasina, Benazir Bhutto — many were skilled politicians.

(4) **No worse than self-perpetuating non-dynastic leaders**. Modi has been PM for 10+ years without family but with his own brand of dominance.

(5) **Western democracies have dynasties too**. Bush family, Kennedy family, Clinton family in USA. Trudeau in Canada.

These arguments have some validity. Dynasties aren't always or only bad.

**The Bigger Pattern.**

Dynastic politics in India is part of a larger pattern:
- Personalistic politics (focus on leaders, not parties)
- Patron-client networks (favours for support)
- Weak internal party democracy
- Caste and community loyalty
- Money-dependence of politics

To reduce dynasties, all these need to change. Not just families but the whole system.

**The Citizen's Choice.**

When you vote, the choice between dynastic and non-dynastic candidates is one of many considerations. Not all dynasties are bad; not all non-dynastic candidates are good. But all else being equal:

(1) **Prefer candidates** who built their own political careers, not inherited them.

(2) **Prefer parties** with internal democracy.

(3) **Reward outsiders** who challenge dynasties.

(4) **Don't accept "but they're the family of so-and-so"** as a reason to vote for someone.

(5) **Hold dynastic leaders to higher standards** — they have privileges most don't, so they should deliver.

Over time, this kind of voting reduces dynasty's hold.

When you grow up, Indian politics may look different. Dynasties may be weaker. Or stronger. The direction depends partly on voters like you. Choose wisely.

## Why Does Money Matter So Much in Indian Politics?

**Student:** The chapter said elections involve enormous money. Why? Can't politics be cheaper?

**Mentor:** Money in politics is one of the great challenges of every democracy, including India. Let me explain why and what can be done.

**How Much Money?**

Estimates vary widely (much is unaccounted), but:
- A Lok Sabha candidate spends ₹5-50 crore on a single campaign (legal limit is ₹95 lakh, regularly exceeded)
- A state assembly candidate spends ₹50 lakh to ₹10 crore
- A panchayat/zilla level candidate spends ₹1-50 lakh
- The 2024 Lok Sabha elections cost an estimated ₹1.35 lakh crore in total — making Indian elections among the most expensive in the world

**Why So Much?**

Several factors:

(1) **Scale**. India has 970 million voters in 543 Lok Sabha constituencies. Reaching even one constituency requires thousands of workers, hundreds of vehicles, massive transportation.

(2) **Campaigns are long**. Indian campaigns last weeks or months.

(3) **Multiple methods**. Rallies, door-to-door, posters, advertisements, social media, paid news, gift distribution.

(4) **Worker payments**. Polling agents, campaign workers, drivers, organisers — all need to be paid (legally or otherwise).

(5) **Vote buying**. Illegal but widespread — cash, liquor, food, gifts given to voters.

(6) **Logistics**. Vehicles, fuel, food, accommodation for workers.

(7) **Media spending**. Advertising in newspapers, TV, online. Some news is paid (called "paid news").

(8) **Security**. Bodyguards, secure venues for top candidates.

(9) **Lawsuits and disputes**. Election-related legal battles.

(10) **Festivals during elections**. Distributing free items at religious events.

**Where Does the Money Come From?**

Several sources:

(1) **Personal wealth of candidates**. Many MLAs and MPs are very wealthy. ADR data shows over 90% of MPs are crorepatis.

(2) **Corporate donations**. Businesses donate to parties.

(3) **Electoral Bonds** (struck down by Supreme Court in 2024). Were anonymous donations to parties.

(4) **Cash from supporters**. Unaccounted, often black money.

(5) **Illegal sources**. Some politicians have alleged links to organised crime.

(6) **State funding** — minimal in India (some indirect through subsidised electoral expenses).

**The Problems.**

(1) **Corruption**. Donors expect favours. Politicians must repay them through contracts, regulatory decisions, appointments.

(2) **Inequality**. Wealthy candidates can outspend poorer ones, regardless of merit.

(3) **Class bias**. Working-class candidates effectively can't compete. Politics becomes a profession of the wealthy.

(4) **Decision capture**. Government decisions are influenced by who donates. Poor and middle-class interests get less attention.

(5) **Erosion of policy focus**. Energy goes to fundraising, not policy.

(6) **Black money in politics**. Most political money is undocumented.

(7) **Vote buying**. Erodes democratic principle.

(8) **Pay-to-play culture**. Companies pay parties to get policies they want.

**The Electoral Bonds Story.**

The **Electoral Bonds scheme** was introduced by the central government in 2018. Donors could buy bonds from State Bank of India and donate them to parties anonymously. The aim was supposedly to clean up donations.

But critics pointed out:
- **Anonymity** — voters couldn't know who was paying parties
- **One-way transparency** — government could see donors (through SBI), but public couldn't
- **Skewed benefits** — ruling parties received most donations
- **Increased corporate power** — companies could fund whoever they wanted

In **February 2024**, the Supreme Court **struck down the Electoral Bonds scheme** as unconstitutional. Information about who bought bonds was made public. The data showed massive donations from corporate interests to ruling parties.

The case revealed:
- Some companies donated very large amounts
- Donors often had government contracts or pending regulatory issues
- The ruling BJP received the majority of bonds
- Other parties also benefited proportionally

This was a landmark moment exposing money in Indian politics.

**What Can Be Done.**

Several reforms have been proposed:

(1) **State funding of elections**. Public money to fund campaigns, reducing dependence on private donations. Many democracies do this.

(2) **Lower spending limits, enforced**. Current limits are violated openly.

(3) **Full transparency**. All donations above small amounts must be public.

(4) **Cap on individual donations**. Limit how much any one source can give.

(5) **Stricter audit**. Independent auditors of party finances.

(6) **Ban on corporate donations** (or very strict limits). Several countries do this.

(7) **Public funding linked to votes received**. Parties get state funds proportional to their popular vote.

(8) **Online and small donations encouraged**. Many small donors are healthier than few large ones.

(9) **Reduce campaign length**. Shorter campaigns cost less.

(10) **More free media coverage**. Public broadcaster gives equal time to all parties.

**International Examples.**

(1) **UK**: Limited campaign spending; state funding of opposition parties; transparency requirements.

(2) **Germany**: Significant state funding; spending limits.

(3) **Sweden**: Mostly state-funded; very low individual spending.

(4) **USA**: Different — almost unlimited private spending allowed; "money is speech." Critics see this as corrupt.

India is moving slowly. Some progress (Supreme Court decisions); but money in politics remains a serious issue.

**Why It's Hard to Reform.**

(1) **Politicians make the rules**. They benefit from current system; have little incentive to change.

(2) **Existing players entrenched**. Wealthy politicians don't want to lose their advantage.

(3) **Corporate resistance**. Companies that benefit from current system fight reform.

(4) **Implementation complexity**. State funding requires careful design.

(5) **Voter apathy**. Many voters accept "this is just how it is."

(6) **Constitutional constraints**. Some reforms need amendments.

**What Citizens Can Do.**

(1) **Reject candidates with unexplained wealth**. Look at candidate affidavits.

(2) **Don't accept bribes**. Refuse cash, gifts, liquor offered for votes.

(3) **Demand transparency**. Ask candidates about their finances.

(4) **Support transparency advocates**. Associations like ADR (Association for Democratic Reforms), candidates who push reforms.

(5) **Vote based on policy and record**, not on money or freebies.

(6) **Speak up about corruption**. Make it socially unacceptable.

(7) **Engage with media** that exposes corruption.

(8) **Use RTI** to expose financial irregularities.

**The Bigger Picture.**

Money in politics is one of the great paradoxes of democracy. Elections are supposed to be about ideas and accountability. But running elections costs money. So money enters politics. And once money is in politics, it distorts everything.

This is not unique to India. Every democracy struggles with it. But Indian elections are particularly expensive and particularly opaque.

The fight to clean up money in politics is one of the great struggles of democratic citizenship. It is slow. It is frustrating. But it matters.

When you turn 18, you'll be voting in an election system distorted by money. Don't let that distortion shape your vote. Choose based on substance. Reject those who buy votes. Demand better. Together, voters can change the system over time — they have done so on many other issues.

This is the work of every generation of citizens. Yours is next.
