## A Question Worth Asking

After studying democracy through power-sharing, federalism, identity politics, and political parties, one big question remains:

**Does democracy actually deliver?**

It's not a frivolous question. Many people — in India and around the world — are losing faith in democracy. They look at the corruption, the slow decisions, the partisan fighting, the unfulfilled promises, and ask: "Is this really better than alternatives?"

This chapter answers that question carefully. The answer is: **yes, democracy is better — but not always in the simple ways we might think**.

## How to Assess Democracy

Before judging democracy, we need to be clear about what we expect.

In Class 9, we noted that democracy is supposed to:
- Promote **equality** among citizens
- Enhance **dignity** of the individual
- Improve **quality of decision-making**
- Provide a **method to resolve conflicts**
- Allow room to **correct mistakes**

These are reasonable expectations. But how does democracy actually perform on each?

We also have to think carefully:

(1) **What is being compared?** Democracy works imperfectly. So does dictatorship. The question is which is worse. Democracy may be flawed; dictatorship is typically worse.

(2) **What time scale?** A new democracy may struggle initially. Mature democracies often do better. Compare like with like.

(3) **What conditions?** Some countries are easier to govern (small, homogeneous, prosperous). Some are very hard (India: 1.4 billion, hugely diverse, low per capita income). Don't compare apples to oranges.

(4) **What outcomes matter?** Economic growth? Equality? Freedom? Stability? Different people weight these differently.

(5) **What is democratically achieved?** Not all outcomes are because of democracy. Some are despite it. Some are unrelated. Be careful.

With these cautions in mind, let us examine democracy's record.

## 1. Quality of Government

### Does Democracy Produce Better Governance?

Compared to dictatorships and monarchies, democracies tend to:

**Better at**:
- **Holding government accountable** — elections force change
- **Avoiding extreme errors** — opposition catches mistakes
- **Self-correction** — bad governments can be replaced
- **Adaptive policy** — when situations change, government can change
- **Citizen voice** — people can express grievances peacefully

**Worse at** (or no better than dictatorships):
- **Speed of decisions** — debates, opposition, courts slow things down
- **Long-term planning** — election cycles favour short-term thinking
- **Tough but unpopular reforms** — politicians fear losing votes
- **Resistance to populism** — politicians sometimes pander to mob sentiment

### Examples

(1) **India vs China**: China decides faster (single-party rule). India decides slower (debates, courts, parliament). Both have advantages.

China built more infrastructure faster. India has slower but more sustainable growth. China can plan 20 years ahead more easily. India is more responsive to popular grievances.

(2) **India vs Pakistan**: Both started similar in 1947. India remained democratic; Pakistan oscillated between democracy and military rule. Today, India is more stable, more developed, more peaceful internally. Democracy seems to have helped.

(3) **India vs Indonesia/Brazil**: All large diverse democracies. All have struggled with corruption, inequality, slow growth at times. Indian democracy has produced reasonable outcomes — neither stellar nor terrible.

### What Democracy Promises

Democracy doesn't promise wise decisions or great leaders. It promises:
- **Government BY consent** (legitimacy)
- **Government UNDER law** (rule of law)
- **Government SUBJECT TO change** (accountability)
- **Government RESPECTING rights** (rights-based)

These are valuable in themselves, even when specific decisions are flawed.

## 2. Economic Growth and Development

### The Big Question

Does democracy lead to economic growth? Or hinder it?

The evidence is **mixed**:

**Democracies that grew well**: USA, India, Germany, Japan, South Korea (later democratic), Brazil at times, Indonesia.

**Democracies that grew poorly**: Argentina, Venezuela (lost it later), Philippines, Nigeria.

**Dictatorships that grew well**: China (after 1978 reforms), South Korea (early military rule), Taiwan (early), Singapore.

**Dictatorships that grew poorly**: North Korea, Cuba, Soviet Union after 1970s, Zimbabwe under Mugabe, Venezuela.

So democracy is **neither necessary nor sufficient for growth**. Many factors matter.

### India's Economic Performance

India's growth rate over 75+ years:
- 1947-1980: about 3.5% per year (the "Hindu rate of growth")
- 1980-1991: about 5.5% per year
- 1991-2024: about 6-7% per year on average

Since the 1991 economic reforms, India has been one of the fastest-growing major economies. By 2027, India is the world's 5th largest economy and projected to become 3rd by 2030.

But: this growth has been uneven. Inequality has risen. Many remain poor.

Was democracy a help or hindrance? Difficult to say definitively. Democracy slowed some reforms (workers oppose privatisation, farmers oppose land acquisition). But democracy also allowed gradual, sustainable reforms rather than shock-therapy disasters.

### Inequality

Most large democracies — India, USA, Brazil — have significant inequality. Top 10% often have 50%+ of national income.

Democracy doesn't automatically produce equality. It can — if voters demand redistributive policies (welfare, taxation, education for all). But majorities don't always demand this.

In India:
- Top 1% holds about 40% of wealth
- Bottom 50% holds about 6%
- Gini coefficient (inequality measure): about 0.35 (moderate by global standards but rising)

This inequality is a problem democracy hasn't solved. Whether democracy can solve it depends on political mobilisation around equality.

### Conclusion on Economic Outcomes

Democracy doesn't guarantee economic growth or equality. It does:
- Generally allow gradual progress
- Avoid catastrophic failures (no famines in democratic India, except possibly Bengal 1943 under British rule)
- Allow self-correction over time
- Provide political channels for economic grievances

This is meaningful — but not as dramatic as some democracy enthusiasts hope.

## 3. Inequality and Social Differences

### Reduces Inequality?

Democracy is supposed to make all citizens equal politically (one person, one vote). But this doesn't automatically produce social or economic equality.

Different democracies have different inequality levels. India has more inequality than Scandinavia, less than the USA in some respects.

What helps reduce inequality:
- **Progressive taxation** (rich pay more)
- **Free education**
- **Universal healthcare**
- **Welfare programmes**
- **Minimum wages**
- **Labour protections**

These all require political support. In some democracies, voters demand these; in others, they don't.

### India's Record

India has made significant progress in some ways:
- **Poverty reduction**: from 50%+ in 1970s to about 10% in 2024
- **Literacy**: from 18% in 1951 to 78% in 2024
- **Life expectancy**: from 32 years in 1947 to 70+ years now
- **Maternal mortality**: dramatic decline
- **Reservations**: brought Dalits, tribals, OBCs into education and government

But:
- Income inequality has WORSENED in recent decades
- Land inequality persists
- Access to quality healthcare is unequal
- Access to quality education is unequal
- Caste discrimination persists in social life

So Indian democracy has reduced absolute poverty but not relative inequality.

## 4. Accommodating Social Diversity

This is where democracy clearly shines.

**India's diversity**: 22 official languages, hundreds of dialects, multiple religions (Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi, tribal, atheist), hundreds of castes, tribal groups, regions.

**The democratic accommodation**: through federalism, linguistic states, reservations, religious freedom, multiple parties, coalition politics, free press, civil society.

**Result**: India has stayed UNITED despite enormous diversity. Compare to:
- Pakistan (lost East Pakistan in 1971)
- Yugoslavia (broke into multiple countries)
- Soviet Union (broke into 15 countries)
- Czechoslovakia (broke into 2)
- Sudan (broke into 2)

India is one of the few large post-colonial countries that has stayed together.

Democracy is partly the reason. By giving each group political voice, by accommodating diversity, by allowing peaceful expression of grievances — democracy made India one country.

This is one of the great achievements of Indian democracy. Often overlooked because it's a NEGATIVE achievement (we didn't break apart). But it is profound.

## 5. Dignity and Freedom

### Individual Dignity

Democracy is built on the idea that **every individual has inherent dignity** — that the citizen is sovereign, that the state serves the people, not vice versa.

In practice:
- **In democracies**, citizens can speak, write, organise, protest, sue the government, vote it out
- **In dictatorships**, citizens are subjects, not sovereigns; they can be arrested, killed, exiled for displeasing the government

This makes a profound difference in daily life — even if you never personally challenge the government, knowing you COULD do so changes your relationship to the state.

### Indian Experience

In India, you can:
- Criticise the Prime Minister without arrest
- File a court case against the government (and win sometimes)
- Vote politicians out of power
- Refuse to vote
- Convert to another religion (mostly)
- Marry someone of another religion (with some social difficulty)
- Move anywhere in the country
- Choose any profession
- Speak any language
- Practise any religion

In an Indian dictatorship, none of these would be reliably available.

Compare to:
- North Korea: cannot leave the country; arrested for criticising leader; one official ideology required
- China: limited rights to criticise government; certain topics forbidden; surveillance everywhere
- Saudi Arabia: limited religious freedom; women's rights restricted; political opposition banned

Indian democracy preserves freedoms that many in the world envy.

### Caste, Gender, Religion

But: dignity is not just about rights against the government. It's also about how citizens treat each other.

Indian democracy hasn't ended:
- Caste discrimination in daily life
- Gender violence and patriarchy
- Religious tensions
- Class discrimination

These persist socially even when government doesn't enforce them. The Constitution gives rights; society sometimes denies them in practice.

This is the work of every generation — using democratic tools to push society toward genuine equality and dignity for all.

## 6. The Cost of Democracy

Democracy isn't free. It has costs:

(1) **Slower decisions**. Debates, opposition, courts, public consultation — all take time. Authoritarian regimes decide faster.

(2) **Compromise**. Democracy requires negotiation. Solutions are often compromises, not ideal.

(3) **Politicians**. Democracy creates political class. Politicians may be corrupt, self-serving, mediocre.

(4) **Elections cost money and energy**. Massive resources go into campaigns rather than governance.

(5) **Populism risk**. Politicians may pander to mob sentiment, ignore difficult truths.

(6) **Short-termism**. Election cycles favour short-term wins over long-term planning.

(7) **Internal conflict**. Public disagreement is normal; can be exhausting.

(8) **Vulnerability to manipulation**. Through misinformation, propaganda, vote-buying.

These are real costs. Democracy isn't perfect or cheap.

But: **the costs of authoritarianism are usually higher**. Lost lives. Lost freedoms. Lost futures. Lost ability to course-correct.

## Should We Expect Democracy to Solve Everything?

**No.**

Democracy is **a method of decision-making**, not a guarantee of good outcomes.

It can:
- Allow citizens to choose leaders
- Allow leaders to be removed
- Allow grievances to be heard
- Allow conflicts to be resolved peacefully
- Allow rights to be protected (in principle)

It cannot:
- Make leaders wise
- Make citizens informed
- Make policies effective
- Make economies grow
- Make society equal
- Make people good

These require WORK — by citizens, by institutions, by leaders. Democracy gives the structure; humans have to do the building.

## The Indian Verdict

After 75+ years, what is India's verdict on democracy?

**Strengths**:
- India has stayed united
- India has remained democratic (rare for newly independent countries)
- Major famines have been avoided
- Civil liberties largely protected
- Many social reforms accomplished
- Significant economic progress
- Continued ability to course-correct

**Weaknesses**:
- Inequality remains
- Corruption is widespread
- Communal tensions occur
- Educational quality varies
- Healthcare is uneven
- Some institutions have been weakened
- Money in politics distorts

**Mixed verdict**:
- Democracy has worked better than most expected in 1947
- Democracy has not delivered all the promises
- Democracy continues to be tested
- Each generation has to make democracy work

**No serious alternative has emerged**:
- Authoritarianism (military rule, monarchy, single-party) has worse track record
- Each Indian crisis (Emergency 1975-77, economic crisis 1991, banking crisis, communal flare-ups) has been resolved democratically
- Citizens have consistently chosen democracy

## What Indian Citizens Get From Democracy

In daily life, you get:
- **The right to vote** — change government peacefully
- **Freedom of speech** — criticise, debate, advocate
- **Freedom of religion** — practise your faith (or none)
- **Freedom of movement** — go anywhere in India
- **Right to education** (until 14)
- **Equal treatment by law** (in principle)
- **Access to courts** — sue the government
- **Information rights** — RTI
- **Right to assemble peacefully** — protest
- **Right to property** (with caveats)
- **Right to choose your profession** (with caveats)
- **Equal opportunity in public employment**

These are not trivial. Most of humanity throughout history has not had these. Most non-democratic countries today do not provide them reliably.

## What Citizens Must Do

For democracy to deliver:

(1) **Vote** — when 18, in every election
(2) **Stay informed** — read news, understand issues
(3) **Engage** — participate in civic life
(4) **Hold power accountable** — through votes, RTI, courts
(5) **Protect institutions** — courts, Election Commission, media
(6) **Defend rights** — yours and others'
(7) **Reject demagogues** — those who promise easy answers
(8) **Build social trust** — across communities, religions, regions
(9) **Demand reforms** — when democracy falls short
(10) **Set example** — be a good citizen in daily life

Democracy isn't a vending machine. You can't just press buttons and expect good outcomes. It is a garden. You have to tend it.

## Conclusion

Democracy delivers a mixed bag — not perfect outcomes, but better than alternatives; some achievements, some failures; meaningful protections, but not guaranteed prosperity.

The Indian record over 75+ years shows: democracy WORKS in India. It has held the country together, made significant progress, protected freedoms. It has also disappointed on inequality, corruption, education quality, and many other issues.

The work continues. Each generation has to make democracy better — through engagement, through reform, through pressure for accountability. Yours is next.

When you grow up, you may sometimes feel frustrated with democracy. You may think dictatorship would be faster, simpler, better. Resist this temptation.

The cost of authoritarianism is enormous. The benefits of democracy — even imperfect democracy — are real. Make democracy better; don't abandon it.

India is one of humanity's largest democratic experiments. It is also one of the most successful, considering where it started. Be proud of it. Defend it. Improve it. Pass it on.

## Key Concepts to Remember

- Assessing democracy: compare to realistic alternatives; consider conditions; multiple outcomes matter.
- **Quality of government**: democracy is better at accountability, self-correction, citizen voice; worse at speed.
- **Economic growth**: democracy is neither necessary nor sufficient for growth, but compatible with sustainable growth.
- India's growth: ~3.5% (1947-80), ~5.5% (1980-91), ~6-7% (1991-now).
- **Inequality**: democracy can reduce inequality (via welfare, taxes) but doesn't automatically.
- India: poverty reduced from 50%+ to 10%; inequality has worsened in some measures.
- **Diversity accommodation**: democracy's biggest success in India — keeping country united despite diversity.
- **Dignity and freedom**: democracy preserves civil liberties; authoritarianism doesn't.
- **Costs of democracy**: slow decisions, compromise, money in politics, populism risk.
- **Democracy is a method, not a guarantee** — outcomes depend on citizens, institutions, leaders.
- **Indian verdict**: imperfect but successful overall; no serious alternative has emerged.
- **Citizens' role**: vote, engage, hold power accountable, defend institutions, reject demagogues.
