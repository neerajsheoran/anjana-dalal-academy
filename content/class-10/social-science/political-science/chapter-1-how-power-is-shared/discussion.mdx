## Why Did Sri Lanka Fail Where Belgium Succeeded?

**Student:** Both Belgium and Sri Lanka had multiple language communities. Why did Belgium succeed at unity while Sri Lanka collapsed into civil war?

**Mentor:** Several factors. Let me unpack them.

**1. Recognition of diversity as legitimate.**

Belgium recognised early that its diversity was a legitimate political reality. Dutch, French, and German communities each had real cultural identity. Belgian leaders accepted this and tried to design a state that accommodated it.

Sri Lanka, after independence, viewed Tamils as a problem to be assimilated or suppressed. Sinhala-Buddhist nationalism dominated political discourse. The state was designed for the majority, not for everyone.

**2. Constitutional commitment to power-sharing.**

Belgium amended its constitution four times between 1970 and 1993 to create one of the most elaborate power-sharing systems in the world. These were **constitutional protections** that could not be easily reversed.

Sri Lanka's constitutional history went the other way. The 1972 constitution centralised power and entrenched Sinhala-Buddhist dominance. Tamil demands for federal autonomy were rejected.

**3. Political leadership choices.**

Belgian leaders, from both communities, chose accommodation over dominance. Even strong Flemish nationalists eventually accepted that Brussels (with its French-speaking majority) needed special arrangements.

Sri Lankan Sinhala leaders, especially after 1956, chose mass mobilisation against Tamils to win elections. They promised what their majorities wanted (Sinhala only) rather than what was wise for the whole country.

**4. International context.**

Belgium was integrated into Europe — first European Coal and Steel Community, then European Union. Membership in larger structures softened internal differences and provided checks on extreme nationalism.

Sri Lanka had no such regional framework. India was involved sporadically (Indian Peace Keeping Force 1987-90) but failed to bring peace. International pressure on Sri Lanka was inconsistent.

**5. Economic incentives.**

Belgium's economy was highly integrated. Flemish, Walloon, and Brussels economies depended on each other. Breaking apart would have hurt everyone economically. This created pragmatic pressure for compromise.

Sri Lanka's economy was less integrated. The Tamil-majority north and east could function relatively independently of the Sinhala south. This made conflict economically more "feasible" (though still devastating).

**6. Religious dimension.**

In Belgium, the language divide didn't track a religious divide — both Flemish and Walloons were largely Catholic. Religion was not weaponised in the conflict.

In Sri Lanka, the ethnic divide tracked a religious one (Sinhala Buddhism vs Tamil Hinduism). Religion intensified the conflict, brought in foreign supporters on both sides, and made compromise harder.

**7. Demographic ratios.**

Belgian Flemish (59%) and Walloon (40%) were close enough in numbers that neither could simply dominate. Both had to share.

Sri Lanka had a clear majority (74% Sinhala) that COULD dominate if it chose. Power-sharing required restraint that the majority did not show.

**8. Timing.**

Belgian power-sharing was developed gradually, over decades, in response to tensions. Each crisis was met with constitutional reform.

In Sri Lanka, opportunities for early compromise (1950s, 1960s) were missed. By the time real reform was discussed (1970s, 1980s), positions had hardened.

**9. The role of violence.**

Once Sri Lankan Sinhala mobs attacked Tamils in 1958 and 1983, normal politics became almost impossible. Tamils armed themselves. The LTTE emerged. Spiral into war.

Belgium had political tensions but never widespread inter-communal violence. Politics remained possible.

**10. Choices, not destiny.**

The most important point: this was NOT inevitable. Both countries had similar challenges. Belgium chose accommodation. Sri Lanka chose dominance. The OUTCOMES were not predetermined by ethnicity or geography. They were **political choices**.

**What can we learn?**

(1) **Recognise diversity as normal**, not as a problem to be eliminated.

(2) **Constitutionalise power-sharing** — make it hard to reverse.

(3) **Leaders choose** — visionary leaders accommodate; majoritarian leaders divide.

(4) **Early reform is cheaper than late conflict** — accommodate before tensions explode.

(5) **Economic integration helps** — interdependence creates incentives for unity.

(6) **Avoid weaponising religion** — keep religious identity out of politics where possible.

(7) **Power-sharing is hard but worth it** — the alternative is far worse.

**Application to India.**

India's choice has been Belgium-like — sharing power among languages (states formed on linguistic lines), religions (secularism), castes (reservations), and regions (federalism). This is constitutionalised and protected.

Critics argue some of these arrangements have become tools of vote-bank politics rather than genuine accommodation. Defenders argue they have prevented India from going the Sri Lanka route despite its much greater diversity.

The lesson for every Indian: **the choice to share power must be renewed every generation**. The day Indians choose majoritarian dominance — whether by religion, language, or region — is the day we start down the Sri Lankan path.

So far, the Indian people, the Constitution, and the institutions have held. Continued vigilance is required.

## Is Power-Sharing Really Wisdom, or Just Compromise?

**Student:** The chapter says power-sharing is wisdom. But isn't it just compromise — taking the easy way out rather than making hard decisions?

**Mentor:** Excellent challenge. Let me address it.

**Some compromise is wisdom; some is weakness.**

**Compromise as weakness** — when you give up on principles to avoid conflict. When you accept ongoing injustice. When you placate bullies. When you "let problems fester."

**Compromise as wisdom** — when you accept that other people, with legitimate interests different from yours, also need a say. When you choose stable peace over total victory. When you build institutions that accommodate diversity.

Power-sharing is the second kind.

**Why power-sharing is more than just compromise.**

(1) **It is based on a principle**: that all citizens are equal and have legitimate claims to influence. This is the democratic principle. Power-sharing operationalises it.

(2) **It builds institutions**: that survive changes in leadership and tension. Constitutional power-sharing is harder to reverse than mere informal compromise.

(3) **It produces better outcomes**: not just less conflict, but more legitimate decisions, more inclusion, more long-term stability.

(4) **It teaches political skills**: how to work with different groups, how to compromise, how to negotiate. These skills make democracies functional.

(5) **It signals respect**: that minority and other groups matter, that they are part of the country, not enemies.

**When power-sharing is wrong.**

That said, power-sharing is not always right. Sometimes:

(1) **You should not share power with antidemocrats.** If a group wants to destroy democracy itself, giving them power-sharing rewards bad behaviour.

(2) **You should not share power based on illegitimate divisions.** Sharing power along caste lines may legitimise caste; sharing along religious lines may permanently divide.

(3) **You should not over-share.** Some decisions need to be made; endless deadlock is worse than imperfect decisions.

(4) **You should not protect minority privileges that are unjust.** If a minority has historic dominance unjustly held, sharing power may freeze that injustice.

So power-sharing is a tool, not a magic solution. **The wisdom is in knowing when and how to apply it**.

**The Indian debate.**

In India, debates about power-sharing are ongoing:

- **Reservations**: Are SC/ST/OBC reservations power-sharing for justice, or perpetuating divisions? Both views have validity.

- **Religious accommodation**: Should religious personal laws (Hindu, Muslim, Christian) be uniform or different? Currently different, with ongoing debate.

- **Language policy**: Should Hindi dominate? Should English be preserved? Should regional languages get more space?

- **Federalism**: Are states being weakened by central agencies (CBI, ED) in opposition states?

- **Tribal autonomy**: Are tribal areas getting genuine self-rule, or just nominal?

These are debates about WHERE and HOW to share power. They are not settled. Each generation has to think about them.

**The bigger philosophical point.**

The strongest argument for power-sharing isn't about avoiding conflict (though that matters). It's about **what kind of democracy we want**.

A democracy where one majority always dominates is not really democracy — it is **majoritarianism**. True democracy includes everyone. Power-sharing is one of the main tools of true democracy.

When the Constitution makers chose to share power across many dimensions in 1947, they weren't being weak. They were building something **morally serious**: a country where every citizen would matter, regardless of which majority they fell outside.

That is wisdom. Not perfect (no system is). But wise.

When you grow up and engage with politics, you'll have to decide: do you want a politics of majoritarian domination ("I have the votes, I do what I want") or a politics of accommodation ("Even if I have the votes, I need to listen and accommodate")?

The first feels stronger. The second is wiser.

The Belgian and Sri Lankan stories tell you the result. Belgium today: stable, prosperous, EU headquarters. Sri Lanka today: still recovering from civil war, economic crisis, fragility.

Choose wisely. Power-sharing isn't the loser's choice. It is the long-term winner's choice.

## How Does India Actually Share Power?

**Student:** The chapter talks about Belgium's elaborate power-sharing. How does India share power in practice?

**Mentor:** India has one of the most complex power-sharing systems in the world. Let me give you a tour.

**1. Horizontal Power-Sharing (Between Organs of Government).**

India's Constitution creates three organs:

- **Legislature**: Parliament (Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha) at the centre; State Legislatures.
- **Executive**: President (nominal), Prime Minister (real) at centre; Governor, Chief Minister at state level. Plus civil services.
- **Judiciary**: Supreme Court, High Courts, lower courts.

The Constitution gives each its own powers. The Supreme Court can strike down unconstitutional laws (1973 Kesavananda Bharati case: even Parliament can't change the "basic structure"). Parliament can impeach judges (a hard process). The PM can be removed by Parliament's no-confidence motion. Etc.

**Strengths**: Multiple veto points prevent any one organ from dominating.

**Tensions**: Sometimes courts strike down laws Parliament passed (e.g., the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, 2014, struck down in 2015). Sometimes governments resist court orders. These tensions are normal — they show power-sharing working.

**2. Vertical Power-Sharing (Federalism).**

India is a **federation** with three levels of government:

(a) **Union (Central) Government**:
- Defence, foreign affairs, currency, atomic energy, railways
- These are on the **Union List** (97 items)
- Constitution gives these powers exclusively to Centre

(b) **State Governments**:
- Police, public health, agriculture, local government, state roads
- These are on the **State List** (66 items)
- Constitution gives these powers exclusively to States

(c) **Concurrent List** (47 items):
- Education, criminal law, marriage, forests
- Both Centre and States can legislate; central law prevails if there's conflict

(d) **Residuary powers**:
- Anything not in any list goes to Centre (unlike USA, where it goes to states)
- This makes India more centralised than some federations

(e) **Local Governments** (added by 73rd and 74th Amendments, 1992):
- Panchayati Raj for villages
- Municipalities for cities
- Constitutional protection now

**3. Power-Sharing Among Social Groups.**

India recognises that certain groups have been historically disadvantaged. Constitutional power-sharing includes:

(a) **Reservations**:
- For SCs (Scheduled Castes) — 15% in legislatures, government jobs, education
- For STs (Scheduled Tribes) — 7.5%
- For OBCs (Other Backward Classes) — 27% in government jobs and central educational institutions (since 1990s; longer in some states)
- For Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) — 10% (since 2019)
- For women in panchayats — 33% to 50% in many states

(b) **Religious freedom**:
- Equal treatment of all religions
- Right to practise, profess, propagate
- Minorities have right to establish educational institutions
- Religious personal laws preserved (Hindu, Muslim, Christian)

(c) **Linguistic rights**:
- 22 official languages in the Eighth Schedule
- States formed on linguistic lines (1956)
- Three-language formula in schools

(d) **Special provisions**:
- Sixth Schedule for tribal areas in Northeast (Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram)
- Fifth Schedule for tribal areas in other states
- Special status (in various forms) for some states

**4. Power-Sharing Among Political Parties.**

India has a **multi-party system** (more on this in Chapter 4):

(a) **Many parties at national level** — BJP, Congress, AAP, and others
(b) **Many regional parties** — DMK, AIADMK, TMC, BJD, JDU, BRS, TDP, YSR Congress, etc.
(c) **Coalition governments common** — from 1989-2014 mostly coalitions; even now BJP rules through NDA alliance
(d) **Strong opposition role** — main opposition leader has constitutional status
(e) **Alternation** — different parties win at different times

**5. Power-Sharing Through Independent Institutions.**

Many institutions in India are deliberately independent of the government in power:

- **Election Commission** — runs elections
- **Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG)** — audits government
- **Supreme Court** — interprets Constitution
- **Reserve Bank of India** — monetary policy
- **Lokpal** — anti-corruption ombudsman
- **National Human Rights Commission**
- **National Commission for Women**
- **National Commission for Minorities**
- **National Commission for SCs**, NCST, NCBC

These create additional veto points and accountability.

**6. Power-Sharing Through Media and Civil Society.**

Beyond formal institutions, power is shared through:
- **Free press** — newspapers, TV, online media
- **Civil society** — NGOs, advocacy groups
- **Trade unions** and **employer associations**
- **Religious bodies**
- **Universities and intellectuals**
- **Social movements** (Chipko, NBA, Right to Information, etc.)

These are non-governmental but exert real power.

**7. Power-Sharing Through Federal Bodies.**

Several formal bodies coordinate Centre and States:
- **NITI Aayog** (replaced Planning Commission in 2014)
- **GST Council** — for goods and services tax
- **National Development Council**
- **Inter-State Council**

These give states a voice in national decisions.

**8. Some Power-Sharing Tensions Today.**

India's power-sharing is contested in several ways:

(a) **Centre vs States**: Opposition-ruled states often complain Centre uses CBI/ED disproportionately against them. GST has reduced state fiscal autonomy.

(b) **Reservations debate**: Some say reservations should be expanded; others say they should be phased out; courts have set 50% as a general cap (with exceptions).

(c) **One nation, one X**: "One nation, one election" idea is debated — would centralise election cycles. "Uniform Civil Code" would replace religious personal laws. "Hindi as national language" issue keeps recurring.

(d) **Strong centre**: Critics say recent years have seen central agencies (CBI, ED, IT) used to weaken states. Defenders say strong centre is needed for national progress.

(e) **Special status revocation**: Removal of Article 370 (Jammu & Kashmir's special status) in 2019 was a major power-sharing event — pro and con debated.

**The Big Picture.**

India is one of the most elaborately power-sharing democracies in the world — sharing across:
- Organs of government
- Levels (Centre, State, Local)
- Religions
- Languages
- Castes (SC, ST, OBC, EWS, others)
- Regions
- Genders (in panchayats and increasingly elsewhere)
- Political parties

This complexity is sometimes frustrating. Decisions can be slow. Coalitions can be fragile. Federal disputes can paralyse projects.

But this complexity is also India's strength. It has kept India together despite enormous diversity. Few countries with such diversity have stayed united.

When you grow up and engage with politics, you'll see these power-sharing arrangements in action. You'll see some working well, others struggling, others contested.

Your job as a citizen: **understand them, value them, work to improve them**. Don't take them for granted. Don't easily accept arguments to reduce power-sharing in the name of efficiency.

A more centralised India may be faster. It would also be less Indian — less true to the deep diversity that has been India's strength for millennia.

Power-sharing, well-designed and well-maintained, is one of India's quiet achievements. Be a guardian of it.
