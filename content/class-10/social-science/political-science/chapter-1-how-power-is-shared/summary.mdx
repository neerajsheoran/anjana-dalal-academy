## In One Paragraph

A common intuition says "**Sharing power = weakening the country.**" Modern political science proves the opposite: in diverse societies, sharing power makes a country stronger and more stable; concentrating power eventually causes conflict and collapse. Two contrasting stories illustrate this. **Belgium**, a small country with Dutch-speaking (59%), French-speaking (40%), and German-speaking (1%) communities, faced tensions in the 1950s-60s. Belgians chose elaborate power-sharing — equal representation in central government, regional governments with constitutional powers, separate Brussels and community governments — and stayed united. **Sri Lanka**, with Sinhalese (74%) and Tamil (13%) communities, chose majoritarianism — Sinhala as the only official language (1956), Buddhism as state religion, job and education preferences for Sinhalese. The result: 30 years of civil war (1983-2009), 100,000+ dead, economic ruin. There are **two reasons to share power**: prudential (avoids conflict) and moral (democracy demands inclusion). Power can be shared in many ways: **horizontally** (between legislature, executive, judiciary), **vertically** (Centre, State, Local), among **social groups** (reservations, community governments), among **political parties** (coalitions), and through **pressure groups** (trade unions, civil society). India chose power-sharing through federalism, three-tier government, religious freedom, and reservations — one reason India has stayed united despite its huge diversity.

## Key Takeaways

- **Power-sharing is wisdom, not weakness** — diverse societies become stronger by accommodating, not by suppressing differences.
- **Belgium** (success story): Dutch-French-German linguistic mix; complex power-sharing keeps country united. Equal ministers, regional governments, community governments.
- **Sri Lanka** (cautionary tale): Sinhala-majoritarian approach after 1948 led to 30-year civil war (1983-2009), 100,000+ dead.
- **Two reasons for power-sharing**: (1) **Prudential** — avoids conflict, civil war is costly; (2) **Moral** — democracy = inclusion of all citizens.
- **Forms of power-sharing**:
  - **Horizontal** — between organs of government (legislature, executive, judiciary) — "separation of powers" (Montesquieu).
  - **Vertical** — between levels (Centre, State, Local) — federalism.
  - **Among social groups** — through reservations, community governments, vetoes, quotas.
  - **Among political parties** — multiple parties competing, coalitions, opposition role.
  - **Through pressure groups and movements** — trade unions, civil society, media, NGOs.
- **India's approach**: federalism + reservations (SC/ST/OBC) + religious freedom + three-tier government + linguistic states.
- **Criticisms of power-sharing**: slower decisions, possible deadlock, institutionalises divisions. But the alternative (authoritarianism) is worse — civil war is more destructive than slow decisions.
- **Around the world**: USA, UK, Switzerland, Northern Ireland, South Africa, Lebanon, Iraq, India — all use different forms of power-sharing suited to their contexts.

## A Phrase to Remember

> **Power-sharing isn't weakness. It is wisdom.**

A country that concentrates power may seem decisive in the short run. It almost always becomes brittle, unstable, and eventually conflicted in the long run. Sharing power is the harder, slower path — and the path that builds lasting unity.
