## Why Talk About Sharing Power?

A common intuition in politics is: **"Sharing power = dividing power = weakening the country."**

If you have ever heard a strong-willed person say "I should be in charge — that way we can decide quickly without arguing" — you've heard this intuition. It is the temptation of every strong leader.

But the deepest lesson of modern political science is that **this intuition is wrong**. In any diverse society, **sharing power makes the country stronger, not weaker**. Concentrating power may seem decisive in the short run, but it usually produces violence, breakdown, and eventual collapse in the long run.

In this chapter, we will see two stories — **Belgium** and **Sri Lanka** — that show what happens when countries choose differently. Then we will look at the general logic of power-sharing in modern democracies.

## Story 1 — Belgium: A Success Story

**Belgium** is a small country in Europe — smaller than Haryana in area, with a population of about one crore (10 million).

But its ethnic composition is complex:
- **59% Flemish** (Dutch-speaking) — mostly in the north
- **40% French-speaking** (Walloon) — mostly in the south
- **1% German-speaking** — small eastern region
- **Brussels** (the capital) is 80% French-speaking, 20% Dutch-speaking — though it's surrounded by Dutch-speaking territory

### The Tension

After World War II, the French-speaking minority was relatively richer and held more power. The Dutch-speaking Flemish, who were the majority of Belgium overall, resented this. The Flemish gradually demanded more rights, more political power, more development for their region.

This wasn't just about culture. It was about jobs, schools, government offices — real things that affected people's lives.

By the 1960s-70s, tensions were high. There were riots. There were demands for separation. Belgium could have broken apart.

### The Belgian Solution — Power-Sharing

Instead of one side winning and the other losing, Belgians chose to **share power**. Between 1970 and 1993, the Belgian constitution was amended four times to create one of the most complex power-sharing systems in the world.

**Key features**:

1. **Equal representation in central government.** In the central government, both Dutch and French-speaking ministers must have **equal numbers**, regardless of population. Some special laws need the support of both communities.

2. **Powers given to regional governments.** Many powers were transferred from the central government to the **regional governments** (Flanders, Wallonia, Brussels). These are constitutional powers — the central government cannot take them back.

3. **Brussels gets a separate government.** Brussels has its own government with equal representation of both communities.

4. **A "Community Government" for cultural matters.** A separate government deals with cultural, educational, language issues — elected only by people belonging to that language community, regardless of where they live.

5. **Strong protection for minorities.** No community can be outvoted on important issues.

### The Result

Belgium has stayed united. There are still tensions, occasional crises, even periods without a national government (one stretched over 500 days!). But the country functions. Different communities live together. There has been no civil war.

Belgium is now home to the headquarters of the European Union and NATO. Its capital, Brussels, is the de facto capital of Europe. Power-sharing made this stable Belgium possible.

## Story 2 — Sri Lanka: A Cautionary Tale

**Sri Lanka** is an island nation just south of Tamil Nadu, with about 2 crore people.

Its population:
- **Sinhalese**: 74% (mostly Buddhist)
- **Sri Lankan Tamils**: 13% (mostly Hindu, descendants of ancient settlers)
- **Indian Tamils**: 5% (descendants of plantation workers brought during colonial times)
- **Muslims**: about 7%

### Independence and Majoritarian Politics

After independence in 1948, the Sinhala majority assumed they would dominate. Several decisions reinforced this:

- **1956**: An Act made **Sinhala the only official language**, ignoring Tamil.
- Preference in **government jobs and universities** for Sinhala speakers.
- **Constitution favoured Buddhism**, making it the official religion.

The result: Tamils felt **marginalised in their own country**. Their language, religion, and culture were treated as second-class. Government jobs and university seats became harder to access.

### The Spiral into Civil War

Tamil parties demanded more autonomy, then federal arrangement, then equal rights, then eventually their own homeland (Eelam). Initially these were peaceful political demands.

The Sinhala-majority government either ignored or violently suppressed these demands. **Pogroms** (organised violence against Tamils) erupted at various times, especially in 1958 and 1983 ("Black July").

By the 1980s, Tamil militant groups emerged. The most notable was the **Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE)**, who turned to armed struggle.

Sri Lanka descended into a **civil war that lasted nearly 30 years (1983-2009)**.

### The Cost

The Sri Lankan civil war was one of the most brutal in modern history:
- **70,000–100,000 people killed** (some estimates higher)
- **Hundreds of thousands** displaced or refugees
- **Economy devastated** for decades
- **Pioneered suicide bombing** (LTTE used it extensively, including to assassinate Indian PM Rajiv Gandhi in 1991)
- **Final phase (2009)** was extremely violent — both sides committed atrocities, civilians caught in between, perhaps 40,000 killed in the final months

The Sri Lankan government won militarily in 2009. But the country has been struggling ever since — economic crisis in 2022, ongoing ethnic tensions, calls for accountability.

### The Tragic Lesson

Sri Lanka had a choice in 1948. It could have shared power with its Tamil minority. It chose majoritarian dominance instead.

The result: 30 years of civil war, massive death, economic ruin, ongoing instability.

This is what happens when a diverse country refuses to share power.

## Belgium vs Sri Lanka — The Key Lesson

The contrast is stark:

| Aspect | Belgium | Sri Lanka |
|--------|---------|-----------|
| Approach | Power-sharing | Majoritarian |
| Outcome | Unity, prosperity | Civil war, instability |
| Toll | Tensions but stable | 100,000+ dead |
| Today | Functioning democracy | Recovering, fragile |

Both countries had similar challenges — ethnic diversity and language differences. Both could have gone either way. **The choice to share or not share power made all the difference**.

## Why Power-Sharing Is Wise

There are two big reasons.

### 1. Prudential Reason — It Avoids Conflict

Sharing power is **prudent** because it reduces conflict:
- Diverse societies have multiple groups with different interests
- If one group dominates, others feel oppressed
- Oppressed groups eventually resist — often violently
- The cost of suppressing them is far higher than the cost of sharing power
- Civil wars destroy countries; power-sharing keeps them whole

So even from a purely self-interested perspective, **power-sharing is rational**.

### 2. Moral Reason — Democracy Demands It

Power-sharing is also **morally required** in a democracy:
- Democracy = rule of the people
- All people are equal citizens
- A democracy where one group dominates is not a true democracy
- It treats some citizens as second-class
- True democratic legitimacy comes from including everyone

So power-sharing is **the very spirit of democracy**.

These two reasons reinforce each other. **Practical wisdom and moral right point the same way: share power.**

## Forms of Power-Sharing

How can power be shared? In several ways.

### 1. Horizontal Power-Sharing (Among Organs of Government)

Power is shared among:
- **Legislature** — makes laws
- **Executive** — implements laws
- **Judiciary** — interprets laws

Each organ checks the others ("checks and balances"). No one can dominate.

This is the classical theory of **separation of powers** — going back to Montesquieu (18th century French philosopher) and earlier. It has become the standard structure of modern democracies, including India.

In India: Parliament makes laws; the Prime Minister and Cabinet implement them; the Supreme Court interprets them. The judiciary can strike down laws that violate the Constitution. Parliament can pass laws to override (some) court decisions. Each balances the others.

### 2. Vertical Power-Sharing (Between Levels of Government)

Power is shared among:
- **Central government**
- **State government**
- **Local government** (villages, towns, cities)

This is called **federalism** when constitutionally protected (as in India, USA, Germany). It is **decentralisation** when the central government can take it back (as in unitary states like UK or France that have devolved some powers).

In India, the Constitution clearly divides powers between Union and State (Seventh Schedule with Union List, State List, Concurrent List). The 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992) added local government as a third level.

### 3. Power-Sharing Among Social Groups

In diverse societies, power can also be shared among:
- **Religious groups**
- **Linguistic groups**
- **Ethnic groups**
- **Regional groups**

This can be done through:
- **Reservations** (seats in legislatures for specific groups)
- **Community government** (separate decision-making for cultural matters)
- **Veto powers** (no decision can pass without certain groups' consent)
- **Quotas in employment, education**

In India: SC/ST reservations in legislatures, government jobs, and education. Reservations for OBCs since 1990. The Belgium model has even more elaborate community-based power-sharing.

### 4. Power-Sharing Among Political Parties

In a democracy, multiple political parties compete. Power is shared through:
- **Coalition governments** — when no single party has majority
- **Opposition role** — opposition checks the ruling party
- **Alternation** — different parties win at different times
- **Pressure groups** — civil society, business associations, trade unions all influence policy

This is **competition for power** — different parties, all sharing the right to seek power through elections.

### 5. Power-Sharing Through Pressure Groups and Movements

Even outside formal government, power is exercised through:
- **Trade unions** (workers)
- **Business associations** (employers)
- **Farmers' organisations**
- **Religious groups**
- **NGOs** and **civil society**
- **Media** as a "fourth pillar" of democracy

These groups don't run for elections, but they influence policy. They are part of how power is distributed in modern democracies.

## Why India Embraced Power-Sharing

India in 1947 faced challenges similar to (and bigger than) Belgium's:
- Many languages
- Many religions
- Many regions
- Hundreds of castes and communities

The Constitution makers chose power-sharing in multiple ways:

- **Three organs of government** — separate legislature, executive, judiciary
- **Three-level federalism** — Centre, State, Local
- **Reservations** — for SCs, STs (and later OBCs)
- **Religious freedom** — equal for all faiths
- **Language protection** — official languages, three-language formula
- **States reorganised on linguistic lines** (1956) — letting each language community have its own state

This was a deliberate, conscious choice. The framers had seen what Partition did when religious differences turned into political division. They chose accommodation, not domination.

Imperfect? Yes. India has had communal riots, language agitations, regional struggles. But India has stayed united. Unlike many post-colonial countries, India did not break apart along its many fault lines.

Power-sharing is one of the reasons.

## Critics of Power-Sharing

Not everyone agrees power-sharing is wise:

- **Critics say**: power-sharing slows decision-making
- **Critics say**: it gives veto power to minorities to block national progress
- **Critics say**: it institutionalises divisions rather than helping people move past them
- **Critics say**: strong central authority is needed for development

These criticisms have some validity. Power-sharing can be slow. Coalition governments can be unstable. Vetoes can block needed reforms.

But the alternative — strong authoritarian central government — has worse problems:
- Suppresses dissent
- Cannot adapt to diverse needs
- Eventually triggers rebellion
- Civil war is more destructive than slow decisions

The trick is to **design power-sharing well** — strong enough to govern, balanced enough to include all. Modern constitutional design tries to thread this needle.

## Power-Sharing Around the World

Different countries do power-sharing in different ways:

- **USA**: Strong federalism + strict separation of powers between President, Congress, Supreme Court
- **UK**: Strong central government, but devolution to Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland
- **Switzerland**: Multi-language federalism + frequent referendums
- **Belgium**: Complex linguistic federalism
- **India**: Federal structure + reservations + religious freedom + three-tier government
- **Northern Ireland**: Power-sharing between Catholics and Protestants
- **South Africa**: Government of National Unity (after apartheid)
- **Lebanon**: Confessional system (positions reserved for specific religious communities)
- **Iraq**: Sharing power among Sunni, Shia, Kurd

Each system reflects its country's specific history and challenges. There is no single "correct" form. The principle is universal: **share power, share success**.

## Conclusion

Power-sharing is not a sign of weakness. It is a sign of **maturity** — political maturity that recognises diverse interests and accommodates them.

Concentrating power may seem strong in the short run. It always proves weak in the long run.

The Belgian story shows this works. The Sri Lankan story shows the alternative.

India's choice was for power-sharing. The Constitution embodies this choice. Every generation of Indians has the responsibility to keep this commitment alive — accommodating diverse communities, sharing power across regions and groups, balancing the strong central government with state autonomy and local democracy.

When you encounter politicians or commentators arguing for concentrating power, remember the lesson: **power-sharing isn't weakness. It's wisdom.**

## Key Concepts to Remember

- **Power-sharing**: distributing power among different groups, levels, organs of government.
- **Belgian model**: power-sharing among linguistic communities — both at central and regional levels.
- **Sri Lankan model**: majoritarianism — Sinhala dominance led to civil war.
- **Two reasons for power-sharing**: prudential (avoids conflict) and moral (democracy demands inclusion).
- **Forms of power-sharing**: horizontal (organs), vertical (levels), social groups, political parties, pressure groups.
- **India's approach**: federalism + reservations + religious freedom + three-tier government.
- **The big lesson**: sharing power makes diverse societies stronger, not weaker.
