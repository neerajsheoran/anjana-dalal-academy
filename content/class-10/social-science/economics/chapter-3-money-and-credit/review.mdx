## In One Paragraph

**Money** solved an old problem — **barter** required a **double coincidence of wants** (both parties wanting what the other had). Money is a common medium of exchange that everyone accepts. Modern Indian money takes two main forms — **currency** (notes and coins, issued by the **Reserve Bank of India** as legal tender) and **deposits with banks** (mostly **demand deposits** that can be transferred by **cheque**, debit card, UPI, or net banking). Banks keep only a small fraction (about 15%) of deposits as cash; the rest is lent out. They pay depositors **4–6% interest** and charge borrowers **8–12%** — the difference is their profit. The **RBI** issues currency, acts as banker to commercial banks, and supervises the whole banking system. Indian credit flows through two channels — **formal** (banks, cooperatives — regulated, paperwork, collateral, fair rates) and **informal** (moneylenders, traders, relatives — unregulated, fast, but often charging **36–60% per year**). Every loan has four terms: **interest rate, collateral, documentation, mode of repayment**. The poor pay six times more for credit than the rich, and many fall into **debt traps**. India's response includes **Self-Help Groups** (small groups of women pooling savings, supported by **NABARD**), the pioneering **SEWA Bank** (Ahmedabad, 1974, founded by Ela Bhatt), and recent policy pushes — **Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (2014)** and **MUDRA loans (2015)**. The **2010 Andhra microfinance crisis** is a reminder that lending to the poor is not automatically good — it must be on fair, transparent terms.

## Key Takeaways

- **Barter** required a **double coincidence of wants** — both parties wanting what the other had at the same time. **Money** solved this by being acceptable to everyone.
- Money serves three uses — **medium of exchange**, **measure of value**, and **store of value**.
- **Modern money** in India = **currency** (notes and coins, issued by RBI, **legal tender**) + **deposits** in banks (mostly **demand deposits**).
- Demand deposits can be moved by **cheque**, debit card, **UPI**, and net banking — making them work like money.
- Banks keep only about **15% of deposits as cash** and lend out the rest. This is the foundation of modern banking.
- Banks pay **4–6%** to depositors and charge **8–12%** to borrowers — the gap (interest spread) is their profit.
- The **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** — founded 1935, headquartered Mumbai — has **three big roles**: (1) **issuer of currency**, (2) **banker to banks** (sets repo rate, manages reserves), (3) **supervisor** of commercial banks.
- India has **two channels of credit**: **formal** (banks, cooperatives — RBI-regulated) and **informal** (moneylenders, traders, relatives — unregulated, often charging **36–60% per year**).
- Every loan has **four terms** that decide its real cost — **interest rate, collateral, documentation, mode of repayment**.
- The **poor pay more** for credit because they lack paperwork and collateral. Many fall into **debt traps** where interest grows faster than income.
- **Self-Help Groups (SHGs)** — 15–20 women per group, pool weekly savings, lend within the group, then borrow from banks as a group. Backed by **NABARD** since the 1990s; now over 1 crore SHGs and 12+ crore members in India.
- **SEWA Bank** (Ahmedabad, **1974**), founded with **Ela Bhatt**, was a cooperative bank by and for poor self-employed women — pioneered Indian microfinance.
- The **2010 Andhra microfinance crisis** showed that micro-credit without strong regulation can become a new debt trap. RBI later capped MFI rates and tightened rules.
- Recent reforms — **Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (Aug 2014)** brought 40+ crore households into banking; **MUDRA loans (Apr 2015)** offer up to ₹10 lakh to small businesses without classical collateral.

## Key Terms

- **Barter** — direct exchange of one good for another, without money.
- **Double coincidence of wants** — both parties needing what the other has, at the same time and place.
- **Currency** — notes and coins issued by the central authority (RBI in India); legal tender.
- **Legal tender** — money that by law must be accepted in payment of debts.
- **Demand deposit** — money in a bank account that can be withdrawn or transferred on demand.
- **Cheque** — written instruction to a bank to pay a stated amount from the writer's account.
- **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** — India's central bank; issues currency, supervises banks, sets monetary policy.
- **Collateral** — an asset (land, gold, deposit) pledged as security for a loan.
- **Formal credit** — loans from banks and cooperatives, regulated by RBI.
- **Informal credit** — loans from moneylenders, traders, employers, relatives, friends — unregulated.
- **Self-Help Group (SHG)** — small group, usually of women, that pools savings and lends among members.
- **NABARD** — National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development; backs SHG–bank linkage.

## A Phrase to Remember

> **Trust the poor with credit, on fair terms, and they will repay.**

Indian banking spread further in the last decade than in the previous fifty years — through Jan Dhan, UPI, MUDRA, and the SHG movement. But the test of every new scheme is the same one the moneylender failed: does it give the borrower a fair chance, or quietly trap them deeper? Money is a public agreement. Credit is the place where that agreement is most honestly tested.
