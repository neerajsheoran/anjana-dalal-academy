## Why Did Barter Stop Working?

**Child:** Maa, what is the point of money? Can't people just trade things directly?

**Mother:** They can, beta. For thousands of years that is exactly what people did. It is called **barter** — you swap one thing for another. The farmer gives the potter some rice; the potter gives the farmer pots. Both go home happy.

**Child:** That sounds simple. So why did we invent money at all?

**Mother:** Because it only works when each person wants what the other has, at the exact same time. Imagine the potter is hungry today. He goes looking for rice. The farmer with the rice already has plenty of pots — he doesn't need any more. What happens?

**Child:** They can't trade.

**Mother:** Right. The potter now has to find a third person — someone who has rice AND wants pots AND wants to trade today. Maybe a fourth person, a fifth. Hours go by. The potter is still hungry. That awkward problem has a name in your textbook — the **double coincidence of wants**. Both parties have to coincide — both must want what the other has, both at the same time.

**Child:** So in a small village it was just about manageable.

**Mother:** In a small village with a few products, yes. But imagine a town with a hundred different trades — carpenters, washermen, tailors, teachers, oilmen, milkmen. Now your tailor wants oil, the oilman wants pots, the potter wants milk, the milkman wants tuition for his son, the tutor wants a shirt. Try matching all those swaps in one day. Half the town goes home without what it needs.

**Child:** So money was the fix.

**Mother:** Yes. Money is the one thing that **everybody agrees to accept**. The tailor sells his shirts to anyone for rupees. With those rupees he buys oil from anyone. The double coincidence problem vanishes. Money doesn't have value because of what it is. It has value because all of us agree to take it. It is a public agreement, beta. Nothing more, nothing less.

## What Does the RBI Do That Other Banks Don't?

**Child:** Maa, you have an account at State Bank. Where does the RBI fit in?

**Mother:** I cannot open an account at the RBI, beta. Nobody can — not you, not me, not the richest businessman in the country. The RBI is a different kind of bank — it sits above all other banks.

**Child:** So it doesn't take deposits at all?

**Mother:** Not from ordinary people. It does three big things. **One**, it prints and issues all the rupee notes in India. Look at any ten-rupee note — at the top it says "Reserve Bank of India" and below the promise is the Governor's signature.

**Child:** What is the second thing?

**Mother:** It is the **banker to all other banks**. State Bank, HDFC, ICICI — every commercial bank in India has an account with the RBI. When State Bank runs short of cash, it borrows from the RBI. When it has extra, it parks the money there. The rate at which the RBI lends to other banks is called the **repo rate**. When the repo rate goes up, your home loan interest also tends to go up.

**Child:** So when I see news about the RBI changing the repo rate, that is what is happening?

**Mother:** Exactly. And the third role — it **supervises** all the commercial banks. It tells them how much cash they must keep aside, watches whether they are lending fairly, and steps in if a bank is in trouble.

**Child:** So if a bank somewhere collapses?

**Mother:** The RBI steps in. It might force a merger with a stronger bank, or arrange a rescue. It is the ultimate referee of the Indian money system, beta.

## Why Do Moneylenders Charge So Much?

**Child:** Maa, my friend's father took a loan from a moneylender at 5 per cent per month. The teacher said that is 60 per cent per year. A bank charges only nine. Why is the moneylender so much more expensive?

**Mother:** Three reasons, beta. The first is that he can. Many poor families have no other option. They cannot produce salary slips or land papers. The bank says no. The moneylender says yes. When a person is desperate — for a wedding, an illness, a sowing season — they will pay almost any rate just to get the money in their hand today.

**Child:** And the second?

**Mother:** The moneylender takes more risk than the bank, or at least he claims to. He does no paperwork, takes no formal collateral, has no court system to fall back on. If the borrower runs away, he has to chase them himself. So he says — "If one borrower out of five doesn't repay, I must charge the other four enough to cover my loss." That is partly true.

**Child:** Only partly?

**Mother:** Yes, because the third reason is the worst one — **monopoly power**. In many villages there is only one or two moneylenders. The borrower cannot shop around. The moneylender knows this and squeezes. He may even insist that the borrower also sell the harvest to him, at a price he sets. Multiple costs are loaded on top of the interest. The whole thing is designed so that the borrower can never quite clear the loan.

**Child:** So they get trapped.

**Mother:** This is called a **debt trap**, beta. Interest grows faster than income. The original three thousand becomes five thousand, then ten. Sometimes the family loses their land — pledged for a small loan, lost forever. Across rural India, millions of families are caught in some version of this trap. It is one reason the country pushes so hard to expand bank lending, SHGs, and Jan Dhan accounts. The cheaper the formal credit, the weaker the moneylender's grip.

## How Are SHGs Different from Banks?

**Child:** Maa, my class teacher said her sister is part of an SHG in the village. What is it really?

**Mother:** A **Self-Help Group**, beta. It is one of the most beautiful ideas in modern Indian banking. Picture fifteen or twenty women, all from the same neighbourhood, who meet once a week. Each one puts a small fixed amount into a common pot — maybe twenty-five rupees, maybe fifty.

**Child:** And then what happens to that money?

**Mother:** They lend it back to each other. One member needs three hundred rupees to repair her sewing machine — the group lends to her. Another needs five hundred rupees for school fees — the group lends. Each loan is decided collectively in the weekly meeting. The group also keeps the books.

**Child:** Why doesn't it become like a moneylender?

**Mother:** Because the group is the lender, beta — and the lender is also the neighbour, the friend, the woman who sits next to her in the meeting. The interest is fair. Repayment is enforced gently — through reminders, not through threats. And every rupee of profit stays inside the group.

**Child:** What is the role of the bank then?

**Mother:** After the group has shown it can save and repay for a year or so, a bank steps in. It lends a larger sum — say one lakh rupees — to the group as a whole, at the regular bank rate. The group then onlends to its members. The bank trusts the group, the group knows its members. It works because the trust is local.

**Child:** That sounds different from a normal bank.

**Mother:** Very different. A bank is a building, an SHG is a circle of neighbours. A bank lends to individuals; an SHG lends to its own members. A bank wants collateral; an SHG wants commitment. There are over a crore SHGs in India today, beta, with more than twelve crore women members. They have changed rural India in ways the textbooks are still catching up with.

## What's a "Demand Deposit" Anyway?

**Child:** Maa, what is a demand deposit? It sounds complicated.

**Mother:** It is not complicated at all, beta. When you put your money into a savings account, the bank holds it for you. You can ask for it back **any time you want** — that is, you can **demand** it. So the bank calls it a **demand deposit**. That's all.

**Child:** But the money is just sitting there?

**Mother:** Not really. This is the part most people don't realise. When you deposit money in a bank, the bank does not lock it in a vault. It keeps only a small fraction as cash — about fifteen per cent — and lends the rest to other people. Your salary deposit might be funding a tractor loan in Punjab or a shop loan in Indore.

**Child:** Wait — so if everyone goes to the bank tomorrow and asks for their money back, the bank can't pay?

**Mother:** Correct. In a panic, no bank in the world can pay everyone at once. But normally, this never happens. Some people deposit, some withdraw, and the bank balances the two. The RBI also monitors how much cash each bank must keep aside, so that everyday withdrawals are always met.

**Child:** And demand deposits act like money?

**Mother:** Exactly. You don't need to carry rupee notes to pay your school fees. You write a cheque, or you do a UPI transfer, or you swipe a card. Money moves from your account to someone else's account without a single note changing hands. That is what makes demand deposits a form of money.

**Child:** So most of the money in India is invisible?

**Mother:** Yes, beta. The notes in our hands are the smaller part. The bigger part is in deposit accounts, moving every day through cheques, debit cards, and UPI. Modern money is mostly numbers in a computer that everyone trusts.

## What Should I Take From This?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter has so many topics — money, banks, RBI, SHGs, SEWA, the 2010 crisis. If I had to remember just a few things, what should they be?

**Mother:** Five things, beta. I'll keep them short.

**One — Money is trust, not paper.** The note in your hand is valuable because the whole country agrees to accept it. Lose the trust and the paper becomes paper. Every banking crisis in history is at its heart a crisis of trust.

**Two — Most modern money is invisible.** Far more rupees sit in bank accounts than in pockets. UPI, debit cards, cheques — these are how the bulk of Indian money actually moves. You can run a whole business now without ever touching a note.

**Three — Every loan has two faces.** It can lift a family up — a small business loan, a home loan, a tractor loan paid back honestly. Or it can pull a family down — when the interest is 60 per cent and the lender holds your land. The same word "loan" hides very different stories. Always ask: at what cost?

**Four — The poor pay more for credit, and they shouldn't.** This is one of the deepest unfairnesses in our economy. The whole point of Jan Dhan, MUDRA, SHGs, NABARD, and SEWA is to fix that gap. Slowly, it is closing.

**Five — Institutions matter.** RBI, NABARD, SHGs, SEWA Bank, cooperative banks — these are not abstract things in a textbook. They decide who in India can borrow, on what terms, with what dignity. When you grow up, beta, and you read about banking schemes or microfinance scandals, ask the simple question — does this help the woman pushing a vegetable cart, or harm her? That one question gets you to the heart of every story about money in India.

**Child:** Trust, invisible money, two faces of credit, fairness for the poor, and institutions.

**Mother:** That's the chapter. Now go put your pocket money in your piggy bank — the smallest bank in our house, but the one you control.
