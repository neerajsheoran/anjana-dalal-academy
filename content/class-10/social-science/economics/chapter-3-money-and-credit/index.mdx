## Why Talk About Money at All?

Walk into any Indian city or village and watch what happens in a single hour. A vegetable vendor in Pune sells a kilo of tomatoes for forty rupees. A schoolteacher in Bhopal pays her electricity bill on her phone. A weaver in Varanasi takes a small loan to buy silk thread. A tea-stall owner in Patna deposits the day's collection at a nearby bank branch before going home.

Each of these tiny acts depends on something we almost never stop to think about — **money**. And several of them depend on something even less visible — **credit**, which is just a polite word for borrowed money.

Without these two inventions, none of modern life would be possible. You could not buy a bus ticket, run a tuition class, or save for college. You could not start a kirana shop, repair a tractor, or build a home. Money and credit are the quiet engines that make the economy move.

This chapter asks four big questions. **One** — why did human beings ever need money in the first place? **Two** — what counts as money in India today? **Three** — what does the Reserve Bank of India actually do? And **four** — when poor families need to borrow, why is the cost of credit so different for them than for the rich?

## The Problem Before Money — Double Coincidence of Wants

Imagine a village 3,000 years ago. There is no money. A potter has surplus pots. A farmer has surplus rice. The potter is hungry and wants rice. The farmer needs new pots. They meet, swap, and go home happy. This is **barter** — direct exchange of one good for another.

Now imagine a different day. The potter is hungry and wants rice. He meets the farmer — but the farmer already has plenty of pots and doesn't want any. The exchange cannot happen.

The potter must now find someone who **both wants pots and has rice** — at the same time, in the same place, for the same quantity. This is called the problem of **double coincidence of wants**. Both parties must want what the other has, at the same moment.

In a small village with a few products, this is awkward but possible. In a town with hundreds of trades — carpenters, cobblers, oilmen, washermen, teachers — it becomes nearly impossible. You cannot pay your child's teacher with a sack of mustard seed if the teacher prefers a wedding sari.

The invention of money solved this problem. Money is anything that a society agrees can be used as a **common medium of exchange**. The potter sells his pots to anyone for money. He then takes that money and buys rice from anyone who has it. The double coincidence problem disappears, because money is acceptable to everyone.

Money also lets people **measure value** (a goat is worth so many rupees, a phone is worth so many rupees) and **store value** for the future (you can put money aside, but you cannot put aside tomatoes for very long).

## Modern Forms of Money — Currency and Deposits

In India, money takes two main shapes.

**Currency** — the rupee notes and coins you carry in your pocket. In India, the Reserve Bank of India issues all currency notes on behalf of the central government. The smallest note is ten rupees; the largest is five hundred. Coins are minted by the government. Look closely at any note — it carries the signature of the RBI Governor and a promise: "I promise to pay the bearer the sum of …"

The rupee is **legal tender**. That means no Indian shopkeeper can legally refuse to accept it for a transaction. Currency is accepted because **the government stands behind it** — not because the paper itself is valuable.

**Deposits with banks** — the second, and today by far the larger, form of money in India. When you put your salary into a savings account, you do not stop owning it. The bank holds it for you, pays you a small interest, and lets you withdraw it whenever you want. These are called **deposits**.

Because you can demand this money back at any time, the most common type is called a **demand deposit**. Demand deposits work like money because they can be transferred easily. You can pay a shopkeeper without ever touching cash — by writing a **cheque**, by using a debit card, by scanning a UPI QR code, or by doing a net-banking transfer. In each case, money moves from your account to the shopkeeper's account without a single rupee note changing hands.

A cheque is a written instruction to your bank to transfer a specific amount from your account to another person's account. UPI and net banking achieve the same outcome — just faster and on a phone.

In modern India, **deposits make up the vast majority of money in circulation**. Currency is the visible part. Deposits are the larger, invisible part.

## Why Deposits Work — Because Banks Hold Only a Little Cash

Here is a fact that surprises many students. If every Indian walked into their bank tomorrow and demanded their entire balance in cash, the banks could not pay. Banks do not keep all your money in their vaults. They keep only a small fraction — usually around **15 per cent** — as cash to meet daily withdrawal needs. The rest is loaned out to other people.

This sounds risky, but it works because depositors **do not all withdraw at the same time**. On any normal day, some people deposit money, some withdraw, and the bank balances the two. The RBI also monitors how much cash banks must keep aside, so that ordinary withdrawals are always met.

This idea — that banks keep a small reserve and lend out the rest — is the foundation of modern banking. It is also the reason banks can earn a profit, as we will see in the next section.

## How Banks Make Money — The Interest Spread

Banks are profit-making businesses. They earn their profit from one simple gap.

Banks **pay** interest to depositors — currently about **4 to 6 per cent** per year on a savings or fixed deposit. So if you keep ten thousand rupees in a savings account for a year, you might earn about five hundred rupees in interest.

Banks **charge** interest from borrowers — currently about **8 to 12 per cent** per year for personal, business, or home loans. So a borrower who takes ten thousand rupees for a year pays about a thousand rupees in interest back to the bank.

The difference between the two — about **4 to 6 per cent** — is called the **interest spread**. This spread covers the bank's salaries, branch rents, software, fraud losses, and still leaves a profit.

Multiplied across crores of depositors and borrowers, this small spread runs the entire formal banking industry. Your savings, sitting in an account, are being put to work — helping a small business in Indore buy a delivery van, a farmer in Hoshiarpur buy seeds, a couple in Coimbatore buy their first home. This **transfer of money from those who have surplus to those who need it** is one of the most useful things banks do for the economy.

## The Reserve Bank of India — The Bank Above All Banks

Sitting above the entire banking system is the **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)**, set up in 1935 and headquartered in Mumbai. The RBI is not a normal bank. You cannot walk into the RBI and open a savings account. Its job is to run the country's money system. It performs three big roles.

**One — Issuer of currency.** All rupee notes are printed and issued by the RBI on behalf of the Government of India. The RBI decides how much currency the economy needs, and replaces old or torn notes. The "I promise to pay" line on every note is signed by the RBI Governor.

**Two — Banker to banks.** Just as you have an account with State Bank or HDFC, every commercial bank in India has an account with the RBI. Banks borrow from the RBI when they are short of cash and park surplus funds there when they have extra. The RBI also decides the **repo rate** — the rate at which it lends to banks — and this rate ultimately decides the interest you pay on your home loan.

**Three — Supervisor of commercial banks.** The RBI watches the banking system closely. It decides how much **cash reserve** every bank must keep, makes sure banks do not give loans only to rich industrialists, checks how much of their lending goes to farmers and small businesses, and steps in if a bank is in trouble. When a bank fails or behaves badly, it is the RBI that intervenes.

Beyond these three, the RBI also manages India's **foreign exchange reserves** (the dollars, euros, and gold that India holds) and acts as the **banker to the Government of India**. Every important number in the Indian money system — the lending rate, the inflation target, the cash reserve ratio — passes through the RBI.

## Two Channels of Credit — Formal and Informal

When an Indian family needs to borrow money, where does it go?

The answer depends almost entirely on whether the family is rich or poor. There are two **channels of credit** in India, and they work very differently.

The **formal sector** includes banks and cooperatives. Their rates are regulated by the RBI. They demand proper paperwork — identity proof, address proof, income proof, often a guarantor. They ask for **collateral** — something of value (land, gold, a fixed deposit) that the borrower pledges so that if the loan is not repaid, the lender can recover the money. They keep proper records of how much was borrowed, at what rate, and when it must be returned.

The **informal sector** includes moneylenders, traders, employers, landlords, friends, and relatives. They are not regulated by anyone. They lend on trust, on muscle, on relationships. They keep informal records — sometimes scribbled in a notebook, sometimes only in memory. They lend quickly, but at any rate they choose.

In India, the formal sector covers only **about half** of total credit. The other half still comes from the informal sector — and almost all of that informal credit is taken by **poor and rural households**.

## The Cost of Credit — Four Things Every Loan Has

Every loan, formal or informal, has **four terms** that decide what the loan really costs the borrower.

**One — Interest rate.** The most visible cost. A bank home loan today is around 8–10 per cent per year. A moneylender's loan in a village can be anywhere from **36 to 60 per cent per year**, sometimes higher. On a five thousand rupee loan from a moneylender, the interest alone can be three thousand rupees in a single year.

**Two — Collateral.** What the borrower pledges as security. Banks insist on it for most loans. Moneylenders may ask for the borrower's land documents, gold, or even crops as collateral. If the loan is not repaid, the collateral is taken away. Many small farmers in India have lost their land because of unpaid informal loans.

**Three — Documentation.** Banks require lots of paperwork — ID, address, income statements, salary slips, bank statements, guarantors. For a daily wage labourer with no salary slip and no permanent address, this is a wall they cannot climb. The moneylender, in contrast, asks for almost no paperwork.

**Four — Mode of repayment.** When and how the loan is to be returned. A bank loan is repaid in fixed monthly instalments (EMIs). A moneylender's loan may have to be repaid all at once at harvest time, or daily, or whenever the lender demands. Some moneylenders insist that the borrower also sells his entire harvest to the lender at a low price — adding a hidden cost on top of the interest.

A loan is **cheap** when all four terms are favourable — low interest, reasonable collateral, simple paperwork, fair repayment terms. A loan is **expensive** when any of these are stacked against the borrower.

## The Poor Pay More — India's Credit Problem

A small farmer in Bihar needs three thousand rupees to buy seeds before sowing. He goes to the village moneylender, who lends at **5 per cent per month** — that is **60 per cent per year**. The farmer signs a piece of paper. The lender takes the land documents as security.

In a good year, the farmer harvests, sells the crop, and repays. He has just paid sixty rupees in interest for every hundred rupees he borrowed — a punishing cost. In a bad year, the harvest fails. He cannot repay. The interest keeps mounting — three thousand rupees becomes four, then five. The land is taken away. The family slides into poverty.

This is called a **debt trap** — when interest grows faster than the family's income, and the loan can never be fully repaid. Across rural India, millions of small farmers and labourers are caught in some version of this trap.

Meanwhile, in the same village, the large landlord who owns thirty acres can walk into a State Bank branch and borrow three lakh rupees at **9 per cent per year**. He uses the cheap loan to buy a new tractor, irrigate more land, earn more profit — and grow richer.

The poor pay six times more for credit than the rich. The rich use credit to grow richer. The poor use credit to survive — and sometimes lose even what little they had. This is one of the most stubborn forms of inequality in the Indian economy.

The reason behind this gap is simple. Banks do not lend to the poor easily, because the poor cannot produce salary slips or collateral. The moneylender will lend without paperwork — but at a price that turns survival into slavery.

This is why **expanding formal credit to poor households** is one of the most important goals of Indian economic policy. Every farmer who can borrow from a cooperative or a bank instead of a moneylender saves enormous amounts of interest, keeps their land, and gets a real chance to climb out of poverty.

## Self-Help Groups — A Quiet Revolution in Indian Villages

Since the 1990s, a clever new model has spread across rural India to bridge this gap — the **Self-Help Group**, or **SHG**.

An SHG is a small group, usually of **fifteen to twenty women** from the same neighbourhood. They meet once a week or once a month. Each woman puts a small fixed amount of money — say twenty-five or fifty rupees — into a common pool. Over months and years, this pool grows.

The group then lends this pooled money to its own members at a fair interest rate, for any purpose the member needs — buying seeds, buying a sewing machine, repairing a hut, paying for a child's school books, dealing with an emergency. Decisions are taken collectively. The group itself watches over repayment, because every woman in the group has a stake in the money.

After about a year or two of regular saving and lending, an SHG can also approach a bank for a **group loan**. The bank lends to the SHG as a whole (not to any single woman), at the regular bank rate. The group then onlends to individual members. The bank has just one customer to track — the group — and the group has the local knowledge to lend safely.

This model is now backed at the national level by the **National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development (NABARD)**, set up in 1982. NABARD provides the framework, training, and refinance that allows banks to lend confidently to SHGs.

The achievement is remarkable. As of the mid-2020s, **more than a crore SHGs** are active in India, involving **over twelve crore women**. Lakhs of crores of rupees have flowed through this channel into the hands of poor rural women. Repayment rates are higher than for many commercial loans. Many SHG members have started small businesses, sent daughters to school, and gained social confidence they did not have before.

The SHG is not just a credit scheme. It is a **social institution** — women who once had no voice now meet weekly, manage money, take decisions, and speak in public. The economic effect is real; the social effect is even bigger.

## The Story of SEWA Bank — A Bank Built by Working Women

The single best-known story of women's banking in India comes from Ahmedabad.

In 1972, a young Gandhian lawyer named **Ela Bhatt** helped found the **Self-Employed Women's Association**, or **SEWA** — a trade union of vegetable vendors, ragpickers, embroidery workers, and other women who worked in the informal economy. These women earned daily, often had no savings, and were trapped by moneylenders who lent them seed capital every morning at extortionate rates.

The women told Bhatt: "We don't need charity. We need a bank that will treat us with dignity."

In **1974**, **SEWA Bank** was registered as a cooperative bank, with the founding share capital raised from the savings of four thousand women — most of whom could not read or write, and signed their names with a thumbprint. It was perhaps the first bank in the world owned, used, and run by **poor self-employed women**.

SEWA Bank offers savings accounts, small loans for tools and stock, loans for housing repair, and basic insurance — all designed for women who earn in cash, daily, and often have no paperwork. Loans are given without collateral, on the basis of group trust and local knowledge.

The lessons of SEWA Bank shaped the wider Indian SHG movement and inspired similar work across Asia and Africa. In 2007, Ela Bhatt received the Padma Bhushan; in 2011, the Niwano Peace Prize. When you read about Indian microfinance today, the road begins in Ahmedabad in 1974.

## When Microfinance Went Wrong — The 2010 Crisis

The SHG model and the SEWA model both work because they keep interest rates fair and repayment voluntary. But not every "microfinance" scheme has been so careful.

In the years before 2010, private companies called **Microfinance Institutions (MFIs)** grew very fast in Andhra Pradesh and other states. They borrowed cheaply from large banks and onlent at high rates — sometimes **24 to 36 per cent per year** — to poor women, often without checking whether the same woman had already borrowed from another MFI. Loan officers, paid by commission, pushed women to borrow more than they could repay. When repayments failed, agents used aggressive tactics, including public shaming and threats.

In late 2010, **dozens of poor women in Andhra Pradesh took their own lives** under the pressure of unpayable MFI loans. The state government stepped in with an emergency ordinance restricting MFI activity. Repayments collapsed. The industry shrank overnight.

The post-mortem was sobering. **Lending to the poor is not automatically good.** Without strong checks — on interest rates, on multiple borrowing, on collection tactics — even "micro-credit" can become a new form of the old moneylender trap, just dressed in modern clothes.

After 2010, the RBI brought MFIs under tighter regulation. Interest rates were capped, borrower protections were strengthened, and the worst practices stopped. But the warning remains: **expanding credit is not the same as reducing poverty**, unless the credit is fair, transparent, and matched to what borrowers can repay.

## Formal Credit Reform — Jan Dhan and MUDRA

In the last decade, India has tried two big new pushes to widen formal credit.

**Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY)**, launched in **August 2014**, set out to give every Indian household a basic bank account. The account could be opened with minimal paperwork and zero balance, and came bundled with a debit card and small insurance cover. Within a few years, over **forty crore Jan Dhan accounts** had been opened — many for women, many in households that had never seen the inside of a bank before. Subsidies for cooking gas, MGNREGA wages, and pandemic relief were transferred directly into these accounts, cutting out middlemen.

**Pradhan Mantri MUDRA Yojana**, launched in **April 2015**, focused on small business credit. Through MUDRA loans, vegetable vendors, beauticians, mechanics, small workshop owners, and tailors can borrow up to ten lakh rupees from banks — without classical collateral, with simple paperwork. Crores of MUDRA loans have been disbursed, with a large share going to women borrowers.

Together, these schemes — alongside Aadhaar identity, UPI digital payments, and the SHG network — have started to bring formal banking to people the old system had ignored. The gap between rich and poor credit is still very real, but it is no longer as wide as it was a generation ago.

## What This Chapter Asks You To Carry Forward

Five ideas are worth carrying with you out of this chapter.

**One — money is a public agreement, not a magic substance.** Rupee notes are valuable because Indians trust the RBI and the government that stands behind them. Lose the trust and the notes become paper. Every currency system in history rests on this fragile thing called trust.

**Two — modern money is mostly invisible.** The bigger part of money in India sits in bank accounts and moves through cheques, debit cards, and UPI. You can run a small business today and almost never touch a five-hundred rupee note.

**Three — credit is two-sided.** It can lift a small business into prosperity or push a poor family into a debt trap. The same word, "loan", can mean very different things at 9 per cent and at 60 per cent.

**Four — formal credit is one of the great equalisers.** When the poor can borrow from banks, cooperatives, and SHGs instead of moneylenders, they keep more of what they earn, hold on to their land, and have a real chance to rise.

**Five — institutions matter.** The RBI, NABARD, SEWA Bank, the SHG movement, the Jan Dhan account, the MUDRA loan — these are not abstract things. They are the scaffolding that decides who in India can borrow, at what cost, and with what dignity. Building and improving them is some of the most important work the country is doing.

When you next pay for a samosa with UPI, look at your screen for a moment. Behind that two-second transfer is a hundred years of monetary history — and a giant, mostly silent system that, when it works well, holds the Indian economy together.

### Three Short Stories About Money and Credit

**Story 1 — Daadi's No-Bank-Account Generation.** Your grandmother was born in a village in the 1950s. Through her whole working life, she never opened a bank account. Her savings went into three places — a small steel box behind the rice tin, gold earrings she wore every day, and a hidden cloth pouch tied to her sari. When her son needed money for college, she sold one earring. When her daughter married, she sold another. Her money worked, but it was vulnerable to theft, fire, and the temptation of relatives. In 2017, at age 67, she opened her first Jan Dhan account. She still keeps the steel box — old habits die hard — but now, for the first time, her pension comes directly into her own name.

**Story 2 — The Vegetable Vendor's Daily Loan.** Every morning at 5 a.m., Razia pushes her cart to the Crawford Market wholesale yard in Mumbai. She buys vegetables worth one thousand rupees. She doesn't have one thousand rupees. The wholesaler lends it to her each morning at "ten per cent" — meaning she repays eleven hundred by evening. By the end of a long day on the street, she has earned maybe two hundred rupees of real profit. Her annual interest rate, calculated honestly, is over 3,000 per cent. A single bad rainy day wipes out her savings. A bank loan at 12 per cent would change her life — but she cannot get one without paperwork. Razia is exactly the customer that MUDRA loans, SHGs, and Jan Dhan were designed for.

**Story 3 — The SEWA Women's Bank.** In a side lane in old Ahmedabad, on the upper floor of a faded yellow building, sits a bank counter that looks like any other — except every customer is a woman, and most of them work with their hands. A vegetable seller comes to deposit forty rupees. An embroidery worker comes to repay a loan of three thousand rupees she had taken to buy thread. A widow comes to withdraw her insurance claim. The bank was started in 1974 by Ela Bhatt and four thousand women who pooled their savings. Fifty years later, it has lent more money to poor women than many large commercial banks — and barely a rupee has gone bad. The lesson it teaches is simple. Trust the poor with credit, on fair terms, and they will repay.

### DIY Activity: Where Does Money Live in Your Home?

This week, do three small investigations.

**One — The Family Survey.** Ask five people you know — parents, grandparents, neighbours, the building watchman, a shopkeeper — the same question: "**Where do you keep your savings?**" Likely answers include: in a bank account, in a post office account, in gold or jewellery, in a fixed deposit, in cash at home, in property, in a chit fund, in an SHG. Make a small table with the names (or initials) and the savings places. Note which generation each person belongs to. You will see a pattern.

**Two — Your Own Money Flow.** For one week, write down every rupee that comes to you (pocket money, gifts, refunds) and every rupee that goes out (snacks, bus fare, recharge, books). At the end of the week, add up both columns. The difference is your weekly **net flow**. If positive, you saved. If negative, you borrowed (probably from a parent). Notice how easily money disappears in small amounts.

**Three — Visit an SHG or a Bank Branch.** If you live in a town or village where SHGs meet, ask if you can sit in on one weekly meeting (most welcome curious visitors). Notice the register, the cash box, the discussion. If SHGs are not near you, visit a bank branch and ask the manager what kind of loans are most common there. Most managers are happy to talk to a school student.

### A Question to Keep Asking

Indian banking has spread further in the last ten years than in the previous fifty. UPI, Jan Dhan, MUDRA, and SHGs have brought tens of crores of new customers into the formal system. But moneylenders have not disappeared. Razia in Mumbai still borrows from the wholesaler at 3,000 per cent.

The question for your generation is this — **how do we make formal credit reach every Indian, in time, on terms that respect them?** When you next read about a banking scheme, a UPI failure, or a microfinance crisis, ask yourself: would this help Razia, or harm her? The answer to that small question is the answer to one of India's biggest problems.
