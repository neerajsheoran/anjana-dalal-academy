**Child:** Maa, when I was little, you said our country can't make all these phones. Now suddenly we make iPhones in India. What happened?

**Mother:** Beta, you've noticed something important. That sentence — "our country can't make all these phones" — was the entire Indian story for forty years. And now in your lifetime, it has turned upside down. Sit, let's go through it slowly. This is one of the biggest changes any Indian generation has lived through, and you should understand it not just for marks but for life.

## Why Did India Open Its Economy in 1991?

**Child:** What was India like before 1991? You and Papa keep saying things changed after that year.

**Mother:** Picture an India that decided, after Independence, to make almost everything for itself. The reasoning was honest. We had just been colonised by Britain for 200 years, our handloom weavers had been destroyed by cheap Manchester cloth, our raw materials had been carted off to feed British factories. Nehru and his planners said: never again. We will build our own steel, our own cars, our own everything. We will not depend on the West.

**Child:** That sounds good though.

**Mother:** It sounded very good. And for some things it worked. We built our own steel plants, our own dams, our own space programme eventually. But there was a hidden cost. **When you protect Indian companies from foreign competition for too long, they stop trying to improve.** The Ambassador car of 1985 looked like the Ambassador of 1960. A scooter took a five-year waiting list. To install a telephone took up to 7–10 years. Your dadaji used to say — "We have applied for a phone connection; God willing, your generation will get it."

**Child:** Seriously seven years for a telephone?

**Mother:** Seriously. And the licence-permit raj made it worse. To start a factory, expand a factory, import a machine — you needed permission from Delhi. The permissions came slowly, and often only if you "knew" the right official. Industry was lazy and corrupt. The few who could afford it sent their relatives abroad to bring back things — a Yardley powder, a Sony Walkman, a Phillips iron. Anything foreign was special.

**Child:** So what changed in 1991?

**Mother:** A crisis changed it. In 1990–91, Iraq invaded Kuwait. Oil prices shot up. Lakhs of Indians working in the Gulf had to be evacuated, and the money they used to send home — the remittances — dried up. Suddenly India did not have enough foreign exchange to pay for the oil and food we had to import. **Our reserves fell to a level where we had about two weeks of imports left.** That's all. Two weeks.

**Child:** What did they do?

**Mother:** India literally airlifted gold to London — a few tonnes of it — as security to get an emergency loan from the IMF. That's how serious it was. A new government had just come in, led by Prime Minister **P.V. Narasimha Rao**. His Finance Minister was a quiet, soft-spoken economist named **Dr Manmohan Singh** — the same person who later became Prime Minister for ten years.

**Child:** And they changed things?

**Mother:** They had to. They cut tariffs, dismantled the licence raj for most industries, opened up Foreign Direct Investment, devalued the rupee, and told the world: "India is open for business." Beta, this is the most important economic moment in modern India. Without 1991, the iPhone in your hand, the Maruti car outside, the IT job your cousin has — none of it would exist. **A crisis, in other words, forced the change that should have happened much earlier.**

## What Does an MNC Actually Do?

**Child:** Everyone keeps saying "MNC, MNC". What does an MNC actually do that a normal company doesn't?

**Mother:** Good question. A normal company makes things in one country and sells them. A multinational corporation — MNC — does something more interesting. **It looks at the world like a giant jigsaw puzzle and places each piece of its work wherever it gets the best deal.**

**Child:** Give me an example.

**Mother:** Take your iPhone. Apple is an American company. But it designs the iPhone in California, gets the chips made in Taiwan, the screens in South Korea, the camera modules in Japan, and assembles all of it now in factories in China and, increasingly, in Tamil Nadu near Chennai. The marketing happens through ads made all over the world. The accounting often runs through Ireland for tax reasons. **One product. Many countries. Each step where it makes the most economic sense.**

**Child:** Why does Apple do all this jumping around? Why not just make it in America?

**Mother:** Because making it in America would mean paying American wages — maybe twenty times what an Indian assembly worker earns. The iPhone would cost two lakh rupees instead of eighty thousand. People would not buy as many. So Apple does what every MNC does — **moves the work to where it can be done well but cheaply**.

**Child:** And India fits in where?

**Mother:** India fits in two famous slots. **Software services and BPO** — call centres, code writing, accounting, customer support. A graduate from Coimbatore can sit in Bengaluru, talk to an American customer at 9 p.m. local time, and earn three times what her parents earned. The same work in America would cost the company five times as much. **Both sides win — though not equally.**

**Child:** And iPhone making?

**Mother:** That's newer. The government created subsidies — Production-Linked Incentives — to encourage companies to make phones in India. Foxconn, which assembles iPhones for Apple, now has huge plants in Tamil Nadu. By 2024 India was the second-largest mobile phone maker in the world, after China. That sentence would have sounded like science fiction in 2010.

**Child:** Are MNCs good for India then?

**Mother:** Mixed. They bring money, technology, jobs, and discipline — Indian workers in MNC factories learn new methods, then take those skills home. But they also take large profits back to their home countries, and they can squeeze their Indian suppliers very hard. **An MNC's loyalty is to its shareholders, not to India.** That's why government policy matters — to make sure India gets a fair share.

## Who Wins and Who Loses from Globalisation?

**Child:** You keep saying "winners and losers". Tell me who exactly?

**Mother:** Sit, this is the most important part of the chapter. Globalisation is not magic; it does not lift everyone equally. Some people have done extremely well, some are just okay, and some have been crushed. An honest student must hold all three pictures.

**Child:** Tell me the winners first.

**Mother:** **The first big winners are educated, urban, English-speaking Indians.** The IT industry employs over 5 million people directly. Whole cities — Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Gurugram, Pune, Chennai — have been transformed by it. Families that were lower-middle-class in 1990 now have flats, cars, and children studying abroad. The second winners are **all of us, as consumers.** Your phone is better, your two-wheeler is better, your TV is better, your data is dirt cheap thanks to Jio's 2016 launch. Even small-town India has access to goods unimaginable in 1990.

**Child:** And the big companies?

**Mother:** A third winner group: **large Indian companies that adapted and became MNCs themselves**. Tata bought Corus, the British steel giant, in 2007. Tata also bought Jaguar Land Rover. Sun Pharma is among the world's largest generic drug makers. Infosys and TCS are huge global IT firms. Reliance Jio has changed global telecom thinking. **Indians are no longer just employees of foreign MNCs — Indian MNCs hire foreigners now.** That is a quiet revolution.

**Child:** And the diaspora?

**Mother:** Yes — fourth winners. Indians working abroad sent home **over $125 billion in 2024 in remittances**, the highest in the world. Whole districts in Kerala, Punjab, parts of Tamil Nadu live on these flows. Your aunt's family in the Gulf is one example.

**Child:** Now the losers.

**Mother:** Several groups, beta. **Small producers crushed by cheap Chinese imports.** When China joined the WTO in 2001 and cheap goods flooded India, thousands of small workshops — toy makers, plastic moulders, basic electronics assemblers — simply closed. They could not compete on price. **Farmers exposed to global price swings.** When edible-oil tariffs were cut, Indian groundnut and mustard farmers struggled against subsidised American soy and Malaysian palm oil. When global cotton prices crashed, Vidarbha farmers — already in debt — were pushed into a tragic crisis. **Informal workers.** Workers in tiny factories supplying MNC brands often work brutal hours at low wages because the brand has set a ruthless target price. A garment worker in Tirupur stitching shirts for Zara is part of globalisation — but she is at its hardest, most invisible end.

**Child:** Traditional artisans?

**Mother:** Yes — power looms have crushed handloom weavers; cheap plastic toys have killed the wood-and-clay craftsmen. The romantic image of "village industries" survives in handicraft shops more than in actual villages. So when someone tells you globalisation is purely good or purely bad, push back. **It is a powerful force that has lifted huge numbers up and pushed some others down. Smart policy widens the winners and cushions the losers. India has done some of both, with mixed results.**

## Why Are International Trade Rules So Often Unfair to India?

**Child:** What is the WTO? My teacher mentioned it but I didn't follow.

**Mother:** The **World Trade Organization** is a body set up in 1995, with about 165 member countries today. Its job is to set rules for international trade — what tariffs are allowed, what subsidies are allowed, how disputes between countries are settled. The basic idea is good: fewer barriers, freer trade, more growth for everyone.

**Child:** Sounds fair. Each country, one vote?

**Mother:** Officially yes. In practice, **the rules are written more for rich countries than for poor ones**. Here is the example that has angered Indian negotiators for two decades. The United States pays its farmers about $40 billion a year in subsidies. The European Union pays even more. So when American cotton or European dairy reaches India, it can be sold below the actual cost of producing it, because the American government has already paid part of the cost.

**Child:** But India also gives subsidies — fertiliser, MSP for wheat?

**Mother:** Yes, and that's exactly the irony. **India's subsidies are much smaller in dollar terms, but the WTO frequently challenges them as "trade distorting".** The American farmer earning $5,000 a month gets to keep his subsidy. The Indian farmer earning ₹5,000 a month is told his subsidy is too generous. That is not a level playing field; that is a tilted one.

**Child:** Why does India keep saying yes to the WTO then?

**Mother:** Because India needs the rules-based system. Without the WTO, big countries could bully small ones even more openly. **The right strategy is to stay in the WTO but fight to make its rules fairer.** India has often led developing countries in pushing back — sometimes blocking trade deals it considered unfair. The Doha Round of WTO talks has been running since 2001 partly because India and other developing countries refuse to sign deals that protect rich farmers while opening poor markets.

**Child:** So the WTO is not the villain, but it isn't fully fair either.

**Mother:** Exactly. Hold both ideas. Rules-based trade is better than a jungle. But the rules can be improved, and that fight is ongoing.

## How Did Bengaluru Become an IT City?

**Child:** Why Bengaluru? Why not Mumbai or Delhi?

**Mother:** Beautiful question. Bengaluru becoming the IT capital was partly luck and partly planning. Three threads came together.

**Child:** Tell me.

**Mother:** **One — the engineering colleges.** Bengaluru and Karnataka had a strong tradition of technical education going back decades. The Indian Institute of Science was set up in Bengaluru in 1909, courtesy of Jamsetji Tata and the Maharaja of Mysore. By the 1980s, Karnataka had dozens of engineering colleges churning out tens of thousands of graduates. **The raw material — talented engineers — was already there.**

**Child:** Second thread?

**Mother:** **Public-sector tech.** Bengaluru hosted Hindustan Aeronautics Limited, Indian Space Research Organisation, Bharat Electronics Limited, and others. Generations of engineers worked there, and many of their children grew up in an engineering culture. The city had a mind for technology long before software became a global business.

**Child:** Third?

**Mother:** **A few risk-takers who placed early bets.** Infosys started in 1981 with a small capital. Wipro pivoted from cooking oil to computers. Texas Instruments became the first major MNC to set up an R&D centre in Bengaluru in 1985. Once these existed, **a snowball effect kicked in** — more engineers stayed, more companies came because engineers were available, more colleges opened because companies were hiring, and so on. By the late 1990s, when Y2K work flooded into India, Bengaluru was the obvious place to handle it. By 2010, it had become "the Silicon Valley of India".

**Child:** And the city today?

**Mother:** Glass towers, traffic that doesn't move, rents that crush middle-class families, weather still pleasant, and a generation of young Indians earning what their parents could not have imagined. Bengaluru is the **physical face of globalisation in India** — the bright side and the chaos side, both visible from the same flyover.

## What Should I Take From This?

**Child:** What should I take from all this for my own life?

**Mother:** A few things, beta. Hold them carefully — they will matter for the next sixty years of your life.

**Child:** Tell me.

**Mother:** **One — globalisation is not going away. It is the world you have inherited.** The phone, the food, the music, the jobs — all of these are products of a globally connected India. Don't argue with this reality; understand it. **Two — your skills matter more than ever.** In a globalised world, an Indian who can do high-quality work — whether coding, surgery, writing, design, manufacturing — can command real income from anywhere in the world. An Indian without skills will be squeezed by global competition. **Education is your passport.**

**Child:** Three?

**Mother:** **Three — keep your eyes open to who is winning and who is losing.** When you see a fancy IT couple in Bengaluru living well, also see the migrant worker building their flat. When you buy a cheap T-shirt online, ask which factory in Tirupur stitched it and at what pay. The honest Indian citizen holds both pictures.

**Child:** Four?

**Mother:** **Four — Indian companies and Indian ideas can lead globally.** UPI, Aadhaar, ONDC, Reliance Jio's data revolution — these are Indian inventions the world is now studying. Don't grow up thinking "the West is always ahead". On many fronts, India is ahead. You can be part of that.

**Child:** Five?

**Mother:** **Five — and most important — democracy decides how the gains are shared.** Globalisation by itself is a force; it does not have a conscience. Whether the gains reach the small farmer in Vidarbha or the garment worker in Tirupur depends on government policy, on smart regulation, on public investment in education and health, on the political pressure citizens like you put on government. **In a democracy, you have a vote and a voice. Use them.**

**Child:** That's a lot to carry, Maa.

**Mother:** It is, beta. But you have time. Read carefully, think honestly, work seriously, vote thoughtfully. And the next time you walk into a shop or a mall or even our own kitchen, keep asking one small question: **"Who made this, where, and what kind of life are they living because I bought it?"** That single question is the moral heart of globalisation. It is what turns an economics chapter into a way of seeing the world.

**Child:** Okay Maa. I'll remember.

**Mother:** Good. Now finish your homework. Globalisation can wait fifteen minutes; your school cannot.
