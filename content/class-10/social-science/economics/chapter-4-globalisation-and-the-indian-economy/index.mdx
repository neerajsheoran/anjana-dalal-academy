## What This Chapter Is Really About

If you walk into a mid-sized Indian home today and look around carefully, you will find pieces of the whole world. The phone in your hand was probably designed in California and assembled near Sriperumbudur in Tamil Nadu, with chips from Taiwan and a screen from South Korea. The television runs an OS made in Seoul or Mountain View. The instant noodles on the kitchen shelf are made by a Swiss company. The shampoo bottle says "Hindustan Unilever" but the parent company sits in London and Rotterdam. The auto-rickshaw outside burns Russian crude oil refined in Jamnagar. The boy next door takes a call at 2 a.m. for an American insurance customer he will never meet.

None of this would have been possible — or even imaginable — for your grandparents in 1980.

This chapter asks a simple question with a large answer: **how did India go from a country that made almost everything for itself, badly and slowly, to a country that is wired into the global economy through every cable, container, and call centre?** The technical name for this change is **globalisation**, and we will unpack it carefully — its meaning, its causes, its winners, its losers, and the place of India in it.

## What Globalisation Actually Means

A textbook definition: **globalisation is the rapid integration of countries' economies through the freer movement of goods, services, capital, technology, ideas, and (sometimes) people across borders**.

Notice what that sentence quietly assumes. It assumes there were once strong walls between countries — high taxes on imports, restrictions on foreign investment, limits on currency conversion, controls on which Indian could travel where. Globalisation is the steady knocking-down of those walls. Some walls have come down completely. Some are lower but still standing. A few have been rebuilt in recent years. The story is not finished; the walls move every decade.

There is, however, one important catch. **Globalisation is not the same as trade.** Trade between countries is at least 5,000 years old — Indus Valley merchants sold beads to Mesopotamia in 3000 BCE. What is new about modern globalisation is two things:

1. **Production itself crosses borders.** A single shirt is now stitched in Bangladesh from Chinese cloth made of American cotton, designed in Italy and sold in a shop in Bengaluru. No single country "makes" the shirt. That kind of split-up production was rare even in 1980.

2. **Speed and scale.** A factory in Vietnam can change tomorrow's order because a buyer in Berlin clicked a button at midnight. Container ships, fibre-optic cables, satellite communications, and cheap aviation have collapsed both distance and time.

Both these features depend on something even bigger: **multinational corporations**.

## Multinational Corporations — The Engine of Modern Globalisation

A **multinational corporation (MNC)** is a company that owns or controls production in more than one country. Coca-Cola is American but bottles in 200 countries. Samsung is South Korean but makes phones in Noida. Tata Motors is Indian but owns Jaguar Land Rover in Britain. Reliance is Indian but partners with BP and Saudi Aramco.

What MNCs actually do is more interesting than the definition suggests. **An MNC chooses where to put each piece of its production based on where it is cheapest, fastest, or most useful.** Think of a global jigsaw. The corporate headquarters and brand may sit in one country. The high-tech research will happen wherever the best engineers live. The labour-intensive assembly will go wherever wages are low but workers are skilled enough. The back-office paperwork (billing, customer support, accounting) will go wherever English-speaking graduates are cheap.

India fits into this jigsaw in two famous slots. **Software services** (writing code, maintaining IT systems for foreign clients) flow into Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune, Gurugram, and Chennai. **Business process outsourcing (BPO)** — call centres, payroll processing, medical transcription, animation — flow into smaller cities too, from Noida to Kochi. A 22-year-old graduate from Coimbatore can sit in a glass tower at 9 p.m. local time, answer an American customer's broadband complaint, and earn three times what her parents earned at her age.

The cost gap is what makes this happen. An Indian software engineer earned, in 2024, perhaps 7–12 lakh rupees a year for work that would cost 60–90 lakh rupees per year in San Francisco. To the company, that is a saving of crores per project. To the engineer, it is a middle-class life her parents could not have dreamt of. **Both sides "win" — though, as we will see, not equally.**

MNCs do not only outsource work. They also invest directly. When Suzuki entered India in the early 1980s in a joint venture that became Maruti Suzuki, Suzuki put up money, brought Japanese engineers, and trained Indian workers. The factory in Manesar belongs to a Japanese-controlled company but employs Indians, pays Indian taxes, and serves Indian buyers. This is called **foreign direct investment (FDI)** — investment by a foreign company in setting up factories or buying control of Indian firms. FDI is one of the loudest streams of globalisation.

## How MNCs Spread — Four Common Patterns

To understand globalisation, it helps to recognise the four ways an MNC enters a foreign market.

**(1) Buy an existing local firm.** Cadbury was bought by Mondelez (an American company); Corus (a British steelmaker) was bought by Tata Steel. The factories don't change overnight, but the owner — and often the brand — does.

**(2) Set up production jointly with a local company.** Maruti-Suzuki, Bharti-Walmart (briefly), Hero-Honda (now split). Each side brings something: the foreign side brings money and technology, the local side brings market knowledge and political contacts.

**(3) Place orders with local producers.** Walmart and Zara don't own clothing factories in Tirupur — they place huge orders with Indian manufacturers, who then push their workers to deliver on tight deadlines at tight prices. Walmart sets the design; the Indian factory takes the risk.

**(4) Build new factories from scratch.** Foxconn's iPhone assembly plants in Sriperumbudur and Devanahalli, Samsung's largest phone factory at Noida, Apple's expanding India footprint — these are greenfield investments where MNCs set up entirely new facilities.

In every case the MNC is shaping production decisions inside India, even when the workers and the buildings are Indian.

## Foreign Trade and the Integration of Markets

When goods flow across borders cheaply and reliably, something deeper happens than just buying and selling — **markets get connected**. A drought in Spain pushes up olive oil prices in Bengaluru. A war in Ukraine pushes up the price of cooking gas in Bhopal. A bumper apple crop in Washington State means Kashmiri apple farmers get less for their fruit at the Azadpur mandi in Delhi.

This is what economists mean by **integration of markets**. Once you can move a product across borders cheaply, the price of that product in different countries tends to come closer together. There is one global price (roughly), and local prices follow it.

For consumers, this is mostly good. You get **more variety, better quality, and often lower prices** than your parents had. Look at the bicycle market in 1985 versus today, or televisions, or two-wheelers, or smartphones. Almost everything you can buy is better and cheaper in real terms than it was forty years ago.

For producers, the picture is more complicated. **Indian companies that can compete benefit hugely** — Tata Motors selling cars in 100 countries, Bajaj selling two-wheelers in Africa, Infosys earning dollars from American banks, Sun Pharma selling generic medicines to the world. But **Indian companies that cannot compete are squeezed or driven out** — small-scale toy makers shut by cheap Chinese imports, traditional weavers undercut by power-loom factories, edible oil farmers forced to compete with subsidised American soy.

In Class 9 and earlier, you may have read that markets always "balance" themselves. The reality is messier. Markets do equalise — but they do so painfully, and the pain is not shared equally.

## The Three Things That Made Modern Globalisation Possible

Why did globalisation accelerate so dramatically after about 1980? Three forces came together at roughly the same time.

### Technology

The **shipping container** sounds boring — it is a steel box about 20 or 40 feet long. But it transformed world trade. Before containers (introduced in the 1950s, widespread by the 1970s), each crate had to be loaded by hand onto a ship; theft was rampant; loading took weeks; shipping was expensive. After containers, loading takes hours, theft is rare, costs collapsed. A pair of jeans can be shipped from Bangladesh to Mumbai for a few rupees.

Then came **computing and the internet**. By 1995 a designer in New York could send a complete clothing design — pattern, fabric specs, colours — by email to Tirupur in the time it takes to make a cup of chai. By 2010, video conferencing meant an Indian engineer could attend a daily meeting with a Boston team without leaving Bengaluru.

Then came **mobile telephony and cheap data**. When **Reliance Jio** launched in 2016 with free data and near-free voice, the cost of being online for an Indian collapsed almost overnight. By 2024, India had over 800 million internet users, most of them on smartphones. Suddenly a farmer in Bilaspur could check today's mandi prices, a tailor in Murshidabad could take orders by WhatsApp, and a college student in Aurangabad could learn coding from a free YouTube course in Hindi.

Technology, in other words, did not just make globalisation possible. It made it cheap enough to reach the village.

### Liberalisation of Trade — India's 1991 Turn

Until 1991, India followed a model called **import substitution**. The idea, championed by Nehru and his planners, was straightforward: a poor country should make its own goods rather than buy them from rich countries. So India put high taxes (called **tariffs**) on imports, refused permission for many foreign companies, and required licences for almost everything (the famous **"licence-permit raj"**). The Indian rupee was not freely convertible. You could not just buy a foreign machine; you had to apply, wait, and prove your "need".

This protected Indian industry. But over decades, the **protection turned into laziness**. Indian companies did not have to compete with the world, so they did not bother to improve quality. The "Ambassador" car of the 1980s looked the same as the Ambassador of the 1960s. A telephone connection took five years to get. Even basic consumer goods — toothpaste, soap, shoes — were dull and expensive compared to what other countries had.

Then came the crisis. In 1990–91, the **Gulf War** spiked oil prices, remittances from Indians in the Gulf collapsed, and India's foreign exchange reserves fell so low that the country had **only about two weeks of imports left**. India had to physically ship gold to London as collateral for an emergency IMF loan. The country was at the edge of default.

Out of this crisis came the **1991 reforms**, designed and pushed through by Prime Minister P.V. Narasimha Rao and his Finance Minister, **Dr Manmohan Singh**. The core moves:

- **Tariffs cut sharply.** Imports became cheaper.
- **Licence raj dismantled.** Most industries no longer needed government permission to expand or set up factories.
- **FDI opened up.** Foreign companies could now invest in many sectors, often up to 51% or more.
- **Rupee devalued and partially convertible.** Foreign exchange flowed more freely.
- **Capital markets opened.** Foreign investors could buy Indian shares.

This shift is sometimes called **liberalisation** — the loosening of government controls. It is the political event without which the India of the 2020s could not exist.

### The World Trade Organization (WTO)

In 1995, an older trade body called GATT was upgraded into the **World Trade Organization** (WTO). The WTO has roughly 165 member countries today. Its job is to set rules for international trade — what tariffs are allowed, what subsidies are allowed, how disputes between countries are resolved.

The basic idea behind the WTO is liberalisation: **fewer barriers, freer trade, more growth for everybody**. Its rules push countries to lower their tariffs, reduce their farm subsidies, and treat foreign companies as well as they treat their own.

In theory the WTO is fair: every country, big or small, gets one vote. In practice — and this is one of the central tensions of modern globalisation — **the rules are written more for rich countries than for poor ones**, as we will see in the next section.

## Is It Really a Level Playing Field? The Subsidy Problem

Here is a small example that has angered Indian farmers and trade negotiators for two decades.

The United States pays its farmers **about $40 billion a year** in subsidies. The European Union pays even more. So when American cotton or European dairy reaches Indian shores, it can sell at prices below what it actually costs to produce, because the foreign government has already paid part of the bill.

Meanwhile, India pays its farmers subsidies too — on fertiliser, on power, on minimum support prices for wheat and rice. But Indian subsidies are smaller in dollar terms, and the WTO frequently challenges them, arguing they "distort trade".

The Indian negotiator's response is sharp: **the WTO will let a $5,000-a-month American farmer keep his subsidy, but tell a ₹5,000-a-month Indian farmer his is too generous.** That is not a level playing field; it is a tilted one.

The WTO has tried to negotiate fairer rules — the so-called "Doha Round" of trade talks has been running since 2001 — but progress has been painfully slow. Rich countries protect their farmers and their markets; poor countries are told to open theirs. India has often led the developing world in pushing back, sometimes blocking WTO deals that it considered unfair.

## Winners and Losers — Who Gains from Globalisation?

This is the harder, more honest section of the chapter. **Globalisation has not been kind to everyone in India.**

### Clear Winners

**(1) Skilled, English-speaking urban Indians.** The IT industry alone employs over 5 million people directly and supports tens of millions indirectly. A software engineer's family that was lower-middle-class in 1990 is comfortably middle-class today. Cities like Bengaluru, Hyderabad, and Gurugram have been transformed.

**(2) Indian consumers (almost all of them).** Variety, quality, and (often) prices have improved. A two-wheeler is more reliable, a refrigerator runs better, mobile data is dirt cheap. Even small-town and rural India have access to goods and services unimaginable in 1990.

**(3) Large Indian companies that adapted.** Tata, Reliance, Mahindra, Bharti Airtel, Sun Pharma, Bajaj, TCS, Infosys, Mahindra, Wipro — these "world-class Indian MNCs" themselves became global players. Tata bought Corus and Jaguar Land Rover. Sun Pharma is among the world's largest generic drug makers. Reliance Jio has reshaped global telecom thinking.

**(4) Migrants and remittance receivers.** Indians abroad — software engineers in California, nurses in the Gulf, construction workers in Saudi Arabia — sent home **over $125 billion in remittances in 2024**, the highest in the world. Whole districts in Kerala, Punjab, and parts of Tamil Nadu live on these flows.

### Squeezed or Losing

**(1) Workers in industries that could not compete with cheap imports.** Small-scale battery makers, plastic moulders, certain electronics assemblers, traditional toy makers — many were wiped out when Chinese goods flooded in after 2001. Tens of thousands of small workshops closed.

**(2) Farmers exposed to global prices.** When edible-oil tariffs were cut, Indian groundnut and mustard farmers struggled to compete with subsidised American soy or Malaysian palm. When cotton prices crashed globally, farmers in Vidarbha — already loaded with debt — were pushed into crisis.

**(3) Informal-sector workers.** A large part of Indian workers — perhaps 80%+ — are in informal work without contracts, leave, or benefits. As MNCs push their Indian suppliers to deliver cheaper and faster, the squeeze gets passed down to these workers. A garment worker in Tirupur or a contract worker in a Manesar auto plant often works long hours for low pay because the buyer's brand has set a ruthless price.

**(4) Traditional handicrafts and small artisans.** Power looms have crushed handloom weavers. Plastic toys have crushed wood and clay craftsmen. The romantic image of "village industries" survives more in handicraft shops than in actual villages.

### The Honest Verdict

Globalisation is **not** a simple "good" or "bad". It is a powerful force that has lifted huge numbers of Indians up and pushed some others down. The job of policy is to **maximise the gains and cushion the losses**. India has done some of both, with mixed success. A balanced honest assessment must hold both sides at the same time.

## The Indian Government's Recent Moves

Since the early 2000s — and especially in the last decade — the Indian government has tried to shape globalisation rather than simply submit to it.

**Free trade agreements (FTAs).** India has signed agreements with Japan, ASEAN, UAE, Australia, and the European Free Trade Association. Each agreement reduces tariffs between India and the partner country, opening more markets to Indian exporters and more imports to Indian consumers.

**Make in India (2014 onwards).** A push to make India a manufacturing hub. The idea is that India's growth so far has been driven mostly by services (IT, finance); to create enough jobs for its young population, India needs to manufacture too. Some sectors — mobile phones, defence electronics, semiconductors, electric vehicles — have seen real progress. Mobile phones, in particular, are now made in India in such volume that India is the world's second-largest mobile phone maker after China.

**Production-Linked Incentives (PLI).** The government pays companies (Indian and foreign) cash incentives if they make certain products inside India in large volume. Foxconn, Tata Electronics, Samsung, and Pegatron have built or expanded factories partly because of this.

**Strategic caution on Chinese imports and apps.** After border tensions in 2020, India banned several Chinese apps and tightened scrutiny of Chinese FDI. This is a reminder that globalisation is not only economics — it interacts with geopolitics, security, and national pride.

**Boost to digital infrastructure.** Aadhaar, UPI, ONDC (the open digital commerce network) — these are Indian-built systems that other countries are now copying. They show that globalisation can flow outward from India, not just inward.

The direction of policy is clear: India wants to be part of the global economy, but on terms that grow Indian jobs, Indian companies, and Indian influence.

## What This Chapter Asks You to Carry

If you remember nothing else from this chapter, hold on to four ideas.

**(1) Globalisation is the rapid integration of economies — through goods, services, capital, technology, and (sometimes) people.** Markets that were once separate are now linked.

**(2) The engine of modern globalisation is the multinational corporation, which splits up production across countries to find the cheapest, fastest, or most skilled location for each step.** India is a major node in this global jigsaw — chiefly for software, BPO, pharmaceuticals, and increasingly for assembly of electronics.

**(3) Three forces drove modern globalisation: technology (container, internet, mobile), liberalisation (India's 1991 reforms), and rules (the WTO, 1995).** None of them works alone; all three reinforce each other.

**(4) The benefits and costs are real but unequally shared.** Skilled urban Indians and large Indian companies have gained enormously. Many small producers, farmers, and informal workers have been squeezed. Smart policy tries to widen the winners and cushion the losers.

The Indian story is far from over. The next two decades will probably see India become the world's third-largest economy, with hundreds of millions still to be lifted into middle-class life. How fairly that lifting happens depends on choices being made now — by governments, by companies, and (in a democracy) by citizens like you.

## Apply What You've Learnt

### Story 1: Your Daadi's "Videshi Maal" Box

Sit down with your grandmother — or any elder above 65 — and ask her what "videshi maal" (foreign goods) meant when she was your age. She will probably remember a cousin returning from Dubai with a Sony Walkman that the whole street came to see. She will remember kitchen items "from foreign" that came once in a decade and were preserved like temple property. A bottle of Yardley talcum powder, an Onida TV bought after three years of saving, a small bottle of Old Spice from a visiting uncle.

Then walk her through your own home — your phone, your shampoo, your refrigerator, the OTT subscription on the TV. Ask her: **"Could you have imagined this house in 1985?"** Listen carefully. Her answer is the whole chapter in one conversation.

### Story 2: Anitha's Career Arc

Anitha grew up in Salem, Tamil Nadu, in a family where the highest-earning member — her father — was a postal clerk earning ₹4,000 a month in 1995. She studied computer science in a small engineering college, joined a Bengaluru IT firm in 2007, and within ten years was leading a team of 18 people supporting a Texas insurance company. She bought her parents a small flat. Her own daughter now studies in an international school.

Anitha is not famous. She is one of millions of women whose lives were rewritten by India's connection to the global software economy. The story of globalisation is not always told through prime ministers and trade ministers. Often it is told through young women like Anitha, working night shifts in glass towers, learning to speak in two accents, sending money home each month, and quietly becoming the first middle-class generation of their family.

### Story 3: When Walmart Came to India

In 2007, the American giant **Walmart** partnered with India's Bharti Group to start cash-and-carry (wholesale) stores in India, mainly serving small retailers like the kirana shopkeeper down your street. The arrival caused enormous debate. Some argued that big-box retail would crush millions of small shopkeepers; others said it would force prices down, improve farm-to-shop supply chains, and benefit consumers. The Bharti-Walmart partnership eventually ended in 2013, but Walmart later acquired **Flipkart** in 2018 — making it one of the largest foreign investments ever in India. The debate did not end; it simply shifted from physical stores to e-commerce. Whether Indian kirana shops survive Amazon and Flipkart is one of the live globalisation questions of your time.

### Try This Yourself (DIY)

**Pick any one and do it properly.**

**Activity A — The Country-of-Origin Audit.** Pick 10 products in your home (anything: a toothpaste, a shampoo, a phone, a kitchen mixer, a packet of biscuits, a piece of clothing). Read the label carefully. Where is it made? Who owns the company? Note your findings in a small notebook. You will be surprised how many "Indian" brands have foreign parents — and how many "foreign" brands are actually made inside India.

**Activity B — Mall Map of MNCs.** Visit any mall (or any high street) in your town. Walk through it slowly. Identify three multinational corporations from the brand boards. For each, find out: which country is the parent company in? How long has it been in India? What does it sell here that it doesn't sell back home? You will see the shape of MNCs become real and physical.

**Activity C — The IT/MNC Professional Interview.** Find someone — relative, neighbour, family friend — who works for an IT company or an MNC. Ask them five honest questions: (1) When did you start? (2) Who is the foreign client/parent? (3) What is your day actually like? (4) What is the best thing about this job? (5) What is the worst? Write a short profile (about a page). One conversation will teach you more about outsourcing than a chapter can.

### A Closing Question to Keep Asking

When you walk into a shop, a mall, or even your own kitchen, keep asking the same small question for the rest of your life: **"Who made this, where, and what kind of life are they living because I bought it?"** That single question is the moral heart of globalisation. It is what turns an economics chapter into a way of seeing the world.
