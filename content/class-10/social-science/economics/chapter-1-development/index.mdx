## A Word That Means Different Things to Different People

Ask three people in your colony what "development" means and you will get three different answers.

A **landless agricultural labourer** in a Bihar village will say: development is **more days of work** and a **higher daily wage**. He does not need a foreign holiday. He needs his family to eat three times a day and his children to stay in school.

A **prosperous farmer** in western Uttar Pradesh will say: development is **a higher Minimum Support Price** for sugarcane, **assured electricity** for his tube-well, and a good market for his crop. He already eats well. What he wants is security and profit.

A **young woman from a tribal area in Jharkhand** will say: development is **the right to study after Class 8**, the freedom to choose her own husband, and a **government job** that respects her degree. For her, money matters — but so does dignity and freedom.

A **boy from an urban slum** in Mumbai will say: development is **toilets that work**, **water that comes more than thirty minutes a day**, and a **school where teachers actually show up**. He does not need a car. He needs a system that already exists for the rich to also reach him.

Notice something. None of these four people is wrong. Each one is describing development from where they are standing. **Development goals are different for different people because their lives are different.** A definition of development that ignores this is useless. Worse, it is dishonest.

This chapter asks a simple but heavy question. **What does it mean for a country to "develop"?** Is it more income? More wealth? Or is it something larger — health, education, freedom, dignity, and a planet that survives long enough for your grandchildren to live on it? Indian economists, the United Nations, and the World Bank have spent fifty years arguing about this. By the end of the chapter, you will see why the argument matters — and why a small state like **Kerala** can beat a richer state like **Punjab** at the most important development measures.

## Different Goals, Sometimes Conflicting Goals

The first lesson is uncomfortable. **What is development for one person can be destruction for another.**

A dam on the Narmada brings electricity to factories in Gujarat and irrigation water to farmers in Saurashtra. That is development for them. The same dam drowns 245 villages and displaces 200,000 tribal families who lived in those forests for centuries. For them it is the opposite of development. It is the end of their world.

A new highway connecting Delhi to Mumbai cuts travel time from 24 hours to 12. That is development for the truck driver and the businessman. The same highway cuts straight through a wildlife corridor, kills a tiger reserve, and pushes the leopard population into village boundaries where the leopards then kill goats and the villagers kill the leopards. For the forest, it is a wound.

A shopping mall replaces an old market in your town. The mall owner becomes rich. The big-brand shops boom. **The 80 small vendors** who sold vegetables, plastic buckets, and second-hand books in the same spot lose their livelihood overnight. Their children drop out of school because the family income collapsed.

So development is **not a single number** that goes up for everyone at once. It is a set of changes — and we must ask: **whose** life improves? **Whose** life gets worse? **At what cost** to the environment? Indian planners after 1991 sometimes forgot to ask these questions. Today, no honest student of economics can avoid them.

## National Income and Per Capita Income — The Old Way of Measuring

For most of the twentieth century, governments measured development by counting **money**. The logic was simple. A country produces goods and services. Add up the total value of everything produced in a year. That total is called the **national income** (more precisely, the Gross Domestic Product or **GDP**).

But GDP alone tells you only the size of an economy, not how well its people live. China has a much larger national income than Norway, but the average Norwegian lives more comfortably than the average Chinese citizen. To compare people's wellbeing rather than the size of the economy, economists divide national income by population:

> **Per capita income = National income ÷ Total population**

This gives you the **average income per person per year**. The World Bank uses per capita income (converted into US dollars) to classify countries into three groups in its annual **World Development Report**:

- **High-income (rich) countries** — per capita income of **about US $49,300 or above** per year (figures from the World Development Report 2019). India's friends Japan, Germany, the USA, and Singapore fall here.
- **Low-income countries** — per capita income of **about US $2,500 or less** per year. Most of sub-Saharan Africa, Afghanistan, and Nepal sit here.
- **Middle-income countries** — everything in between. **India**, with a per capita income of about US $6,700 in 2019, falls into the **low-middle-income** category.

This is a useful first sorting. But it is also dangerous. **Averages hide more than they reveal.**

## The Trick of the Average

Imagine two friends, Anu and Bina. Each earns ₹50,000 a month. Their **average income is ₹50,000**.

Now imagine that Anu earns ₹1,00,000 a month and Bina earns nothing — Bina has lost her job. The **average is still ₹50,000**. But Bina cannot eat the average. Anu has comfort. Bina has hunger. The number lies.

This is the great problem of per capita income. India's per capita income may rise every year, but if the **top 10% of households** keep grabbing 60% of the new wealth — which the World Inequality Lab has shown is happening — then crores of poor Indians do not feel the "development" at all. The number says **growth**. Their stomachs say **no change**.

So the question is not just "Is national income rising?" It is also "Is national income reaching the poorest?" Economists call this the **distribution of income**. A country that grows but stays unequal has not really developed. It has only produced more rich people.

## Beyond Income — Why Money Alone is Not Enough

Now comes the chapter's central insight, and the most important one. **Income matters, but it is not the only thing that matters.**

Consider three things money cannot easily buy:

**1. Public Facilities.** If your city has no clean water, even ₹50 lakh a year cannot give you a healthy childhood. The rich buy bottled water; the rest of the city drinks what the corporation supplies. If your village has no good hospital, no amount of money helps when there is a heart attack at midnight. Schools, roads, electricity, **public transport**, hospitals — these are called **public goods**. A rich man in a town with bad public goods is poorer in real terms than a moderately well-off man in a town with good public goods.

**2. Pollution and the Environment.** A businessman in Delhi may earn ₹5 lakh a month. But every winter he and his children breathe air that the WHO calls **hazardous**. His income cannot buy clean air. His income cannot replace lungs damaged by twenty winters of smog. **Bhutan**, a country with a per capita income about one-third of ours, has 70% of its land under forest, clean rivers, and the cleanest air in South Asia. By GDP it is poor. By the things money cannot buy, it is rich.

**3. Equity and Social Justice.** If a Dalit student gets the same marks as an upper-caste student but does not get the job, money has nothing to do with it. If a girl in a UP village is married off at 16 because her family thinks education is wasted on her, no rise in average income will reach her. Development must include **equality before the law**, **freedom from discrimination**, and **the ability of every citizen to lead a life of dignity**. Without those, GDP is a deceiving number.

This is the heart of the modern definition of development. **Development = a long, healthy, educated, dignified life for as many people as possible — within a society that does not destroy its environment for short-term gain.**

## The Human Development Index — UNDP's Answer to "GDP Only"

In 1990, the **United Nations Development Programme (UNDP)** had had enough of the world being measured only by money. Led by the Pakistani economist **Mahbub ul Haq** and the Indian Nobel laureate **Amartya Sen**, the UNDP introduced a new measure called the **Human Development Index (HDI)**.

The HDI combines three things:

1. **Life expectancy at birth** — how many years a newborn baby is expected to live. A society that lets its citizens live longer is doing something right with health, food, water, and safety.
2. **Education** — measured as the **mean years of schooling** of adults (25+) plus **expected years of schooling** of children. A society that keeps children in school longer is investing in tomorrow.
3. **Per capita income** — adjusted for what the money can actually buy locally (this is called **Purchasing Power Parity**, or PPP).

These three are combined into a single score between **0 and 1**. The closer to 1, the better.

The UNDP publishes the **Human Development Report (HDR)** every year, ranking 189 countries.

Some recent figures (2020 HDR):

- **Norway** — HDI of 0.957 (rank 1) — life expectancy 82.4 years.
- **Sri Lanka** — HDI of 0.782 (rank 72) — life expectancy 77 years, with a per capita income of only about $12,700.
- **India** — HDI of 0.645 (rank 131) — life expectancy 69.7 years, per capita income about $6,700.
- **Myanmar** — HDI of 0.583 (rank 147).
- **Nepal** — HDI of 0.602 (rank 142).
- **Bangladesh** — HDI of 0.632 (rank 133).

Notice the surprise. **Bangladesh ranks above India** despite a lower per capita income. **Sri Lanka, with one-fifth of America's per capita income**, has a life expectancy almost equal to the United States. Money is *part* of the story. It is not the *whole* story.

## The Kerala-Punjab Puzzle — Why a Smaller Income Can Win

The most striking proof of "income is not everything" sits inside India itself. Compare two states:

| Indicator | Kerala | Punjab | Bihar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Per capita income (₹, 2018-19) | ~2.04 lakh | ~1.66 lakh | ~0.40 lakh |
| Infant Mortality Rate (per 1000 live births) | **6** | 21 | 32 |
| Literacy Rate | **94%** | 76% | 62% |
| Net Attendance Ratio (Class 9-12) | **83%** | 68% | 43% |
| Life expectancy at birth | **75 years** | 71 years | 69 years |

Punjab has long been one of India's most prosperous states because of its rich agriculture. **Yet Kerala, with a per capita income only slightly higher than Punjab, beats Punjab in every human indicator that matters.** Kerala has fewer babies dying, more children in school, longer-living adults, and a far better-educated population.

How? Kerala chose to **spend its government money on public goods** — schools, primary health centres, ration shops, drinking water, and women's education — long before it was rich enough to do so. Punjab spent more on private wealth — tractors, tube-wells, urea, and farm machinery — which made farmers richer but did not build a public health system. The result: a slightly richer state with a sicker, less-educated population.

This is the **Kerala Model** of development that economists across the world study. The lesson is sharp. **Public spending shapes human lives more than private wealth does. A poor state that invests in schools and health centres can leapfrog a rich state that does not.**

## The Indicators That Really Matter — A Closer Look

Beyond income, the government of India and the UNDP track a set of indicators that capture human wellbeing. You should remember each:

**Infant Mortality Rate (IMR)** — the number of babies who die before their first birthday per 1,000 live births. India's IMR is about **28** (2020). Kerala's is **6**. Sweden's is **2**. IMR measures the joint quality of the **mother's nutrition, the public health system, the cleanliness of water, and access to immunisation**. One number, ten things measured.

**Literacy Rate** — the share of people aged 7 and above who can read and write with understanding in any language. India's literacy is about **77%**; Kerala's is **94%**, Bihar's is **62%**. Female literacy is even more telling — when women are educated, families are smaller, healthier, and more economically productive across generations.

**Net Attendance Ratio** — the share of children of school-going age who are *actually attending* school. Enrolment numbers are easy to inflate; attendance numbers are honest. A net attendance ratio of 83% in Kerala (Class 9-12) vs. 43% in Bihar tells you which state's children are getting an education and which are dropping out into child labour.

**Sex Ratio** — the number of girls per 1,000 boys, especially the **child sex ratio (0-6 years)**. A society that systematically aborts or neglects girl children is sick at its core, no matter how rich it is. India's child sex ratio fell from 945 (1991) to **919 (2011)** — a warning that **economic growth alone does not buy gender justice**. Some prosperous states (Punjab, Haryana) have had the worst child sex ratios in the country.

**Body Mass Index (BMI)** — a rough check of whether a person is undernourished, normal, or overweight. Calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared. A BMI below 18.5 in adults is undernourishment. India's National Family Health Survey-5 (2019-21) showed that **about 19% of Indian women are still undernourished**, while childhood stunting affects **35.5%** of children. Hunger is not just a poverty problem — it is also a development failure.

**Public Health Spending** — how much of GDP a government spends on public health. India spends about **1.5-1.8%** of GDP on public health. Cuba spends 10%. Norway spends 9%. Our shameful low spending shows up in every IMR, MMR, and Covid mortality number. A country that does not pay for its health system pays in dead children.

## The Sustainability Question — Development for the Grandchildren

So far we have asked: **what is development?** A different question is just as urgent. **Will development last?**

The earth has limited resources. Coal, oil, groundwater, forests, fish, fertile soil — all are finite. India's groundwater table has fallen 4-10 metres in many districts since 1980. Punjab is now sinking its tube-wells more than 100 metres deep. The Aral Sea has shrunk to 10% of its 1960 size because cotton farms drank its rivers. The Amazon rainforest has lost 17% of its area in fifty years.

If the present generation **uses up resources at a rate the planet cannot replace**, then "development" today becomes destruction for tomorrow. Our grandchildren will inherit a planet of dry rivers, salty soil, dead forests, and unbearable summers. Was that development?

The famous **Brundtland Commission of 1987** gave the clearest definition the world has of **sustainable development**:

> *"Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."*

Three steps make development sustainable:

1. **Use renewable resources at the rate they can replenish.** A forest that grows 1,000 cubic metres of wood a year should not be cut at 2,000 cubic metres a year. A groundwater aquifer that is recharged at 50 cm a year should not be pumped at 100 cm a year.
2. **Shift away from non-renewable resources.** Coal, oil, and gas must give way to **solar, wind, and hydro** power. India has committed to **500 GW of renewable energy by 2030** — one of the most ambitious targets in the world.
3. **Recycle, reuse, reduce.** A circular economy. The plastic bottle you drink from today should not float in the Indian Ocean for 400 years. The phone you replace every two years has metals that should not become a mountain of e-waste in Moradabad.

Sustainability is no longer a side topic of development. It **is** development. A country that wrecks its environment to chase per capita income has not developed. It has only borrowed against the future at a high interest rate.

## Putting It All Together — What Development Really Means

Let us assemble the chapter into one picture.

Development is **not just income**. It is income **plus**:

- **Public goods** — clean water, schools, hospitals, roads, electricity, public transport, ration shops, working courts of law.
- **Health and education** — long lives, literate citizens, well-fed children, capable adults.
- **Equity** — opportunities open to women, Dalits, Adivasis, minorities, the disabled. No one left behind.
- **Freedom and dignity** — the ability to live without fear, to speak, to choose your work, to marry whom you wish.
- **Environmental sustainability** — clean air, living forests, full aquifers, a stable climate.

A country that has high income but fails on the others is **rich but not developed**. A small country like **Costa Rica** — with a per capita income of $12,400 — has higher life expectancy, better literacy, and more forest cover than the United States. By GDP it is poor; by development it is ahead.

This is what the UNDP, Amartya Sen, Mahbub ul Haq, and the Brundtland Commission have been trying to say for forty years. **Development is the right to a full human life.** Anything less is just bookkeeping.

When India turns 100 in 2047, no one will measure her by what her GDP looks like. They will ask: did her babies live? Did her girls go to school? Did her elders die with dignity? Were her rivers still flowing? Was her air still breathable? Was her democracy still alive? **Those questions are the real exam paper on development.** This chapter is preparation for that paper.

## Apply — From Theory to the Street Outside

Three short stories. One DIY mission. One question to keep asking.

**Story 1 — The Kerala Compounder Who Saved a Punjab Cousin.** In 2019, Ravinder, a young farmer from Bathinda district in Punjab, drove his cousin Manjit to Thiruvananthapuram for cancer treatment. His own state had top private hospitals in Ludhiana, but they cost ₹15 lakh. In Kerala, a government cancer centre treated Manjit for ₹40,000 over six months. Ravinder spent those six months staying in a hostel and watching Kerala work. He saw an ASHA worker visit a slum every morning to check on pregnant women. He saw the local primary health centre opening at 7 AM and a doctor — an actual doctor — sitting there. He saw a government school with computers and a midday meal of fish curry and rice. "Punjab is richer," he said when he came home. "But Kerala is wealthier." That sentence is the whole chapter.

**Story 2 — The ASHA Worker is the New GDP.** In every village of India there is now a woman in a pink sari called an **ASHA — Accredited Social Health Activist**. She is paid ₹4,000-5,000 a month, much less than minimum wage. She walks 5 kilometres a day, visits 200 households, makes sure babies are vaccinated, takes pregnant women for check-ups, distributes iron tablets, counts toilets. India's Infant Mortality Rate fell from **57 (2005)** to **28 (2020)** — partly because of her. No new factory caused that drop. **She did.** The next time someone shows you a GDP graph and calls it development, ask: how many ASHAs does that graph have?

**Story 3 — A Tale of Two Villages.** In **Khera village**, Haryana, every household owns a car. The men are rich, the houses are pucca, the marriages are extravagant. But there is no working school past Class 5. The girls are married at 17 to a neighbouring village. The boys grow up to inherit fields and addiction. In **Vellore Mavalipuram** village in Tamil Nadu, most families are landless. Daily wages are low. But there is a government school where 95% of the children — boys and girls — finish Class 12. Three girls last year became nurses. One became an engineer. **Twenty years from now, which village will have done more for India?** That is the development question.

### DIY — Three Missions for You

1. **Compute your family's HDI across generations.** Find out from your parents and grandparents: How many years did your grandfather go to school? Your grandmother? Your father? Your mother? You? Plot it on graph paper. You will likely see a steep rise in education across three generations. That rise is the real story of post-independence India. Compare it with the *income* of the same generations. Which rose faster — schooling or income? Your answer is a tiny HDI.

2. **Survey five neighbours: "What is development for you?"** Ask them in their own words. Write down their five answers. Sort them: how many said money? Health? Children's education? Pollution? Roads? Freedom? You will discover that the people around you are already economists. They just don't have a vocabulary for it. Give them the vocabulary.

3. **Track three indicators for your state.** Visit the website of your state's Directorate of Economics & Statistics or the **NITI Aayog SDG Dashboard**. Note down your state's (a) Infant Mortality Rate, (b) Literacy Rate, (c) Sex Ratio. Compare to the all-India average. Where does your state shine? Where does it shame? Bring those three numbers to your next family dinner. They are more honest than the news.

**Show what you found** — Bring your three answers to class. Make a wall chart with one column per state. Let the rest of the class add their numbers. By the end you will have a map of human development across India, **drawn by Class 10 students, not by the World Bank**.

**Closing question to keep asking** — Every time you read about a new project — a highway, a coal mine, a shopping mall, a dam, an airport — ask yourself this single question. *"Whose development is this, and at whose cost?"* If you can answer that question clearly, you are already a better economist than half of the people running the country.
