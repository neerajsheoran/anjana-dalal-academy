## Is Development Just About Money?

**Child:** Maa, my friend Aarav's father got promoted last week and they bought a new SUV. His mother said now they have "developed" more than us. Is that true?

**Mother:** Let me ask you something first, beta. Does Aarav's father look happier? Does his mother go to bed without worry? Do they breathe cleaner air than us in Delhi? Are their parents living longer because of that new car?

**Child:** I... no, I don't think the car changed any of that.

**Mother:** Exactly. They have a new car — that is a *thing*. They have not "developed" as people. Development is not the same as buying. A family that buys a new TV every year but never sends its daughter to college has not developed. A family that lives in a small house but sends both children to good schools, eats nutritious food, sees a doctor when needed, and helps the grandparents — that family is developing. **Development is the slow improvement of a life. Shopping is just an event.**

**Child:** So when the news says "India's GDP grew 7%", that is also just an event?

**Mother:** Smart question, beta. GDP growth tells you the *country's economy* is bigger this year than last year. It does *not* tell you whether the poorest 10% of Indians are eating better. It does not tell you whether more children went to school. It does not tell you whether the air is cleaner. GDP measures the size of the kitchen, not the quality of the meal.

**Child:** Then why is everyone obsessed with GDP?

**Mother:** Because it is the easiest thing to count. And what is easy to count tends to take over the conversation. But your generation will have to ask harder questions — about who is being left behind, about what is being destroyed to produce that GDP, about whether the country your children inherit will be liveable. Those questions don't have one number for an answer. They have *many*. That is why this chapter introduces the HDI, the IMR, the literacy rate, the sex ratio. Each one fills in something GDP misses.

## Why Is Kerala on Top of HDI but Not GDP?

**Child:** Mother, I don't understand this. The chapter says Punjab is richer per person than Kerala. But Kerala has fewer babies dying, more children in school, longer-living people. How can a poorer place be better?

**Mother:** Because **what you do with your money matters more than how much money you have**. Imagine two families. Family A earns ₹50,000 a month and spends it on a fancy bike, branded clothes, and weekend pizzas. Family B earns ₹40,000 a month, but spends it on a tuition class for both children, monthly milk and eggs, and yearly health check-ups. In ten years, whose children will be stronger and more educated?

**Child:** Family B's, even though they earn less.

**Mother:** That is Kerala. Kerala for decades chose to **spend its government money on schools and primary health centres** instead of on big factories or showy projects. They paid teachers well. They paid ASHA workers. They built thousands of primary health centres. They made sure every village had a school and a midday meal. Punjab spent its money on tractors, tube-wells, fertilisers — which made farmers rich but did not build a public health system. The result: Punjab has more cars, Kerala has more *years of life*.

**Child:** So Kerala is poor but lives better?

**Mother:** Kerala is not really poor, beta — its per capita income is actually slightly *higher* than Punjab's now. But even when it *was* poorer, it lived better. **Public investment beats private wealth, when it comes to human development.** A small state with good schools beats a rich state with no schools. That is the Kerala Model that economists across the world come to study.

**Child:** Does any country in the world do this even more than Kerala?

**Mother:** Yes — **Costa Rica, Cuba, Sri Lanka, Bhutan**. None of them are rich. All of them have life expectancies close to America. All of them spend a big share of their budget on education and primary health. Bhutan even measures **Gross National Happiness** as seriously as Gross National Product. They are tiny countries, but they have figured out something the big countries forgot. **A government's first job is people, not GDP.**

## What Counts as Public Goods?

**Child:** Mother, the chapter keeps talking about public goods. Our school is private. Our hospital is private. Our water comes from a private tanker because the municipal supply is two hours a day. So what public goods are even left?

**Mother:** That itself is a development failure, beta. In a healthy society, **clean water should come from a tap that the corporation maintains**, not from a private tanker that charges ₹50 per drum. Schools should be government schools strong enough that even a businessman wants to send his child there. Hospitals should be government hospitals where the doctor is qualified, the medicines are stocked, and the wards are clean. **In rich countries, the public schools and the public hospitals are excellent. In our cities, they have collapsed because the rich opted out and stopped pressuring the government.**

**Child:** So public goods are things the government should provide for everyone?

**Mother:** Yes — **roads, public transport, electricity, water, schools, hospitals, ration shops, courts of law, police protection, parks, public toilets**. The list is long. The test is simple: is it something that should serve a king and a beggar the same way? If yes, it is a public good.

**Child:** Why is it so important that they be *public*?

**Mother:** Because private services depend on your wallet. If you have money, you get them. If you don't, you don't. Public services depend on your citizenship. **If you are an Indian, you should get them.** That is what makes a society fair. When the government school is good, the cobbler's son and the doctor's son sit on the same bench and learn the same maths. When school becomes private, that contact ends — and so does the equality. Public goods are not just *services*. They are the connective tissue of citizenship.

**Child:** So when we vote, we should ask politicians about public goods?

**Mother:** Beta, that is the most important question to ask any politician. Don't ask what *they* will give *you*. Ask what they will fix for *everyone*. A good road for everyone is better than a free phone for some.

## How Do We Measure Development?

**Child:** Maa, this part confused me. The chapter says HDI is the main measure. But it also mentions IMR, literacy, BMI, sex ratio. So which one is the *real* measure?

**Mother:** None alone, beta. **All of them together.** Think of it like a medical check-up. The doctor doesn't decide whether you are healthy by your weight alone. She checks weight, blood pressure, blood sugar, eyesight, lungs, heart, mental state. Each one tells a different story. Development is the same. HDI gives you a quick three-in-one snapshot — life expectancy, education, income. IMR tells you how the public health system is treating the *poorest mothers*. Literacy tells you whether the *next generation* will be able to read its rights. BMI tells you whether children are *getting enough food*. Sex ratio tells you whether girls are even *allowed to be born*. Each indicator is one organ of the same body.

**Child:** But people will want one number, right?

**Mother:** They always do. That is why HDI was invented — to give a single number that is honest at least about three big things. But the smart student goes deeper. When someone tells you India's HDI rose from 0.42 to 0.65 over thirty years, that *is* progress. But you should also ask: **did the sex ratio improve? Did the IMR improve? Did inequality fall or rise?** If HDI went up but inequality also went up, then the rise was unfair. If HDI rose but the sex ratio fell, then the rise was hollow. **One number is a starting point, not a final answer.**

**Child:** Why do they say *Net* Attendance Ratio? What's wrong with just "enrolment"?

**Mother:** Enrolment is the number of children whose names are on the school register. **Attendance is the number who actually show up.** In many parts of India, especially rural Bihar, UP, and Madhya Pradesh, the register may say 100 children are enrolled, but only 40 come to school regularly. The rest are working, looking after younger siblings, or just dropped out and the school never updated the records. Net Attendance Ratio is therefore the more honest number. **Anything that can be inflated by paper, will be. Always look for the indicator that walks into the classroom and counts heads.**

## Can Development Hurt Some People?

**Child:** Maa, this question really troubled me. The chapter says a dam can drown 245 villages. A highway can kill a wildlife corridor. A mall can wipe out 80 vendors. But all of these are also called "development" by the government. How can development hurt people?

**Mother:** Because **the word "development" has been kidnapped**, beta. When a politician inaugurates a new airport or a new expressway, he calls it development. But development should not be measured by the size of the ribbon being cut. It should be measured by the lives improved minus the lives damaged.

**Child:** So a project can be bad development?

**Mother:** Yes — or at the very least, mixed. The Sardar Sarovar Dam on the Narmada gives electricity and irrigation to Gujarat. That is real development for them. But it submerged forests and tribal villages, displaced 200,000 people, and many of them were given compensation worth a fraction of what their land was worth. For those families, the dam was destruction. The honest economist says **both** at once — gain for some, loss for others, and asks: *who decided this, and was the loss compensated fairly?*

**Child:** This is uncomfortable.

**Mother:** It should be. Development is not a fairy tale. It is a series of choices about who pays the cost. The forest pays. The tribal pays. The slum pays. The vendor pays. Often the people who *gain* from the project are not the same people who *lose*. That is why a democracy must have **strong courts, free press, and consent from those who will be affected**. Without those, "development" becomes a polite word for taking from the weak to give to the strong.

**Child:** So what should we ask, every time a new project is announced?

**Mother:** Three questions, beta. **Who gains? Who loses? And were the losers told and compensated fairly?** If the answers are honest, you can support the project even when it has costs. If the answers are hidden, then "development" is a lie someone is trying to sell you.

## What Should I Take From This?

**Child:** Mother, this chapter is huge. If I had to take three things into my Class 10 board exam and into my adult life, what should they be?

**Mother:** Three things, beta.

**One — Income matters, but income is not everything.** A society that only chases GDP will eventually have rich people in dirty hospitals, smart phones in dropout villages, and a planet too broken to live on. The HDI, the IMR, the literacy rate, the sex ratio — these are not "extra" measurements. They are the heart of development.

**Two — Public goods build people. Private wealth builds individuals.** A society of rich families with broken schools is *not* developed. A society of modest families with brilliant schools *is*. Always vote, fight, and pay taxes for stronger public goods.

**Three — Whose development, at whose cost?** Every time you hear about a project, a policy, a number — ask those two questions. If the answer is honest, support it. If the answer is hidden, oppose it. **That single question will make you a sharper economist than 80% of the people on TV.**

**Child:** And for the rest of my life?

**Mother:** Add a fourth, then. **Will your grandchildren inherit a planet they can live on?** If the development happening today fails that test, it is not development at all. It is just borrowing. Sustainable development — the kind the Brundtland Commission talked about — is the only honest development. Everything else is theft from the future.

**Child:** That's a lot to carry, Maa.

**Mother:** Carry it, beta. Your generation will need it more than mine did. And remember — **the goal is not to make India rich. The goal is to make India a country where every Indian can live a long, healthy, educated, free, and dignified life, on a planet that survives.** Anything less is not enough. Now go finish your homework.
