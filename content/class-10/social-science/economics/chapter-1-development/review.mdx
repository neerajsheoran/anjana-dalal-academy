## In One Paragraph

Development means different things to different people — a labourer wants steady work, a farmer wants assured prices, a tribal girl wants the freedom to study, a slum child wants clean water. For most of the twentieth century, governments measured development only by **per capita income** (national income divided by population), and the World Bank used it to sort countries into high-income, middle-income, and low-income groups. But averages hide inequality — the same average income can sit on top of widely different lives. Real development includes **public facilities** like schools, hospitals, water, and electricity; **equity and social justice** so that women, Dalits, Adivasis, and minorities also benefit; and **sustainability** so that the planet survives for future generations. The **Human Development Index (HDI)** of the UNDP — created by Mahbub ul Haq and Amartya Sen in 1990 — combines life expectancy, education (mean and expected years of schooling), and per capita income (PPP) into one score between 0 and 1, published every year in the **Human Development Report**. India ranks 131 (HDR 2020), below Sri Lanka, Bhutan, and Bangladesh. Inside India, the **Kerala-Punjab puzzle** shows that Kerala, with only a slightly higher per capita income than Punjab, beats it on every wellbeing indicator (IMR 6 vs 21, literacy 94% vs 76%) because Kerala invested in **public goods**. Indicators like the **Infant Mortality Rate, Literacy Rate, Net Attendance Ratio, Sex Ratio, Body Mass Index, and Public Health Spending** capture the human reality that per capita income misses. The **Brundtland Commission (1987)** defined sustainable development as meeting today's needs without compromising tomorrow's. True development is a long, healthy, educated, dignified life for as many people as possible, within a society that does not destroy its environment.

## Key Takeaways

- **Different goals for different people** — a landless labourer wants work and wages; a prosperous farmer wants better prices and electricity; a young tribal woman wants education and dignity; a slum dweller wants clean water and a working school. Development is not one definition.
- **Development goals can conflict** — a dam that gives electricity to a city can drown tribal villages; a mall that creates rich shop owners can wipe out 80 vendors. We must always ask: *whose* development, at *whose* cost?
- **National Income / GDP** — the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year.
- **Per Capita Income = National Income ÷ Total Population** — used by the World Bank to classify countries.
- **World Bank classification (World Development Report 2019)** — High-income (≥ US $49,300 per capita), Low-income (≤ US $2,500), Middle-income (in between). **India** is a low-middle-income country at about $6,700.
- **Averages hide inequality** — two friends earning ₹50,000 each have the same average income as one earning ₹1,00,000 while the other earns nothing.
- **Income is not enough** — public facilities (water, electricity, hospitals, schools), clean environment, and equity are things money cannot easily buy.
- **HDI — Human Development Index** — UNDP measure introduced in 1990 by **Mahbub ul Haq** and **Amartya Sen**; combines (1) life expectancy at birth, (2) years of schooling, (3) per capita income (PPP). Score between 0 and 1.
- **Human Development Report (HDR)** — published yearly by UNDP; ranks 189 countries. **India ranks 131 (HDR 2020)** with HDI 0.645 — below Sri Lanka (72), Bhutan, and Bangladesh (133).
- **Kerala vs Punjab vs Bihar** — Kerala (IMR 6, literacy 94%, life expectancy 75) outperforms richer Punjab (IMR 21, literacy 76%) because of investment in public health and education. Bihar lags far behind both.
- **Key human indicators** — Infant Mortality Rate, Literacy Rate, Net Attendance Ratio, Sex Ratio, Body Mass Index, Public Health Spending. Each captures a different dimension of wellbeing.
- **Public Health Spending** — India spends only **about 1.5-1.8% of GDP** on public health; Norway spends 9%, Cuba 10%. Our low spending shows up in IMR and Covid mortality.
- **Body Mass Index (BMI)** = Weight (kg) ÷ Height² (m²) — under 18.5 is undernourishment in adults; NFHS-5 (2019-21) shows 19% of Indian women undernourished and 35.5% of children stunted.
- **Sustainable Development (Brundtland Commission, 1987)** — meeting present needs without compromising future generations. Requires renewable use within natural replenishment rates, shift away from fossil fuels, and reuse-recycle-reduce.

## Key Terms

- **Development** — a process of social, economic, and human progress that improves the quality of life for most people, expanding their freedoms and capabilities, while sustaining the environment for future generations.
- **National Income (GDP)** — the total monetary value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a given year.
- **Per Capita Income** — national income divided by total population; the average income per person per year.
- **Human Development Index (HDI)** — a composite measure (0 to 1) created by the UNDP combining life expectancy, education, and per capita income (PPP).
- **Human Development Report (HDR)** — the annual UNDP publication that ranks countries by HDI and analyses development trends.
- **Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)** — an adjustment that compares incomes by what they can actually buy locally, rather than by exchange rate alone.
- **Infant Mortality Rate (IMR)** — the number of children dying before their first birthday per 1,000 live births in a year.
- **Literacy Rate** — the share of the population aged 7 and above who can read and write with understanding in any language.
- **Net Attendance Ratio** — the percentage of children of a school-going age group who are actually attending school.
- **Body Mass Index (BMI)** — weight (kg) ÷ height² (m²); used to assess undernourishment or obesity in adults.
- **Sustainable Development** — development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (Brundtland Commission, 1987).
- **Public Goods** — goods and services provided by the state for all citizens — schools, hospitals, water, roads, public transport, electricity, ration shops — whose absence cannot easily be replaced by private wealth.
