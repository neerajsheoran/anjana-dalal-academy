## Why Three Sectors? Aren't They All Just Jobs?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says farmers don't earn much but most Indians work in farming. How does that even make sense? And why do they split the economy into three "sectors" — at the end, isn't all work just work?

**Mother:** I will answer the second part first because it makes the first part easier.

When you say "all work is just work," you are right at the level of dignity — every person who works deserves respect. But for an economist, the **kind of work** matters a lot, because different kinds of work have different relationships with **nature, with skills, and with machines**.

**The primary sector** is where humans **directly use nature** to produce things. A farmer grows rice; a fisherman catches fish; a miner digs out iron ore. The work depends on land, water, soil, climate. Output per worker is limited by what nature can give — you cannot make a field of wheat produce ten times more just by working harder.

**The secondary sector** is where humans **transform** raw materials. A cotton field gives you cotton. A cotton mill turns it into yarn. A garment factory turns yarn into shirts. Each step **adds value**. The same cotton, after this journey, sells for ten or twenty times its original price as a shirt in a showroom. This sector uses machines, energy, and organised labour to multiply the value of nature's gifts.

**The tertiary sector** is everything else that **supports** the first two and supports itself — transport, trade, banking, insurance, education, healthcare, communications, government, IT. It does not produce a thing you can hold. But without it, the other two collapse. Without trucks, the cotton can't reach the mill. Without banks, the mill can't get a loan. Without teachers, no one knows how to run the mill. Without doctors, the workers fall sick and stop coming.

So the three-sector split is not arbitrary. It captures **three different ways humans create economic value** — by harvesting nature, by transforming materials, and by providing services.

**Child:** Okay, that makes sense. But then why does India have so many people in farming if farming earns so little?

**Mother:** That is the deepest question of the chapter. Let me come to it in the next section.

## Why Do So Many Indians Still Work in Farming?

**Child:** If services produce more than half of GDP with just 30% of workers, and agriculture earns only 16% of GDP with 45% of workers — why don't the farming workers just move to services or factories?

**Mother:** Because in real life, **moving** is not so simple. Three big reasons.

**One — the alternative jobs do not exist in large enough numbers.** India did not industrialise the way Britain or China did. We do not have lakhs of garment factories absorbing village migrants the way Bangladesh or Vietnam do. The high-end services sector — IT, finance, professional services — is growing fast, but it employs maybe 8-10 million people in a country of 1.4 billion. The mass of villagers cannot find a place there.

**Two — the alternative jobs require skills the villagers don't have.** A farmer leaving his field cannot walk into a software company. He cannot walk into a hospital as a nurse. He often cannot even walk into a factory that needs trained machine-operators. The skill gap is enormous. And our system of vocational training and skilling is still very weak.

**Three — the family land is a safety net.** Even if a family's plot is too small to feed everyone, it is **something**. The young man may go to Mumbai for construction work for nine months, but if he gets injured or the work dries up, he comes back to the village. The land does not feed him richly, but it does not let him starve. So millions stay attached to agriculture as **insurance**, even when they aren't really needed there.

These three reasons together explain why India has, in a sense, **too many people in farming**. Many of them are not really needed for the harvest — that is **disguised unemployment**, which we will talk about. The country needs to create work elsewhere for them to move to, and skills so they can take that work. Until then, the farms keep absorbing the surplus.

**Child:** So the farm is a kind of waiting room?

**Mother:** A waiting room, a safety net, a fallback. Beautiful image, actually.

## What's the Difference Between Underemployment and Unemployment?

**Child:** Underemployment, unemployment, disguised unemployment — they all sound similar. Why so many words?

**Mother:** Because they describe three real situations that look similar but matter very differently for policy.

**Unemployment** is the cleanest case. A person **has no work at all**. He is willing to work, looking for work, and finding nothing. We measure this through surveys: "Did you work for pay in the last seven days?" If the answer is no and the person was looking, he is counted as unemployed.

**Underemployment** is when a person **has some work, but not enough or not at his level**. Two sub-types here. First, **not enough work** — a construction labourer who finds work only 12 days out of 30 is underemployed in time; his hands and his hours are partly wasted. Second, **wrong-level work** — a graduate working as a peon, an engineer driving a taxi, a CA working as a clerk. The economy has hired the body but not the brain.

**Disguised unemployment** is the strangest of the three. The person **looks employed** — he works on a farm, he gets a share of the family income, he might tell a surveyor "yes, I work on our land." But the work would be done just as well without him. If he left, the output would not fall. He is one of, say, **five family members on a farm that only needs three**. The other two are surplus, but they are absorbed because there is nowhere else for them to go.

The trick of disguised unemployment is that it is **hidden inside what looks like employment**. Surveys may count such people as employed. Only by asking "would the farm produce less if you left?" do you find the truth.

**Child:** So a survey could be very wrong?

**Mother:** Yes. The official unemployment rate in India often looks small — 6-8%. But the **real picture**, including underemployment and disguised unemployment, is much worse. Probably **150-200 million Indians** are either out of work, underused, or hidden-surplus. That is the human reality behind the dry statistics.

**Child:** Why does this happen so much in farming and not, say, in IT?

**Mother:** Because in IT, an extra employee costs the company money — they will not hire him if there's no work for him. In a family farm, an extra brother **costs nothing extra** to the farm — he eats from the same kitchen, sleeps in the same house. So the family absorbs him without firing him. Disguised unemployment thrives in **family enterprises**, **shared land**, **informal small businesses** — anywhere the cost of the extra worker is not separated from his earnings.

## What's MGNREGA For?

**Child:** The chapter mentions MGNREGA — 100 days of guaranteed work. Why does that exist?

**Mother:** MGNREGA is one of the most important policies India has ever passed, and it directly addresses the puzzle we have been discussing.

Picture a typical small farmer in Jharkhand. He has 1.5 acres. He grows paddy in the kharif season and a little pulse in rabi. His own farm work occupies him, say, 90 days a year. For another 100 days he might find labour on someone else's larger farm. That still leaves him with **175 days a year with no work**.

Earlier, those 175 days meant either eating his savings, taking high-interest loans from the local moneylender, migrating in distress to a city for casual work, or simply going hungry.

MGNREGA changed this. Since **2005**, Parliament has given every rural household a **legal right to 100 days of paid work per year**, on demand. If the panchayat does not arrange the work within 15 days, the family is entitled to an **unemployment allowance**.

Three radical things about it.

**One — it makes work a right.** Until MGNREGA, employment was something the market gave you or denied you. The Indian state had no obligation. MGNREGA created the obligation. That is a legal revolution.

**Two — it directly targets disguised unemployment and underemployment in rural India.** The farmer who has 175 idle days now has a legally enforceable claim on 100 of them. He earns; he stays in the village; he does not migrate in distress.

**Three — the work creates lasting assets.** The 100 days are not "do anything you like" — they are usually spent on **public works**: digging ponds, building check-dams, planting trees, levelling roads, deepening wells, soil conservation. These help the village's long-term productivity. A well dug in MGNREGA may serve the village for 30 years.

**Child:** So is MGNREGA a success?

**Mother:** It has been **partially successful**, with real problems.

The successes — it has provided work to crores of households. It has built lakhs of small rural assets. It has reduced distress migration. It has put cash directly into bank accounts of the poorest. In drought years and during COVID, it was a lifeline.

The problems — wages are often paid late, sometimes by months. Corruption happens — fake names on muster rolls. Some assets are badly built and crumble. Some states implement it well (like Telangana, Andhra, Tamil Nadu, Kerala); some poorly. The official wage rate sometimes lags behind the actual market wage, reducing its appeal.

When you hear MGNREGA being debated — should the budget be cut, should it be expanded, should it be extended to urban areas, should it be replaced by a cash transfer — those are real debates, and reasonable people can disagree. But the **core idea** — that the state owes the poor citizen a right to work — is, I think, one of India's quiet democratic achievements.

## Why Are Some Jobs in the "Organised" Sector and Some Not?

**Child:** The chapter says over 90% of Indians work in the "unorganised" sector. What does that even mean? Aren't all jobs "organised" in some way?

**Mother:** "Organised" here doesn't mean tidy or well-arranged. It is a specific economic term, and the distinction matters.

An **organised-sector job** has all of the following:
- A written contract of employment.
- Regular salary paid through a bank account.
- Fixed working hours (say 9 to 6), with overtime for extra work.
- Paid leave — casual, sick, earned, holidays.
- **Job security** — you cannot be dismissed at someone's whim; there is a process.
- **Social-security benefits** — Provident Fund (PF), gratuity, pension, ESI medical insurance, maternity leave.
- Coverage under labour laws — minimum wage, equal pay, safe workplace, right to form unions.

A government school teacher, a public-sector bank clerk, a factory worker in a large registered factory, a TCS software engineer, an Air India pilot — all are in the organised sector. They might complain about their jobs, but they have these protections.

An **unorganised-sector worker** has **none of the above**, or very few.

The maid who comes to clean someone's house at ₹3,000 a month — she has a verbal arrangement. If the family fires her, she has no recourse. She has no PF. If she falls sick, she does not earn that day. She has no paid leave, ever, in her life. She is in the unorganised sector.

So is the **autorickshaw driver**, the **street vendor**, the **construction labourer at a small site**, the **farm labourer**, the **small kirana-shop assistant**, the **beedi-roller**, the **brick-kiln worker**. So is your **driver** if he is on direct verbal employment.

And — this is the shocking number — **over 90% of Indian workers are in the unorganised sector**. Out of every 100 Indians who work, only about 8-10 have the full set of protections. The other 90 do not.

**Child:** Why so many? Why hasn't this changed?

**Mother:** Several reasons.

**One — most farms and small businesses are too small to be "organised."** Indian labour laws apply mainly to larger establishments. A factory with fewer than 10 workers, a shop with fewer than 5 — they are often outside the net of major labour laws. And most Indian enterprises are small.

**Two — employers prefer informal arrangements.** They are cheaper. No PF contribution, no gratuity, no required leave, easier to dismiss workers. So even some companies that could employ formally end up using contract labour or daily-wage labour instead.

**Three — workers themselves sometimes accept it.** A daily labourer who needs cash today does not negotiate for PF. He takes ₹400 and goes home. The future protection seems abstract; today's food is concrete.

**Four — enforcement is weak.** Even where laws exist (minimum wage, safe workplace), labour inspectors are few, courts are slow, and small employers know they can usually ignore the rules.

This is changing — slowly. The **e-Shram portal**, the **Code on Wages 2019**, the **Code on Social Security 2020** are attempts to bring more workers under some form of protection. The new categories — gig workers, platform workers like Ola drivers and Swiggy delivery boys — are also being recognised. But the gap is huge.

**Child:** So if I become a delivery boy when I am older, what would I be?

**Mother:** Officially, you would be a "**partner**" of the app — that is the word these companies use. In reality, you would have no PF, no paid leave, no job security. The app can deactivate your account tomorrow with no notice. You would be a new kind of unorganised worker — the **gig-economy worker**. How India treats workers like you in the future is one of the biggest questions before our policy-makers right now.

## What About Women's Work?

**Child:** The chapter says women's housework doesn't count in GDP. That sounds unfair. Why is it that way?

**Mother:** It is one of the strangest features of how economists count things, and it is finally being questioned. Let me explain.

GDP counts the value of **goods and services produced for the market** — that is, things you produce **to be sold for money**. A restaurant cooks a meal for ₹400, it counts as ₹400 of GDP. A mother cooks the same meal for her family at home, it counts as ₹0 of GDP.

A creche minds a child for ₹500 a day — it counts. A grandmother minds the same child — it counts as nothing. A tailor stitches a shirt — counts. A mother stitches the same shirt at home — does not count. A nurse cares for an elderly person — counts. A daughter-in-law does the same care — does not count.

The line is **market transaction**. If money changed hands, it counts. If not, it does not.

**Child:** But why?

**Mother:** Historically, economists wanted a measure of **commercial activity** for taxation and trade. Household work was assumed to be too varied, too hard to value, too private. So they left it out. But the consequence — especially in countries like India where women do **enormous amounts of unpaid work** — is that we systematically **undervalue what women do**.

Indian time-use surveys suggest the average Indian woman spends **5-6 hours a day** on unpaid housework and care. The average Indian man spends **less than 1 hour**. Over a year, a woman does roughly **2000 hours of unpaid work**. If we valued it at even ₹100 per hour, that is ₹2 lakh per woman per year — never counted, never paid, never acknowledged in any economic statistic.

And it is not just symbolic. Because this work is invisible:
- We don't invest in things that would reduce it — piped water at home, gas connections, washing machines, creches.
- Women cannot easily take paid work, because the unpaid work is theirs to do.
- India's **Female Labour Force Participation Rate** — the percentage of women who do paid work — is one of the lowest in the world, around 25-30%. In Bangladesh it is around 40%, in Vietnam 70%, in Indonesia 55%. Indian women are not doing less work; they are doing more unpaid work than paid.

**Child:** Can this change?

**Mother:** Yes, in three ways.

**First — share the work.** Men and boys, including you, will need to do their share of housework and care. Cooking, cleaning, caring is **not a women's job**; it is a household's job.

**Second — make work easier.** Piped water, gas, washing machines, dishwashers, creches, eldercare services. These are not luxuries; they free women to work for pay, to study, to rest.

**Third — measure it.** Some countries (UK, Australia, Mexico) now publish "satellite accounts" estimating the value of unpaid household work. India is slowly moving in that direction. What is measured tends to be valued.

The day daadi's twelve-hour day shows up somewhere in an official statistic, even with an estimated rupee value, is the day India begins to see her work as work.

## What Should I Take From This?

**Child:** Okay, this chapter had a lot. If I had to remember three or four things forever, what should they be?

**Mother:** Let me give you the four.

**One — Economic activity comes in three forms: primary, secondary, tertiary.** Primary takes from nature. Secondary transforms it. Tertiary serves it all. Almost every job you will encounter fits one of these. The next time you look around a street, see if you can spot all three sectors at work.

**Two — In India, where people work and what they earn are mismatched.** Most Indians still work in agriculture; most of India's income comes from services. That mismatch is the source of much of our poverty, our migration, our jobless-growth headlines, and our urgent need for "Make in India" and skill development.

**Three — Look at the kind of job, not just the sector.** A delivery boy and a software engineer are both in services, but one has PF, gratuity, paid leave, and a clean office, while the other has none of these. **Organised vs unorganised** is a second axis. **Public vs private** is a third. A complete picture needs all three.

**Four — What is unmeasured tends to be unvalued.** The unpaid work of women — cooking, cleaning, care — is enormous but invisible in GDP. A serious view of the Indian economy has to **see what the statistics hide**.

**Child:** That last one feels almost philosophical.

**Mother:** It is. Economics teaches you to look at the numbers. A good economics education teaches you to look at the **lives behind the numbers**. The next time someone says "India grew at 7%," you should be able to ask: which sector, whose work, organised or not, men or women, and what was hidden from the count.

That is what an educated citizen does.

**Child:** Okay, I will try. One last thing — what should I read or do next?

**Mother:** Three small things to keep this alive.

(1) Look at the people who work in your house — the cook, the cleaner, the driver, the security guard. Find out whether they have a contract, whether they get PF, whether they get any paid leave. You will see organised vs unorganised in five minutes.

(2) Ask your father or mother where the family income comes from. Is it salary, business, pension, rent, farm, freelance? Each is from a sector. Each has a different kind of security.

(3) Next time you order food from Swiggy or Zomato, look at the delivery person. He is a worker in the **gig economy** — the newest kind. Watch how India treats him over the next ten years. That will tell you what kind of economy we are becoming.

**Child:** Got it, Maa. Thanks.

**Mother:** You're welcome. Now go finish your worksheet. That counts as your share of the household economy today.
