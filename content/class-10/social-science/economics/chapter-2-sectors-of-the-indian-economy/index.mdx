## A Country at Work

Step out of any Indian railway station early in the morning and look around.

A vegetable seller is unloading crates of tomatoes from a small truck. A young man on a bike is delivering food from a cloud kitchen. A construction labourer is mixing cement on a half-built apartment block across the road. A schoolteacher is rushing into an auto, balancing a tiffin and a stack of notebooks. A villager has come to the city to sell jaggery his family pressed from sugarcane last week. A software engineer at the café upstairs is typing code on her laptop, working for a client in Germany.

All of them are working. All of them are part of the **Indian economy**. But they are doing very different kinds of work.

How do economists make sense of all this? They use a simple but powerful idea: **classify economic activity into sectors**. Once you classify the work, you can ask the big questions — who earns how much, who employs how many, where the country is heading.

This chapter is about that classification, and the puzzles it reveals. The biggest puzzle of all is this: in India, **the sector where most people work** is not the same as **the sector that produces the most wealth**. That mismatch — between workers and value — shapes almost every economic problem India faces today.

## What Is a Sector, Anyway?

A **sector** is a group of economic activities that have something deep in common. Economists usually split all of economic activity into **three sectors**, based on what kind of work happens and where the value comes from.

**Primary sector** — when we directly use **natural resources** to produce goods. Farming, fishing, dairy, forestry, mining. The earth (or the sea, or the forest) does most of the giving; the worker harvests it. Wheat from the field, fish from the river, iron ore from the mine.

**Secondary sector** — when we **change raw materials into other forms** through some kind of making, processing, or manufacturing. A spinning mill turns cotton into yarn. A bakery turns wheat flour into bread. A car factory turns steel sheets into cars. This is the world of **factories and workshops**, of value being added through human work, energy, and machines.

**Tertiary sector** — when we do not produce a thing at all, but provide a **service** that supports the other two sectors and supports each other. A truck driver moves vegetables from village to city. A bank lends money to a factory. A teacher trains the next workforce. A doctor keeps the engineer healthy enough to write code. This is also called the **service sector**.

A useful way to remember it: the primary sector **takes** from nature, the secondary sector **transforms** what was taken, and the tertiary sector **moves it, manages it, finances it, repairs it, and teaches everyone how to do all of it**.

## Why Three? Aren't There More?

In one sense, yes — economists sometimes break things further. **Information technology**, **knowledge industries**, **finance**, and **tourism** are sometimes called the **quaternary** or **quinary** sector. But for a Class 10 understanding, the three-sector model is enough. It captures the big structural story of any economy.

It also captures something historical. Most countries, when very poor, are mostly primary. As they industrialise, the secondary sector grows. As they get richer, the tertiary sector dominates. So the **shift from primary to secondary to tertiary** is itself a story of economic development.

India is in the middle of that story — but in a strange position, as we will see.

## The Story Told By GDP

How do we measure how much each sector produces? Through **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** — the total value of all final goods and services produced inside a country in a year.

Why "final"? Because if you counted everything, you would double-count. The cotton farmer's cotton is counted in the cost of the shirt; counting them both separately would inflate the total. So GDP counts only the **final value** delivered to the end-user.

In India today, the three sectors contribute roughly:

- **Primary (agriculture and allied)** — about **16-17%** of GDP
- **Secondary (manufacturing, mining, construction, electricity)** — about **27-29%** of GDP
- **Tertiary (services)** — about **53-55%** of GDP

So services produce more than half of everything India earns. Industry produces a little under a third. Agriculture, despite all the attention it gets in our politics, produces less than a fifth.

If GDP were the only number that mattered, India would be called a "service economy." And in one sense it is.

But GDP is only half the story. The other half is **who is doing the producing**.

## The Story Told By Employment

How many people work in each sector? In India today, roughly:

- **Primary** — about **42-45%** of all workers
- **Secondary** — about **25%** of all workers
- **Tertiary** — about **30-32%** of all workers

Stop and look at this. **45% of all working Indians produce just 16% of India's GDP**. That is one of the most important sentences in this chapter. **One sector employs nearly half the country but earns less than a fifth of the country's income**.

The opposite is also true. Services employ about a third of workers but earn more than half of GDP. A bank employee, a software engineer, a chartered accountant — each produces, on average, much more value per person than a farmer producing rice on a half-acre plot.

This gap — high employment, low GDP share in agriculture; low employment, high GDP share in services — is the **central puzzle of the Indian economy**. It explains why farmers' incomes are low. It explains migration to cities. It explains why "Make in India" matters. It explains why every government promises **jobs, jobs, jobs**.

## Why So Many Indians Still Farm

Why have Indians not moved out of agriculture in larger numbers, the way (say) the British or Americans did during their industrialisations?

**First, land is still there but factories are not yet enough.** Britain industrialised in the 19th century — millions of villagers moved to mills in Manchester and Liverpool. India started serious industrialisation only after 1947, and even then, our manufacturing sector has grown slowly. There simply aren't enough factory jobs for the population leaving agriculture.

**Second, services jobs require skills.** A worker leaving a farm cannot walk into a call centre, a hospital, or a coding job without training. The services sector that is growing fast in India is the **high-skill** services sector — software, finance, professional services. The mass of unskilled workers cannot enter it easily.

**Third, family land creates a safety net.** Even a 1-acre plot is something. If a labourer in the city loses his job, he can go back to the village and help on the family field. The land **absorbs** workers it does not really need — they stay in agriculture not because the farm needs them but because they have nowhere else productive to go.

This third reason brings us to one of the most important ideas in the chapter — **disguised unemployment**.

## Unemployment, Underemployment, Disguised Unemployment

These three words sound similar. They are not.

**Unemployment** is the obvious case. A person is willing and able to work, looking for work, but **has no work**. He goes to the labour chowk every morning, no one hires him, he comes home in the evening empty-handed. He is unemployed in the simple sense.

**Underemployment** is when a person works, but **not as much or as well as he could**. A trained mechanical engineer driving a taxi is underemployed — he has the skill for more, but the system has given him less. A graduate working as a peon is underemployed. A construction labourer who finds work only 15 days a month is also underemployed — his time and labour are not being fully used.

**Disguised unemployment** is the trickiest. It happens when **more people are working on a task than are actually needed**. The extra workers appear to be employed — they show up, they do something, they get a share of the family income — but their contribution is essentially zero. If they left, the output would not fall.

Classic example: a family of five works on a small farm. If only three were working, the same crop would still grow. Two are surplus. They appear "employed" but they are really hidden unemployed. The farm is the **only place they can go**, so the farm absorbs them, and their existence is disguised by the fact that they are "working."

Disguised unemployment is **enormous in Indian agriculture**. It is the reason why pulling some workers out of farming — into factories, into services, into other productive work — would actually **raise** average farm output per person. The challenge is creating the alternative work for them to move to.

## How Big Is the Problem?

There are no perfect numbers, but estimates suggest **tens of millions of Indians** are unemployed, underemployed, or disguised unemployed. The official unemployment rate (the simple one) often looks small — sometimes 6-8%. But the **real picture is much worse** once you include underemployment and disguised unemployment.

This is why headlines about "India's GDP grew 7%" can feel disconnected from ordinary lives. Output is growing, but jobs are growing more slowly. Economists call this **jobless growth** — output rises faster than employment. The benefits of growth go to those already inside the formal economy; the millions outside it remain stuck.

## MGNREGA — The Right to Work

In 2005, Parliament passed a remarkable law: the **Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act**, usually called **MGNREGA** (earlier just NREGA).

What does it do? It says: **every rural household has a legal right to 100 days of paid work per year**, on demand, from the government. If the work is not provided within 15 days of application, the household receives an unemployment allowance.

The work is usually **public-asset creation** — digging ponds, building roads, planting trees, deepening wells, building check dams, soil-conservation work. The wage is paid directly into bank accounts (since reforms in the 2010s) at a notified rate per day.

Why is MGNREGA significant?

**It treats work as a right.** Until MGNREGA, employment in India was something the market provided or did not provide; the state had no obligation. MGNREGA changed that. For rural Indians, **work became a legal entitlement**, not a favour.

**It addresses underemployment in agriculture directly.** A small farmer who finds work on his own field for only 50 days a year can now legally claim another 100 days of paid government work. His total earning rises. He stays in the village instead of migrating to a city slum.

**It creates rural assets.** Even imperfectly implemented, MGNREGA has built lakhs of wells, ponds, and roads across rural India. These help the village in the long run, not just on the day of payment.

**It has flaws too.** Wages are often delayed. Corruption in muster rolls (fake worker entries) happens. The work is sometimes ill-designed and the assets crumble. In some states implementation is much better than others. But despite these problems, MGNREGA is one of the largest **right-to-work** programmes anywhere in the world.

You will hear MGNREGA debated all the time — too expensive, too small, too leaky, too important to scale back. The debate is itself a sign that India has accepted, at some level, that **employment is a public concern, not just a private one**.

## Organised vs Unorganised — The Other Big Divide

So far we have looked at sectors by what kind of work happens (primary/secondary/tertiary). But there is **another way** of classifying — by what kind of **employment terms** the worker faces. This split is between the **organised** (or formal) and **unorganised** (or informal) sectors.

**Organised sector** workers have:
- A formal **contract** of employment, often in writing.
- **Regular salary**, usually paid through a bank account.
- **Fixed working hours**, with overtime pay if they work longer.
- **Paid leave** — casual leave, sick leave, earned leave, holidays.
- **Job security** — they cannot be dismissed at whim; due process applies.
- **Social security benefits** — Provident Fund (PF), gratuity, pension, medical insurance (ESI), maternity leave.
- **Coverage under labour laws** — minimum wage, equal pay, safe workplace, the right to form unions.

**Unorganised sector** workers usually have **none of these**:
- They work on **verbal arrangement**, sometimes day-to-day.
- They are paid in cash, often daily, often less than the legal minimum.
- Working hours are whatever the employer demands — often 10-12 hours.
- A "leave" is a day without earning.
- They can be told to stop coming tomorrow.
- No PF, no gratuity, no ESI, no maternity leave.
- Labour laws on paper, but rarely enforced for them.

Who is in each? In India, **over 90% of workers are in the unorganised sector**. This includes most farmers, most agricultural labourers, most construction workers, street vendors, domestic helpers, small shopkeepers, drivers, autorickshaw operators, beedi rollers, brick-kiln workers, most home-based workers. The organised sector is mostly government employees, employees of large private companies, banks, factories above a certain size, IT firms, large services firms.

This is the **second invisible divide** in the Indian economy. Two workers may both be "employed in services" — one is a chartered accountant with PF, gratuity, paid leave and an Uber-pickup home from the office; another is the security guard outside that office building, working 12-hour shifts on a verbal arrangement for ₹14,000 a month with no benefits. Both are "in the tertiary sector." But their working lives are completely different.

## Protections for the Unorganised

Recognising this, the Indian government has slowly built some protections for unorganised workers:

- **Minimum Wages Act** — in principle, no worker should earn below the state-notified minimum wage. In practice, enforcement is weak.
- **Unorganised Workers' Social Security Act, 2008** — gives entitlement to certain welfare schemes.
- **Pradhan Mantri schemes** for unorganised workers — life insurance (PMJJBY), accident insurance (PMSBY), pension (PM-SYM, Atal Pension Yojana).
- **e-Shram portal** — a national database to register unorganised workers and route benefits.
- **Code on Wages, 2019** — consolidates wage laws and extends some protections.
- **Code on Social Security, 2020** — recognises gig and platform workers (Ola, Uber, Swiggy, Zomato drivers) and proposes a fund for them.

These are steps in the right direction, but the gap is huge. **Formalisation of the workforce** — moving more workers into organised, secure jobs — is one of India's biggest long-term economic challenges.

## Public Sector and Private Sector

A third way of splitting the economy is by **who owns the activity**.

The **public sector** is owned by the **government**. Activities and enterprises here include the Indian Railways, India Post, the Reserve Bank of India, public-sector banks (SBI, PNB, Bank of Baroda), Indian Oil, ONGC, Coal India, NTPC, BHEL, AIIMS, government schools, government hospitals, the Indian Army, defence research organisations. The goal of the public sector is not just profit — it is also **public service**, **strategic interest**, and **development of areas where private firms will not invest** (rural banks, remote railway lines, atomic energy).

The **private sector** is owned by **individuals or companies**, where the main goal is profit. From small shops to giants like Tata, Reliance, Infosys, Mahindra, ITC, HUL, this is the bulk of Indian business.

Some activities are best left to the private sector — they are competitive, they need fast decisions, profit is a useful discipline. Others must remain public — defence, basic education, basic healthcare, social safety nets, certain infrastructure. A wise economy uses **both**, in their right places.

Since 1991, India has shifted some activities from public to private — this is called **disinvestment** or **privatisation**. The debate over how much to privatise is one of the long-running arguments of Indian economics.

## Women's Work — The Unpaid Economy

There is one more dimension that GDP misses, and it is huge: **the unpaid work of women**.

A typical Indian woman cooks two or three meals every day. She washes clothes, cleans the house, fetches water (in many villages), gathers firewood, looks after children, cares for the elderly. In rural homes she may also tend cattle, milk them, work on the family field at peak times, sell vegetables in the local market, and help in small home-based work.

How much of this is counted in GDP? **Almost none**. If she cooked the same meal for a paying customer in a restaurant, it would be GDP. If she cooks it for her family, it is invisible. If a nurse looked after the elderly parent for a fee, it would be GDP. If the daughter-in-law does it, it is invisible.

This is not a quirk; it is a global feature of the way GDP is designed. The system measures **market work** — work done for pay. **Non-market work**, done at home for the family, is excluded by definition.

In India, women do an enormous amount of non-market work. Time-use surveys suggest Indian women spend **5-6 hours a day** on unpaid household and care work, while men spend less than 1 hour. This means **a large part of the real Indian economy is performed by women and never appears in any economic statistic**.

This is not just an academic issue. It affects:

- **How we value women's work.** What is unmeasured tends to be unvalued.
- **Women's labour-force participation.** India's official **female labour force participation rate** (women in paid work) is among the lowest in the world — around 25-30%, while in countries like Bangladesh, Vietnam, or Indonesia it is much higher. Part of the reason is that women's time is absorbed in unpaid work, with no way to monetise or share it.
- **Family income.** Two earners would lift millions of households out of poverty. But the unpaid work has to be either shared (by men) or supported by services (creches, eldercare, kitchen appliances, piped water) for women to take up paid work.

A truly modern Indian economy will eventually have to confront this. **GDP will need a companion measure for unpaid care work**, and household labour will need to be shared more equally. Some countries (UK, Australia, Mexico) already publish satellite accounts that estimate the value of unpaid household work; India is slowly moving in this direction.

## Where Is India Headed?

Putting all this together, what does the future of the Indian economy look like?

**More services growth.** The tertiary sector will likely keep expanding — fintech, IT services, e-commerce, healthcare, education, tourism, logistics. India has a comparative advantage in services that use English, education, and digital infrastructure.

**A push to grow manufacturing.** "Make in India" reflects a long-standing concern — that India skipped the **manufacturing-led development** stage. China became a manufacturing powerhouse and lifted hundreds of millions of villagers into factory jobs. India largely missed that wave. Building manufacturing now is harder (because automation has reduced labour needs), but the country is trying through Production Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes for electronics, semiconductors, automobiles, pharma, textiles.

**Shrinking — but not collapsing — agriculture.** Agriculture will not disappear. India has to feed 140 crore people. But the **share of workers** in agriculture must fall, while productivity per farmer must rise. This means fewer, larger, more mechanised farms, alongside food processing, dairy, horticulture, and agri-services that absorb people who leave traditional farming.

**Formalisation.** Slowly, more workers should move into organised employment with contracts and benefits. Aadhaar, the Jan Dhan banking push, e-Shram registration, and digital payment systems are tools to extend the formal economy.

**The gig and platform economy.** A new category of work is emerging — Ola/Uber drivers, Swiggy/Zomato delivery, Urban Company services, Amazon Flex deliveries. These workers do not fit the old organised-unorganised split — they have apps and ratings and digital payments, but no contract or PF or paid leave. They are sometimes called the **fourth category**, and how to regulate and protect them is one of the hottest economic policy questions of the 2020s.

## The Big Picture

If you remember three things from this chapter:

**One** — economic activity has three sectors: primary (nature), secondary (making), tertiary (services). India's GDP is now dominated by services, while employment is still dominated by agriculture. That mismatch is the source of much of our economic stress.

**Two** — the work itself has two more cuts: organised vs unorganised (formal vs informal), and public vs private (government vs business). Most Indians work in the **unorganised**, **private** part of the economy, with little security.

**Three** — the official economy is only half the story. Disguised unemployment hides millions of underused workers in agriculture. Women's unpaid work is invisible in GDP but enormous in real life. A serious economic policy has to see all of it.

The next time you walk through an Indian street and see all the different kinds of work going on — the food cart, the courier, the salon, the office, the factory across the road — you will be able to **see** the sectors, the divides, and the puzzles they carry with them. That is what this chapter has tried to give you: not just definitions, but **eyes for the Indian economy**.

## Apply — See It For Yourself

Once you have read about the sectors, the gaps, and the unpaid economy, look at your own world. Three short stories to think about.

**Story 1 — Daadi's "Unpaid" Day.**
Asha's grandmother, 68, wakes at 5 AM. She lights the stove, makes tea for everyone, sweeps two rooms, cooks breakfast, packs three tiffins, washes utensils, drops the youngest grandchild at the bus stop. By noon she has cooked lunch, mended a torn shirt, sun-dried the pickles, and called the cleaner about a broken tap. In the evening she helps her grandson with homework, cooks dinner, and finally sits down at 9 PM. She has worked 12 hours. None of it shows up in any GDP figure. Daadi sometimes says, "I don't really do anything." Her family laughs. Is she joking — or is she repeating what the economy has told her her whole life?

**Story 2 — The Ola Driver's Real Income.**
Suraj drives an Ola Sedan in Bengaluru. The app shows ₹65,000 earned this month. Sounds good — until he subtracts: ₹22,000 EMI on the car, ₹14,000 fuel, ₹3,000 maintenance, ₹2,500 insurance, ₹1,500 mobile and data, ₹4,000 Ola commission already deducted. Real take-home: about ₹18,000 for 26 days of 11-hour shifts. He has no PF, no gratuity, no paid leave. If he gets dengue next week, he loses the EMI money. The app says he is a "partner." Is he in the organised sector or the unorganised one?

**Story 3 — MGNREGA in Your District.**
At the panchayat office in a village in Bihar, 84 households have applied for MGNREGA work this month. The official says 64 will get it within 15 days; the others "next month, probably." The site is a check-dam being built across a stream. Daily wage: ₹245. Payment was 22 days late last cycle. Workers say the dam was useful last monsoon — water lasted longer in the wells. They also say half the muster roll has fake names that someone in the panchayat is keeping the wages of. Both can be true. Is MGNREGA succeeding or failing? Depends what you measure.

### DIY — Three Field Tasks

**(1) The Sector Interview.** Choose **5 working people** you know — a relative, a neighbour, a shopkeeper, a driver, a teacher, anyone. Ask each: What do you do? Who pays you? Do you have a contract? Do you get PF, paid leave, or insurance? What hours do you work? Then classify each into primary/secondary/tertiary and organised/unorganised. Are you surprised at the distribution in your sample?

**(2) The Unpaid Audit.** Pick **one adult woman in your house** (your mother, aunt, grandmother, didi, bhabhi). With her permission, list everything she does in a single weekday — every cooking, cleaning, fetching, caring, advising task — with how long it takes. Estimate her **weekly unpaid hours**. Compare with one adult male in the same house. Don't argue, just record.

**(3) The Family Income Map.** For **one month**, draw a simple chart of where your household's money comes from. Salary? Pension? Shop? Rent? Farm? Side-business? MGNREGA? Remittances from a relative working in another city or abroad? Tally each. Classify each source into a sector. What does it tell you about which sector your family really lives in?

### Metadata Tag

Apply type: **Economy in your home and neighbourhood**. Skills used: **Observation, classification, careful listening, basic arithmetic**. Approximate time: **30 minutes for Story discussion + 2 hours per DIY task**.

### Show What You Found

Once you have done one of the DIY tasks, share what you found. Make a simple poster, a small slide deck, a one-page summary, or even a short video. Show in your sample:

- How many were in each sector
- How many were in organised vs unorganised
- For the unpaid audit — the hours per week, and what surprises you noticed
- For the family income map — the share each source contributed

You do not need fancy graphs. A neat handwritten chart on chart paper is enough. The act of **seeing your own data** is the point.

### A Question to Keep Asking

When a news headline says **"India's GDP grew at 7%"**, ask yourself:

> *Which sector grew? Whose work created that growth? Did organised-sector workers benefit, or unorganised ones too? Did women's unpaid work get any easier? Did farmers' incomes rise? Did the Ola driver get a holiday?*

A growth number is one number. Behind it lies an entire country of different sectors, different workers, different lives. The educated citizen learns to **read behind the headline**.
