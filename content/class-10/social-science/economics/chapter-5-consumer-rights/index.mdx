## The Buyer Is Not at the Seller's Mercy

Imagine your daadi sends you to the kirana shop for one kilogram of toor dal. The shopkeeper weighs out the dal, takes the money, and hands you the packet. At home, your daadi puts the dal on her old kitchen weighing scale — and it reads 870 grams. Not one kilogram. **130 grams short.**

What can you do?

You could shout at the shopkeeper. You could refuse to buy from him again. You could complain to your neighbours. But can you legally **force him** to give you what you paid for? Can you punish him for cheating? Can you stop him from cheating the next customer?

The answer, in modern India, is **YES.** And the reason is a story about how ordinary buyers — people like your parents, your daadi, you — slowly won the right to be treated fairly. That story is what this chapter is about.

In a marketplace, the seller knows everything: the real weight, the real price, the real ingredients, the real expiry date. The buyer often knows nothing. This **information gap** is what makes cheating possible. **Consumer rights** exist to close that gap — to make sure the buyer is not at the mercy of the seller.

## What "Consumer" Actually Means

A **consumer** is anyone who buys goods or services for their own use — not to resell. Your mother buying onions is a consumer. Your father paying the electricity bill is a consumer. You buying a notebook is a consumer. A shopkeeper buying onions to sell again is **not** a consumer in this legal sense — he is a trader.

When we say "consumer rights", we mean the rights of this final buyer. The person at the end of the chain. The one who pays the money and gets nothing if cheated.

## How Sellers Take Advantage

In any marketplace, four kinds of unfairness keep happening:

(1) **Underweighing.** The shopkeeper's scale is rigged. The "one kilogram" packet has 870 grams. The petrol pump dispenses 4.7 litres instead of 5.

(2) **Adulteration.** Milk is mixed with water. Spices are mixed with brick powder. Honey is mixed with sugar syrup. Petrol is mixed with kerosene.

(3) **Overcharging.** Items priced above the MRP. "Cooling charges" added on top of MRP for cold drinks. Restaurant bills with mysterious extra amounts.

(4) **False or misleading claims.** A face cream that promises "fair skin in 7 days". A coaching ad showing toppers who never went there. A turmeric brand calling itself "100% pure" when lab tests show otherwise.

There is also a darker fifth category — **dangerous goods**. Expired medicines on pharmacy shelves. Toys with toxic paint. Pressure cookers without safety valves. Helmets that crack on first impact. Here the cheating is not just about money. It is about life.

The honest seller is the rule, not the exception, in most markets. But even a small minority of dishonest sellers can do enormous damage if there is no system to catch them. **Consumer rights are the system.**

## A Brief History of Consumer Movements

The modern idea of consumer rights is surprisingly recent. For most of human history, the rule was **"caveat emptor"** — Latin for **"let the buyer beware"**. If you got cheated, that was your problem. You should have been more careful.

This started changing in the **United States in the 1960s**. A young lawyer named **Ralph Nader** wrote a book called *Unsafe at Any Speed* (1965), exposing how American car companies were knowingly making dangerous vehicles. The book triggered an outrage. The US Congress passed safety laws. A worldwide **consumer movement** was born.

In India, the consumer movement grew slowly. People were used to being cheated. Complaining seemed pointless — who would listen? Through the 1970s and early 1980s, citizens' groups, journalists, and lawyers pushed the government to act.

The breakthrough came in **1986**. The Indian Parliament passed the **Consumer Protection Act (COPRA)** — one of the most important consumer laws anywhere in the developing world. For the first time, ordinary Indian buyers had **legally enforceable rights**, and a separate court system to enforce them.

COPRA was modernised in **2019**, with a new Consumer Protection Act that updated the law for the digital age — covering online shopping, misleading celebrity endorsements, and product liability. A new body, the **Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA)**, was created to act on its own when consumers are being cheated at scale.

## The Six Rights Every Indian Consumer Has

Under the Consumer Protection Act, every Indian consumer has six core rights. Learn them — they are your shield in every marketplace transaction.

### 1. Right to Be Informed

The seller cannot hide things from you. The packaging must clearly show:

- **Price (MRP)** — the maximum the seller can charge
- **Weight or quantity**
- **Manufacturing date and expiry date**
- **List of ingredients**
- **Name and address of the manufacturer**
- **Customer care details**
- **Warnings**, if any (e.g., "for external use only", "keep away from children")

For medicines, the rules are even stricter — composition, dosage, side effects, batch number, manufacturing licence, expiry. For food, the FSSAI licence number must be printed.

When a builder sells you a flat, he must disclose carpet area honestly. When a bank sells you a loan, it must tell you the **effective interest rate**, not hide it in fine print. When Zomato shows you a restaurant, the menu and prices must be accurate.

The right to be informed is the foundation. Without it, none of the other rights can work — you cannot choose, refuse, or complain about what you do not understand.

### 2. Right to Choose

The seller cannot **force** you to buy something you do not want.

Examples of this right being violated:

- A gas agency that refuses to deliver your cylinder unless you also buy their "compulsory" stove insurance.
- A coaching institute that won't sell you the Class 12 course unless you also pay for the Class 11 course.
- A car dealer who delays delivery unless you take the "extended warranty" and "anti-rust treatment" packages.
- A hospital that refuses you a particular doctor unless you also book a private room.

These are all called **tied selling** or **bundling**, and they violate your right to choose. The same right also means you must have a real choice between sellers — which is why monopolies (one seller, no alternative) make consumers uncomfortable and need government regulation.

### 3. Right to Safety

The goods and services you buy must not endanger your life or health.

This is why:

- Pressure cookers must have **ISI marks** certifying safety valves work.
- Electrical goods must meet standards so they don't catch fire.
- Helmets sold for two-wheelers must pass impact tests.
- Children's toys must not contain lead paint.
- Restaurants must follow hygiene rules.
- Hospitals must follow medical protocols.

When a product is defective and causes injury — say, a phone battery that explodes, or a contaminated cough syrup that kills children — the manufacturer can be sued under **product liability**. The 2019 Act made this much stronger. Manufacturers cannot hide behind small print saying "company not responsible".

The infamous **Bhopal gas tragedy of 1984**, where thousands died from a leak at the Union Carbide plant, was one trigger for India's consumer protection laws. The lesson: companies that endanger lives must be held accountable.

### 4. Right to Seek Redressal

If you are cheated, you have the right to a **remedy** — a refund, a replacement, repair, or compensation.

You do not have to suffer in silence. You do not have to "let it go" because the amount is small. You do not have to be rich or educated to complain.

The Consumer Protection Act sets up a **three-tier consumer court system** (described in detail below) specifically so that ordinary people, with small grievances, can get fast and cheap justice — without lawyers, without long delays.

### 5. Right to Consumer Education

You have the right to **know your rights**. Sounds circular, but it is profound. Most Indians do not know what they are entitled to. They get cheated and never even realise it is illegal.

This is why consumer rights are part of school textbooks (like this one!). This is why the government runs awareness campaigns — *Jago Grahak Jago* ("Wake up, consumer, wake up") has been on TV and radio since 2005. This is why NGOs like Consumer VOICE, CUTS International, and Mumbai Grahak Panchayat publish magazines and run workshops.

A consumer who does not know her rights cannot use them. Education makes the rest of the rights real.

### 6. Right to Be Heard (Right to Representation)

When governments make rules that affect consumers — about food safety, drug pricing, telecom tariffs, electricity bills — they must **consult consumers**.

This is why consumer representatives sit on bodies like TRAI (telecom), SEBI (stock market), IRDAI (insurance), and FSSAI (food safety). Their job: speak for the buyer, who would otherwise be drowned out by powerful sellers and lobbies.

In practice, this right is weaker than the others — corporate lobbies are usually louder than consumer groups. But the principle matters: policy should listen to those affected by it, not just those who profit from it.

## ISI, Agmark, Hallmark, FPO — The Indian Standards Marks

How can you tell, just by looking at a packet on a shelf, whether the product has been tested for quality? Through **standards marks** — small symbols printed by independent agencies that certify the product meets minimum quality and safety levels.

The four most important marks in India:

(1) **ISI mark** — issued by the **Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS)**. Used on industrial and consumer goods: electrical appliances, LPG cylinders, helmets, pressure cookers, packaged drinking water, cement, steel rods. Look for the ISI logo (a stylised "ISI" inside a triangle) with a licence number underneath.

(2) **Agmark** — issued by the **Directorate of Marketing and Inspection** under the Ministry of Agriculture. Used on agricultural produce: ghee, honey, spices, wheat flour, vegetable oils. The Agmark certifies grade and purity.

(3) **Hallmark** — issued by BIS for **gold and silver jewellery**. A hallmarked gold ornament will show the BIS logo, the purity (e.g., 22K916 means 91.6% gold), and the jeweller's identification mark. Since 2021, hallmarking has been **mandatory** for gold jewellery in most Indian districts. Before that, jewellers could easily sell "22 carat" gold that was actually only 18 carat — daadi lost lakhs that way over a lifetime.

(4) **FPO mark** — issued under the **Fruit Products Order, 1955**, for processed fruit products: jams, juices, sauces, pickles, squash. Mandatory.

There are others — **Eco-Mark** for environmentally friendly products, **FSSAI licence number** for all packaged food, **BEE star rating** for energy efficiency of appliances. But ISI, Agmark, Hallmark, and FPO are the four to remember.

**Important**: Look for these marks, and look for the **licence number** below them. Fake ISI logos with no licence number are common — they look right but mean nothing. A real ISI mark always has a unique six-digit licence number you can verify on the BIS website.

## The Three-Tier Consumer Court System

This is the part most Indians do not know about — and it is the part that turns rights from paper to power.

If a shopkeeper, company, or service provider has cheated you, you can file a complaint in a **Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission** (popularly called consumer court). There are three levels:

(1) **District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission (District Forum)** — handles disputes where the **total claim is up to Rs 1 crore**. Every district in India has one.

(2) **State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission** — handles disputes from **Rs 1 crore to Rs 10 crore**, and also hears appeals against District Forum orders.

(3) **National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission (NCDRC)** — handles disputes **above Rs 10 crore**, and hears appeals from State Commissions. Headquartered in Delhi.

(Earlier limits — Rs 20 lakh / Rs 1 crore — were revised upward by the 2019 Act to reflect inflation and the bigger value of modern consumer disputes.)

### What Makes Consumer Courts Different

These are not normal courts. They were designed to be **friendly to ordinary people**:

- **You do not need a lawyer.** You can argue your own case.
- **Filing fee is very small** — sometimes Rs 100 or Rs 200 for small claims.
- **Procedures are simplified.** No complicated legal language required.
- **Cases must be decided within fixed time limits** — usually three to five months at the District level.
- **You can file the complaint where you live**, not just where the seller lives.
- **Now: you can file online**, through the **e-Daakhil portal** launched in 2020.

### What Consumer Courts Can Order

If the consumer court rules in your favour, the seller can be ordered to:

- **Replace the defective product**
- **Repair it free of cost**
- **Refund your money**
- **Pay compensation** for harm, inconvenience, mental agony
- **Stop the unfair practice** (binding on all future customers)
- **Pay punitive damages** if the cheating was deliberate or repeated
- **Withdraw misleading advertisements**

In serious cases, the **CCPA** (Central Consumer Protection Authority) can impose fines up to Rs 10 lakh and even ban celebrities from endorsing products for up to three years if they misled consumers.

## Related Laws That Strengthen Consumer Rights

Consumer protection is not the work of just one law. Several others fit together:

(1) **Right to Information Act, 2005 (RTI).** Lets any citizen ask any public department for information. If your electricity bill seems wrong, you can RTI the electricity board to see how it was calculated. RTI shifted power dramatically toward citizens.

(2) **Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006.** Created the **FSSAI**, which licenses every food business and sets standards for safety, hygiene, labelling. Restaurants and packaged food must comply.

(3) **Legal Metrology Act, 2009.** Regulates weights and measures. Shopkeepers using rigged scales can be prosecuted under this law. It also enforces MRP — the maximum price beyond which a seller cannot charge.

(4) **Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 (RERA).** Protects home buyers from cheating builders — covers delays, false promises, hidden defects.

(5) **Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940** (updated repeatedly). Regulates medicines and cosmetics, including the right to safe and properly labelled medication.

(6) **Information Technology Act, 2000** (rules updated since). Covers online consumer rights — data privacy, e-commerce fraud, fake reviews.

Together, these laws form a web that catches most kinds of cheating — if the consumer knows how to use them.

## Consumer Rights in the Digital Age

When COPRA was written in 1986, no one had heard of Amazon, Zomato, or Paytm. The market then was a kirana shop, a Maruti showroom, a bank with a passbook. The 2019 Act tried to catch up with the digital age — and the law is still racing to keep up with new forms of cheating.

The big new problems:

(1) **E-commerce fraud.** Products that do not match photos. Counterfeit goods sold as branded. Sellers disappearing after taking payment. "Free trial" subscriptions that auto-charge forever. Returns refused or delayed indefinitely.

(2) **Fake reviews.** Sellers paying for five-star reviews. Bots writing thousands of fake testimonials. Genuine bad reviews getting hidden. In 2022, the BIS issued **fake review guidelines** requiring platforms to verify reviewers and label paid reviews.

(3) **Dark patterns.** Tricks built into apps to make you buy more, share more data, or stay subscribed. Examples: hard-to-find "unsubscribe" buttons, fake countdown timers ("only 2 left!"), pre-ticked add-on insurance, forced account creation, "are you sure you want to leave us?" guilt loops. The CCPA banned **13 specific dark patterns** in 2023.

(4) **Misleading influencer ads.** Celebrities and influencers promoting products without disclosing they were paid. ASCI (Advertising Standards Council of India) now requires **#ad** or **#sponsored** tags. The 2019 Act lets the CCPA fine the celebrity and ban them from endorsements if they mislead consumers.

(5) **Data and privacy.** Apps collecting far more data than they need. The **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023** is India's first major law on consumer data — but its full implementation is still being worked out.

(6) **Delivery and gig-platform issues.** Late deliveries, switched products, refund denials, hidden charges — and complicated questions about who is responsible (the seller, the platform, the delivery worker?). The 2019 Act made the **e-commerce platform** liable in many cases, not just the seller.

The digital marketplace is faster, more convenient, and more powerful than the kirana shop. It is also harder to police. The next decade of Indian consumer law will be about catching up.

## What This Chapter Asks of You

Consumer rights are not abstract. They are tools. They sit in the toolbox until you pick them up and use them. Three habits will make you a powerful consumer for life:

(1) **Check before you pay.** MRP, weight, expiry date, ISI/Agmark/Hallmark marks, FSSAI number. Thirty seconds of looking saves thousands of rupees over a lifetime.

(2) **Ask for the bill.** Always. A bill is your proof. No bill, no consumer court case. Many shopkeepers will avoid giving bills to evade tax — and to leave you with no proof of purchase.

(3) **Complain when cheated.** Even small things. Especially small things. If everyone complains about the underweighed dal, the shopkeeper stops doing it. The cheats stop when the cheating stops being profitable. Your complaint is a brick in the wall.

A market works for buyers only when buyers act like they have rights. Now you know yours.

## Apply: Real-World Practice

### Stories

**Daadi's weighing scale.** Daadi sends her grandson Rohit to the kirana shop near their Pune home for one kg of toor dal. At home, she weighs it on her old kitchen scale: 870 grams. Same shop, same trick, every week. Rohit takes a photo of the bill (Rs 130/kg) and the weighed dal (870g) and shows it to his father, who files a complaint with the local **Legal Metrology Department**. Inspectors check the shop's scale — it has been tampered with. The shopkeeper is fined Rs 25,000 and his licence is suspended. Other customers in the colony stop being cheated too.

**Mumbai woman versus LIC.** Mrs. Kulkarni's father had taken an LIC policy in 1995. After he passed away in 2022, she filed a claim for Rs 2.4 lakh. LIC kept delaying — first asking for documents she had already submitted, then claiming the original policy was lost. After eight months of no response, she filed a complaint at the **Mumbai District Consumer Forum** for Rs 200, without a lawyer. The Forum heard the case in four sittings, ruled in her favour, and ordered LIC to pay the claim **plus Rs 25,000 compensation** for mental agony and Rs 5,000 for legal expenses. Total time from filing to payment: seven months.

**Dark patterns on Indian e-commerce apps.** Arjun, a Class 11 student in Bangalore, wanted to buy a book for Rs 350 on a popular shopping app. By checkout, the total was Rs 612: a Rs 49 "delivery fee" he could not waive, Rs 39 "convenience charge" he had not noticed, Rs 99 "shipment protection" pre-ticked, Rs 75 for a "membership" he never agreed to. He uninstalled the app and filed a complaint with the **National Consumer Helpline (1915)**. The CCPA later issued notices to multiple e-commerce platforms about exactly these "dark patterns" — sneak-into-basket, pre-checked add-ons, basket sneaking, and bait-and-switch pricing. Several were banned in 2023.

### DIY (Do It Yourself)

Pick any one — or better, all three — and actually do it. Then bring what you learned to class.

(1) **Visit or call a consumer court.** Find the address of your **District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission** (Google "District Consumer Forum [your city]"). Either visit the building during working hours and ask the clerk if you can observe a hearing, or call them and ask how a citizen files a complaint. Note: what does the filing fee look like? How long does a hearing take? What kinds of cases do they hear most?

(2) **Label audit of 10 home products.** Walk around your kitchen, bathroom, and study. Pick 10 packaged products. For each, check for: **ISI mark** (with licence number), **Agmark**, **Hallmark** (jewellery), **FPO**, **FSSAI number**, MRP, manufacturing and expiry dates. Make a table. How many products were missing one or more of these? Which brand was the worst offender?

(3) **Three consumer stories from friends and family.** Interview three people you know — daadi, an uncle, a neighbour, a friend's parent. Ask each: "Have you ever been cheated by a seller or company? What did you do?" Write down what happened, what they tried (if anything), and whether they got their money back. Bring the three stories to class.

### A Question to Keep Asking

The next time you, your family, or a friend pays for something — at a kirana, online, at a hospital, in a restaurant — pause for a moment and ask:

**"If something goes wrong here, what rights do I have, and can I actually use them?"**

If the honest answer is "I don't know" or "Not really" — that is a marketplace that needs work. From buyers like you.
