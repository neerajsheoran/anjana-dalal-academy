**Child:** Maa, why does milk packaging always say "BEST BEFORE"? Can't I just trust the shopkeeper?

**Mother:** That is a wonderful place to start, beta. The "BEST BEFORE" date is not really about trust — it is about **proof**. You see, the shopkeeper may be a kind, honest man. He may genuinely believe that the milk packet he is handing you is fresh. But what if it actually came from a delivery truck that broke down for six hours in the heat? What if it has been sitting on the back of the shelf since last Tuesday? The shopkeeper does not know. *You* do not know. The only person who knows when this milk was packed is the dairy — and the only way to communicate that information to you, the buyer, is **a printed date on the packet**.

That date is one of the smallest, quietest examples of a huge idea: the **right to be informed**. The seller must tell you the truth. You must not have to trust him — you must be able to **check**.

**Child:** But Maa, if a shopkeeper is honest, why do we even need this?

**Mother:** Because in a marketplace, you don't get to choose only honest shopkeepers. You walk into a shop, you don't know the man behind the counter, and you certainly don't know who packed the dal in his sacks. Some shopkeepers are honest. Some are not. **The system has to work even when the seller is dishonest** — otherwise the dishonest ones win, and the honest ones are pushed out of business because cheating is cheaper than not cheating. Consumer rights are the rules that keep the marketplace fair even when sellers cannot all be trusted. It's not about insulting honest shopkeepers — it's about protecting them, and you, from the dishonest ones.

## Why Do Consumers Need Special Rights?

**Child:** Okay, so the marketplace can have cheats. But Maa, can't I just be careful? If I check the milk before buying, isn't that enough?

**Mother:** Sometimes yes, beta. But think carefully — how many things in our house did you and I actually check before buying?

**Child:** Hmm... not many. The milk, the bread expiry. The dal, sometimes.

**Mother:** Exactly. Now think about the medicines in our cabinet. Can you and I look at a cough syrup and tell whether the ingredients are pure? Can we tell whether the helmet your bhaiya bought will actually protect his head if he falls? Can we tell, looking at a gold chain, whether it is really 22 carat or only 18? Can we tell, looking at a flat your father is considering buying, whether the carpet area is what the builder claims?

**Child:** Not really.

**Mother:** This is what economists call the **information gap**. The seller knows everything — the real weight, the real ingredients, the real purity, the real cost. The buyer knows almost nothing. We are not stupid; we just **cannot possibly check** thousands of products from the outside.

So we need someone who *can* check. That is what government testing, ISI marks, FSSAI licences, and consumer laws are all for. Independent agencies test what you cannot. They certify with a mark. And if the mark turns out to be fake, the law punishes the cheat.

**Child:** So consumer rights are not about replacing my carefulness — they are about helping me where I cannot be careful?

**Mother:** Beautifully put, beta. Your alertness still matters. But it is not enough alone. The law has to back you up — because the marketplace is bigger than any one buyer.

**Child:** Why didn't this exist earlier? Like in daadi's time?

**Mother:** It did not exist for most of history. For thousands of years, the rule was **caveat emptor** — Latin for "let the buyer beware." If you got cheated, that was your bad luck. Be smarter next time. But over time, two things changed.

First, products got too complex for ordinary people to inspect. Daadi could probably tell good ghee from bad ghee by smell — she made it at home, after all. But today's processed foods, electronics, medicines, financial products? Nobody can tell from the outside. So putting all the burden on the buyer became unfair.

Second, the consumer movement organised. People in different countries — starting with the US in the 1960s, then spreading — said, "We are tired of being cheated. Sellers profit from the information gap. The law must close it." That is how consumer rights were born.

**Child:** Who exactly started it, Maa?

**Mother:** In the US, the most famous figure was a young lawyer named **Ralph Nader**. In 1965 he wrote a book called *Unsafe at Any Speed*, exposing how American car companies were knowingly making dangerous cars. The book caused huge public anger. The US Congress passed safety laws. From the US, the movement spread to Europe, and then to developing countries like ours. India's big moment came in **1986**, with the Consumer Protection Act.

## What Are the 6 Big Consumer Rights?

**Child:** So COPRA gave Indian buyers rights. What are they?

**Mother:** Six of them. Each one closes a different gap in the marketplace. Let me walk through them with examples from our own life.

**(1) Right to be informed.** Every packet must show you: price, weight, ingredients, manufacturing date, expiry date, manufacturer's name and address. Even the bank loan papers your father signs must show the true interest rate — not hidden in fine print. Without this, the buyer cannot judge. With it, you have a chance.

**(2) Right to choose.** Nobody can force you to buy something extra. If the gas agency says, "I will only deliver your cylinder if you also buy our stove insurance" — that is illegal. If a coaching institute says, "We won't sell you Class 12 prep unless you also take Class 11" — that is illegal. The buyer's right to say *no* to add-ons is sacred.

**Child:** Does that happen, Maa? Bundling extra things in?

**Mother:** Constantly, beta. Especially with services — internet packages, insurance, hospital admissions. You always have the right to refuse the extras. Sellers count on customers not knowing this and just agreeing.

**(3) Right to safety.** Products must not endanger your life. This is why pressure cookers must have safety valves. Why helmets must pass impact tests. Why a phone battery that explodes makes the company legally liable. Remember the Bhopal gas tragedy in 1984? Thousands died because the Union Carbide factory was not safe. That tragedy was one reason India hurried to pass COPRA in 1986.

**(4) Right to seek redressal.** If you are cheated, you have the right to a remedy. A refund. A replacement. Compensation. You do not have to "let it go." The Consumer Protection Act set up a three-tier court system specifically so that ordinary people, with small grievances, can get justice. We'll talk about those courts in a moment.

**(5) Right to consumer education.** You have the right to **know your rights**. This is why these chapters are in your textbook. This is why the government runs the *Jago Grahak Jago* campaign. A consumer who doesn't know her rights cannot use them. Education makes everything else real.

**(6) Right to be heard / right to representation.** When the government makes rules about food safety, drug prices, or telecom tariffs, consumer representatives must sit at the table. The buyer's voice must be in the room — not just the powerful seller's voice.

**Child:** Maa, of all six, which one is the most important?

**Mother:** All six need each other, beta. But if I had to pick one — it would be the first, the right to be informed. Because without information, you cannot choose well, you cannot judge safety, you cannot know when to seek redressal. Information is the foundation. The other five rights are how you act *on* that information.

## What Do ISI, Agmark, and Hallmark Mean?

**Child:** Maa, you always check the bottom of every packet before buying. What exactly are you looking for?

**Mother:** Marks, beta. Three letter codes, each from a different agency, each promising something. Let me show you the four that matter most.

**(1) ISI mark** — this one's from the **Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS)**. You find it on industrial and household goods: electrical appliances, LPG cylinders, helmets, pressure cookers, packaged drinking water, cement, steel rods. The logo looks like a stylised "ISI" inside a triangle, with a six-digit licence number printed underneath. **Always look for that licence number.** A fake ISI without a number is meaningless.

**(2) Agmark** — this one's from the Ministry of Agriculture, for agricultural produce: ghee, honey, spices, edible oils. Agmark grades the purity. Have you noticed why our pure ghee is so much more expensive than the local stuff? One reason is the Agmark grade.

**(3) Hallmark** — this is for **gold and silver jewellery**, from BIS. A hallmarked ornament shows three things: the BIS logo, the purity (like 22K916 meaning 91.6% gold), and the jeweller's mark. Since 2021, hallmarking is **mandatory** for gold jewellery in most Indian districts.

**Child:** Why mandatory only since 2021, Maa? What was happening before?

**Mother:** Beta, before 2021, jewellers could and often did sell "22 carat gold" that was actually 18 or 20 carat. Your daadi told me once that when her gold bangles from the 1970s were melted down decades later, they had three to four carats less purity than the jeweller had claimed. Multiply that across crores of Indian women's lifetime savings in jewellery, and you understand why hallmarking finally became mandatory. It was a quiet, massive theft for decades.

**(4) FPO mark** — for processed fruit products: jams, juices, sauces, pickles. Mandatory under the Fruit Products Order. Plus there's also **FSSAI licence number** on all packaged food, and **BEE star ratings** on appliances for energy efficiency.

**Child:** So when I buy something tomorrow, what should I actually look for?

**Mother:** Quick checklist, beta:
- **Packaged food** → FSSAI number, manufacturing date, expiry date, ingredients list
- **Gold jewellery** → BIS Hallmark, purity number, jeweller's mark
- **Electrical appliances** → ISI mark with licence number, BEE star rating
- **Ghee, honey, spices, oils** → Agmark
- **Pickles, jams, juices** → FPO mark
- **Helmet, pressure cooker** → ISI mark (mandatory; not just nice-to-have)

Thirty seconds of looking, every time you buy something. Over a lifetime, it saves lakhs.

## Can I Actually Go to Consumer Court for Small Things?

**Child:** Maa, even if I know I am cheated — going to court sounds expensive and slow. Is it really worth it for, say, a 130-gram shortage in dal?

**Mother:** This is the part most Indians do not know about — and it is the part that turns paper rights into real power. The consumer courts in India are **not normal courts**. They were specifically designed to be **friendly to ordinary people with small grievances**. Let me explain.

**The three tiers.**

- **District Consumer Commission** — every district has one. Handles disputes up to **Rs 1 crore**.
- **State Consumer Commission** — one in every state. Handles **Rs 1 crore to Rs 10 crore**, plus appeals from districts.
- **National Consumer Commission (NCDRC)** — in Delhi. Handles **above Rs 10 crore**, plus appeals from states.

**Child:** Why three levels?

**Mother:** Because small cases should be heard quickly and locally; big cases need national attention; and if a district forum gets it wrong, you should be able to appeal upward. Three tiers do all three jobs.

**What makes consumer courts different.**

- **You don't need a lawyer.** You can argue your own case.
- **Filing fee is tiny** — sometimes just Rs 100 or Rs 200 for small claims.
- **Procedures are simplified.** No complicated legal language.
- **Time limits are fixed** — usually three to five months at District level.
- **You can file in the city you live in**, not just where the seller is.
- **Since 2020 you can file entirely online**, through the **e-Daakhil** portal.

**Child:** What can the court actually order?

**Mother:** It can order the seller to:
- Replace the defective product
- Repair it free of cost
- Refund your money
- Pay compensation for harm, inconvenience, even mental agony
- Stop the unfair practice (so it doesn't happen to future customers either)
- Pay punitive damages if the cheating was deliberate or repeated
- Withdraw misleading advertisements

**Child:** Mental agony, Maa? You can be compensated for being upset?

**Mother:** Yes, beta. The law recognises that being cheated is not just about money — it is also about the stress, the running around, the loss of trust. In Mumbai a few years ago, a woman won her father's LIC death claim plus an extra Rs 25,000 specifically for the mental agony LIC put her through over eight months of delays. The Forum heard her without a lawyer, in four sittings, and ruled in her favour. Total time: about seven months.

**Child:** Even for 130 grams of dal?

**Mother:** For 130 grams of dal in one packet, no — the better route is to complain to the **Legal Metrology Department**. That's the office that checks shopkeepers' weighing scales. They will send an inspector, and the shopkeeper can be **fined Rs 25,000 or more and have his licence suspended**. For a single packet you don't go to consumer court — but you absolutely can, and should, go to the Legal Metrology office. And there is also the **National Consumer Helpline, 1915** — you call them for guidance, and they will tell you which path is best for your case.

**Child:** That's such a small thing for the government to bother with.

**Mother:** No, beta — it is exactly the right size. Because the shopkeeper isn't cheating just you. He is cheating every customer who walks in. Your one complaint stops dozens of future cheats. **One brick in the wall.** When enough citizens complain, the cheating stops being profitable, and the marketplace becomes honest.

## Why Did India Need a Consumer Protection Act in 1986?

**Child:** Maa, before 1986, didn't we have any consumer laws at all?

**Mother:** We had some, beta — there was the old **Sale of Goods Act of 1930** from the British time, and the **Indian Contract Act of 1872**, and a few others. But these were for businessmen to settle disputes with other businessmen. For an ordinary buyer to use those laws, you needed a lawyer, lots of money, and years of patience. The shopkeeper knew this — and so he could cheat with confidence, knowing that no one would actually take him to a normal civil court over a 130-gram dal shortage.

**Child:** So before COPRA, the law existed but was useless for normal people?

**Mother:** Precisely. The Indian government realised through the 1970s and early 1980s that this was a serious gap. Two things pushed the issue:

(1) **The Bhopal gas tragedy of 1984.** A leak at the Union Carbide pesticide factory killed thousands and injured lakhs. It exposed how powerless ordinary citizens were against big companies. People had no fast way to seek compensation. The court cases dragged on for decades — and many victims got far too little, far too late.

(2) **Citizens' movements.** Groups like **Consumer Education and Research Centre (CERC) in Ahmedabad** and **Mumbai Grahak Panchayat** had been campaigning since the 1970s. Journalists like the team behind *Consumer Voice* magazine exposed adulteration scandals. Lawyers started arguing in the Supreme Court for stronger consumer protections.

Parliament responded in 1986 with COPRA — a law designed specifically for ordinary buyers. It set up the three-tier consumer court system. It gave every consumer six legally enforceable rights. It made the system **cheap, fast, and lawyer-optional** — completely different from regular courts.

**Child:** And it was modernised in 2019?

**Mother:** Yes — because by 2019 the marketplace had changed enormously. In 1986, nobody had heard of Amazon or Zomato or Paytm. By 2019, half our shopping was happening on apps. Influencers and Bollywood stars were endorsing products without disclosing they were paid. E-commerce platforms were taking your money but blaming "the seller" when things went wrong.

So the 2019 Act added several new things:

- **Product liability** — if a defective product harms you, the **manufacturer** is responsible, not just the shopkeeper.
- **E-commerce rules** — online platforms can be held liable, not just the third-party sellers using them.
- **Celebrity endorsements** — a celebrity who misleads consumers can be **fined and banned** from endorsements for up to three years.
- **The CCPA (Central Consumer Protection Authority)** — a new body that can act on its own when consumers are being cheated at scale, even before anyone files a complaint.
- **Higher monetary limits** — District Commissions now handle up to Rs 1 crore (was Rs 20 lakh).
- **Online filing** — **e-Daakhil** portal so you can file from your phone.

**Child:** What about dark patterns and fake reviews?

**Mother:** Those came later, beta. The CCPA in 2023 **banned 13 specific dark patterns** — those sneaky tricks in apps like fake countdown timers, pre-ticked insurance add-ons, hard-to-find unsubscribe buttons, "are you sure you want to leave us" guilt loops. The BIS in 2022 also published guidelines requiring platforms to label paid reviews. The law is constantly racing to catch up with new forms of cheating in the digital marketplace.

**Child:** So the Consumer Protection Act keeps growing as the marketplace changes?

**Mother:** That is exactly right, beta. It is a living law. Every time companies invent a new trick, citizens and consumer groups push government to update the law. In 1986 the tricks were rigged weighing scales and adulterated milk. In 2025 they are dark patterns and fake reviews. The tricks change, but the principle stays the same — **the buyer must not be at the mercy of the seller.**

## What Should I Take From This?

**Child:** Maa, this is a lot. If I have to remember only the most important things from all this, what are they?

**Mother:** Six things, beta. I will give you each one as a small habit.

**(1) Remember the six rights.** Right to be informed, to choose, to safety, to seek redressal, to consumer education, to be heard. They are your shield in every transaction.

**(2) Check the label.** Thirty seconds before paying. FSSAI for food, ISI for industrial, Agmark for agricultural, Hallmark for gold, FPO for fruit products. Look for the licence number — fake marks without numbers are everywhere.

**(3) Always ask for the bill.** No bill, no consumer court case. A receipt is your evidence. Shopkeepers who refuse to give bills usually have something to hide — and tax evasion is only one reason.

**(4) Don't let small cheating go.** A 130-gram dal shortage is not "too small" to complain about. Call **1915** (the National Consumer Helpline), or complain to the **Legal Metrology Department** for weighing fraud, or file at **e-Daakhil** for online filing. Every complaint is a brick in the wall against future cheating.

**(5) Consumer courts are designed for you.** Not for lawyers. Not for rich people. For ordinary buyers with ordinary complaints. Small filing fee, no lawyer needed, fixed time limits, online portal. Don't be intimidated.

**(6) The marketplace changes; the principle does not.** Tomorrow there will be new dark patterns, new fake-influencer scandals, new AI-generated reviews. The specific tricks will change. But the principle stays the same: **a fair marketplace requires informed, alert, complaining buyers.** Be one. Help others be one.

**Child:** Maa, one last thing. If I had to explain consumer rights to my younger cousin in one sentence, what would I say?

**Mother:** I would say: *In an honest marketplace, you don't have to trust the seller — the rules and the law back you up. Consumer rights are those rules. Use them.*

**Child:** And if my cousin still doesn't understand?

**Mother:** Then tell him the story of daadi's weighing scale. Tell him that for years, our family was being cheated 130 grams every week — until someone in the family checked, complained, and brought the inspector. That is what consumer rights look like in real life. Not a textbook idea. A 130-gram packet of dal, weighed honestly. Multiplied across a billion Indians.

That, beta, is the whole point.
