## What This Chapter Is Really About

When you hear the word **"globalisation"**, you probably think of phones designed in California and assembled in China, Hollywood films streamed on your laptop in Mumbai, or Indian engineers writing code for a bank in London. It sounds like a very 21st-century word.

The surprise of this chapter is that **globalisation is not new**. It is at least 5,000 years old. What is new is the speed, the scale, and the technology. The forces themselves — traders sailing across oceans, labourers moving between continents, diseases riding along with ships, money flowing where profit beckons — are as old as civilisation.

This chapter tells the long story of how the world became "one world". It is a story with bright chapters (potatoes saving Europe from hunger, the steamship knitting continents together) and dark ones (slavery, indentured labour, the Great Depression, two world wars). Through it all, India sits at a strange and important place — sometimes at the centre of trade, sometimes a victim of it, sometimes a quiet partner. We will follow that thread carefully.

## The Pre-Modern Global World

Long before there were ships with motors or planes or the internet, traders, pilgrims, priests, and soldiers were already moving across vast distances. As early as **3000 BCE**, the people of the Indus Valley were trading with West Asia. Cowrie shells from the Maldives travelled all the way to China and East Africa, where they were used as money. Buddhist monks carried scriptures from India to China and Korea; Arab traders carried Indian spices to Egypt and beyond.

So the first lesson of this chapter is simple: **the urge to connect with distant places is ancient**. The ancestor of the modern container ship is the wooden dhow; the ancestor of the email is the silk-route caravan.

### Silk Routes Link the World

Historians call this dense pre-modern network of trade and culture the **silk routes**. The name comes from the most famous cargo — Chinese silk, bound westward — but everything moved on these roads. Chinese pottery and Indian textiles travelled west; gold and silver flowed east from Europe. Christian missionaries, Muslim preachers, and Buddhist monks followed the merchants, carrying ideas the way merchants carried cloth.

There were several silk routes — some over land, some across the sea — and they thrived from before the Christian era until about the **fifteenth century**. They linked Asia, Europe, and northern Africa into a single, slow-pulsing system of exchange.

### Food Travels: From Spaghetti to the Humble Aloo

If you want to feel the weight of these old global networks, look at your dinner plate. Most of what is on it has travelled enormously over the centuries.

It is believed that **noodles travelled west** from China and slowly became Italian spaghetti. Indian curries influenced food in Southeast Asia. And after **Christopher Columbus accidentally bumped into the Americas in 1492**, an entire stream of new foods — potatoes, tomatoes, chillies, maize, sweet potatoes, groundnuts, soya — was carried back to Europe, Africa, and Asia.

It is almost impossible to imagine Indian food before this. Aloo paratha is a globalisation story. The potato originated in **Peru**. Without 1492, there would be no aloo gobhi, no vada pav, no French fries. The fierce **chillies** you take for granted in your sambar or your achaar came from **Mexico**, not India. The tomato in your dal — also American. **Maize** that feeds half of Africa — American. Even **groundnuts** for chikki — American.

In some places, these new crops made the difference between life and death. Europe's poor began to eat better and live longer with the potato. Ireland became so dependent on it that when a potato disease struck in the 1840s, around **a million Irish people died of starvation** and another million emigrated. One vegetable, one disease, two continents permanently changed.

## Conquest, Disease and Trade

After Columbus, the pre-modern world shrank quickly. The Portuguese and Spanish conquered enormous parts of America during the sixteenth century. We sometimes tell this story as a tale of European bravery and superior firepower, but the truth is darker — and stranger.

The Spanish conquerors' deadliest weapon was not the cannon. It was **germs**. Diseases like **smallpox**, which the Europeans carried in their bodies, found a population in America with no built-up immunity. Smallpox raced ahead of the conquering armies, killing entire communities before any European soldier appeared. In some regions, **90% of the indigenous population died**. With the people gone, the land was easy to seize.

Meanwhile, silver from Peru and Mexico poured into Spanish coffers, financing Europe's expanding trade with Asia. Legends spread about **El Dorado**, a fabled city of gold; men risked everything to find it. The Americas became the great prize of European empire.

And on the unpaid backs of this empire stood another horror: **the Atlantic slave trade**. From the 17th century onward, plantations in the Americas grew cotton, sugar, and tobacco for European markets — using human beings captured in Africa and shipped across the Atlantic in chains. The "triangular trade" — manufactures from Europe to Africa, slaves from Africa to America, sugar and cotton from America to Europe — was one of the cruellest economic engines ever built.

By the eighteenth century, the economic centre of the world began to shift. **China and India had been among the richest countries on earth** for most of recorded history. But China gradually withdrew from overseas trade, and India would soon be drawn into European empire. The centre of world trade moved decisively westwards, to Europe.

## The Nineteenth-Century Global Economy

The nineteenth century — roughly 1815 to 1914 — produced an entirely new kind of global economy. Economists describe international economic life in terms of three **flows**:

1. **The flow of trade** (mainly in goods — cloth, wheat, cotton)
2. **The flow of labour** (people moving in search of work)
3. **The flow of capital** (money invested across long distances)

All three flows now moved faster, larger, and deeper than ever before — and all three affected ordinary lives in ways earlier centuries could hardly imagine.

### A World Food Market

The story begins, oddly enough, with British bread. Population was growing fast in nineteenth-century Britain. Cities expanded, factories demanded workers, and demand for food grain shot up. Under pressure from rich landowners, the British government had passed the **Corn Laws**, restricting imports of grain. This kept food prices high — great for landlords, terrible for factory workers and urban poor.

Eventually, industrialists and city-dwellers forced the Corn Laws to be scrapped. Once grain could be imported freely, food poured into Britain from Eastern Europe, Russia, America, and Australia. British farmers could not compete. Vast tracts of British land were abandoned, and thousands of farm workers lost their jobs. Many migrated to cities or sailed overseas for a new life.

But somebody had to **grow** all the grain that now fed Britain. In America, Russia, Argentina, and Australia, forests were cleared, prairies were ploughed, new harbours were built, and railway lines were laid down to carry crops to ports. All of this required two things — **capital** and **labour**. Capital flowed out of London (the world's banking capital). Labour came as migrants — nearly **50 million people left Europe for America and Australia** during the nineteenth century, and about **150 million people in total** are estimated to have left their homes during this period, crossing oceans for a better future.

By 1890, a **global agricultural economy** had taken shape. Food no longer came from the next village. It came from thousands of miles away, grown by a migrant worker on land that had been a forest a generation earlier, shipped by rail and steamer to a buyer who had never seen the farm.

Closer to home, this same logic transformed parts of India. The British built a network of canals in west Punjab and turned semi-desert into productive cotton- and wheat-farming country. The **Canal Colonies** of Punjab were settled by peasants from other parts of the province. Indian cotton, jute, and tea were grown not for the family's pot or the local bazaar, but for the world market.

### The Role of Technology

None of this would have been possible without three world-changing technologies:

- **Railways** — they could move bulk cargo cheaply across continents.
- **Steamships** — faster, larger, and more reliable than sailing ships.
- **The telegraph** — for the first time, news and prices could travel across oceans in minutes rather than months.

A very humble example: the trade in meat. Until the 1870s, **live animals** had to be shipped across the Atlantic and slaughtered on arrival. They took up enormous ship space, fell ill, and many died on the journey. Meat was a luxury few Europeans could afford. Then came **refrigerated ships**. Now animals could be slaughtered at the source — in America, Australia, New Zealand — frozen, and shipped to Europe at low cost. Suddenly, working-class Europeans could put meat on the table. Their diets improved; their wages bought more; their support for European empires (which made all this possible) grew.

Technology and empire fed each other in a circle.

### The Gold Standard

Behind the trade flows ran an invisible system of money. In the late nineteenth century, most major economies tied the value of their currencies to a fixed amount of **gold**. This **gold standard** made it predictable — a British pound was worth so much gold, and so were so many Indian rupees or French francs. Money could be moved between countries without huge fear of value collapsing. Trade and investment flowed more easily.

This system would later collapse during the world wars, but for a few decades it gave the world economy a kind of stability it had never known.

### Late Nineteenth-Century Colonialism

There was, of course, a dark side. The same period saw a fresh wave of European conquest, especially in **Africa**. Look at any map of nineteenth-century Africa, and you will notice borders running in long, straight lines, as if drawn with a ruler. They were. In **1885**, the great European powers met in **Berlin** and divided Africa among themselves — Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, Italy, Portugal, Spain. Belgium and Germany became new colonial powers; the United States too began acquiring colonies in the 1890s.

For African societies, this meant disruption, dispossession, and forced labour. To grasp how cruelly the new economy worked, consider a single, terrifying disease.

### Rinderpest: A Cattle Plague That Changed Africa

**Rinderpest** was a fast-spreading disease of cattle. It arrived in Africa in the late 1880s, carried by infected animals shipped in to feed Italian soldiers invading Eritrea. From East Africa, it moved west "like forest fire", reaching the Atlantic coast by 1892 and the southern tip of Africa five years later. Along the way, it killed about **90% of African cattle**.

For African societies that depended on cattle for milk, ploughing, and wealth, this was a catastrophe. With their livelihoods destroyed, Africans were forced into the labour market — that is, they had to work for European-owned plantations and mines to survive. Colonial governments and European companies grabbed the surviving cattle, monopolising what little was left. The cattle plague did not arrive *as* European policy, but European empire quickly converted it into a tool of conquest. A single disease helped subdue an entire continent.

## Indentured Labour: Indians Across the World

The same nineteenth-century world that gave Britain cheap bread and gave Henry Ford his assembly lines also produced one of the saddest stories in modern Indian history — the migration of **indentured labour**.

An **indentured labourer** was a worker who signed a contract (an "indenture") to work for a fixed number of years — usually five — on a plantation in a faraway colony. In return, the worker would get a passage to and from the colony and a small wage. On paper, it was a contract between two consenting parties. In practice, it was, in the words of the time, **"a new system of slavery"**.

In the nineteenth century, hundreds of thousands of Indian and Chinese labourers were shipped out to work on plantations, in mines, and on railway projects across the world. Most Indian indentured workers came from **eastern Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, central India, and the dry districts of Tamil Nadu** — regions where cottage industries had collapsed, land rents had risen, peasants were deep in debt, and people were desperate.

The main destinations were:

- **The Caribbean** — Trinidad, Guyana, and Surinam (sugar plantations)
- **Mauritius** (also sugar)
- **Fiji** (sugar again)
- **Ceylon and Malaya** for Tamil migrants (tea, rubber)
- **Assam's tea plantations** within India itself

Recruitment was done by agents paid a small commission, and they were not above lying. Many migrants were told nothing about the long sea voyage. Some were told they were going somewhere nearby. A few were simply kidnapped.

When workers arrived, they found conditions far harsher than they had been promised — long hours, little pay, brutal supervisors, almost no legal protection, no way to leave. Some escaped into forests; if caught, they were punished severely.

And yet — and this is the part that always stays with me — these labourers built **new cultural worlds** out of fragments of the old. In Trinidad, the Muharram procession transformed into a riotous carnival called **'Hosay'** (for Imam Hussain), joined by workers of all races and religions. **Rastafarianism** in the Caribbean, made famous by the Jamaican singer Bob Marley, is said to reflect the influence of Indian migrants too. **Chutney music** in Trinidad and Guyana is a creative fusion of Indian melodies with Caribbean rhythms.

Most workers never returned home. Today, there are large communities of Indian descent in Mauritius, Fiji, Trinidad, Guyana, and Surinam. The Nobel-prize-winning writer **V.S. Naipaul** and West Indies cricketers like **Shivnarine Chanderpaul** are their descendants. Their slightly-Indian names carry the memory of an unjust system.

From the 1900s, India's nationalist leaders began campaigning against the indenture system. It was finally **abolished in 1921**.

### Indian Entrepreneurs Abroad

Not every Indian who travelled overseas was an indentured worker. Indian traders, moneylenders, and bankers — like the **Shikaripuri Shroffs** of Sindh and the **Nattukottai Chettiars** of Tamil Nadu — financed export agriculture across Southeast Asia and East Africa. They invented elaborate systems for sending money across long distances. **Hyderabadi Sindhi traders** set up shops at busy ports worldwide, selling curios to a new class of tourists travelling on safe and comfortable steamers.

So Indians were both the most exploited workers and some of the most sophisticated traders in the new global economy.

### Indian Trade Under Colonialism

What did colonialism do to Indian trade itself? It rewired it.

For centuries, India had exported fine cotton cloth to Europe. With Britain's industrialisation, British factories began producing cotton cloth cheaply at home. The British government slapped tariffs on Indian cloth entering Britain, while flooding the Indian market with British textiles. The share of cotton textiles in India's exports collapsed — from about **30% in 1800 to under 3% by the 1870s**.

In place of finished cloth, India began exporting **raw cotton, indigo, opium, jute, and tea** — raw materials, not finished goods. Britain also used its **trade surplus with India** to balance its trade deficits with other countries, and to pay the so-called **"home charges"** — salaries and pensions of British officials, interest on India's debts, profits remitted home. Colonial India was, in plain economic terms, financing the British Empire.

## The Inter-War Years (1914-1945)

For almost a hundred years — roughly 1815 to 1914 — Europe had been at peace, the global economy had grown, and the world had felt as if it was being knit together. Then, in **August 1914**, the **First World War** broke out, and the long century of progress shattered.

### The First World War

The war was fought mostly in Europe between the Allies (Britain, France, Russia, later the US) and the Central Powers (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Ottoman Turkey). What made it different from earlier wars was the **scale of industrial destruction**. Machine guns, tanks, aircraft, chemical weapons — these were the products of modern factories, used now to kill on a massive scale. About **9 million people died and 20 million were injured**, most of them young men.

To pay for the war, Britain borrowed heavily from American banks. By the end of the war, the United States had become the world's leading lender, and Britain — once the financial king — was deep in debt. The economic centre of the world shifted from London to New York.

### Post-War Difficulties

After the war, recovery was hard. Britain struggled to recapture its old markets in India, where local industries had grown during the war years. By 1921, **one in five British workers was unemployed**. Farmers everywhere suffered too — when wartime restrictions on wheat-growing in Eastern Europe were lifted, the world ended up with too much wheat, prices crashed, and rural debt spiralled.

### The Rise of Mass Production: Henry Ford

The United States, however, recovered quickly. In the 1920s, it became the world's industrial leader, and the symbol of this new America was the carmaker **Henry Ford**. Ford adopted the **assembly line** from a Chicago slaughterhouse and used it to make cars. Each worker stood in front of a moving conveyor belt and performed a single task, again and again, at a pace set by the belt. The Model T Ford rolled off the line every three minutes.

To stop workers from quitting (the work was that miserable), Ford doubled their daily wage to **\$5** in January 1914. He later called this "the best cost-cutting decision" he ever made — because the higher-paid workers could now afford to **buy** his cars. The American consumer economy was born: cars, radios, refrigerators, washing machines, all bought on **"hire purchase"** (instalments). The 1920s American economy roared.

### The Great Depression (1929 onwards)

Then it all collapsed.

The **Great Depression** began around 1929 and lasted till the mid-1930s. Across the world, production fell, prices fell, jobs vanished, banks failed, and farms went bust. There were many causes — too much agricultural production, easy American loans pulled back in a panic, falling demand, banks lending too freely and then suddenly refusing to lend at all. In the United States, by 1933, over **4,000 banks had closed** and about **110,000 companies had collapsed**.

The damage rippled outwards. American banks called back loans, currencies collapsed in Europe, the British pound nearly broke, and agricultural prices crashed everywhere.

### India and the Great Depression

The Great Depression showed just how tightly India had been stitched into the world economy. **Indian exports and imports nearly halved** between 1928 and 1934. Wheat prices in India fell by **50%**. Most painfully, the **price of raw jute collapsed by more than 60%**, ruining the small jute farmers of Bengal who had borrowed money to grow more, expecting better prices.

There was a haunting Bengali jute growers' lament from this time:

> *grow more jute, brothers, with the hope of greater cash.*
> *Costs and debts of jute will make your hopes get dashed.*

Across India, peasants used up their savings, mortgaged land, and sold whatever jewellery they had. India even became a major **exporter of gold** in these years — ordinary families parting with bangles and chains to pay the rent and the tax. The British colonial government refused to lower its revenue demands. Famine and misery deepened.

It was no coincidence that **Mahatma Gandhi launched the Civil Disobedience Movement (1930-31)** at the height of the depression. Rural India was simmering with anger, and the Salt March drew on that anger as fuel.

Strangely, urban India had a quieter experience. Salaried employees and landowners with fixed incomes actually became **better off** because everything was cheaper. Industrial investment grew in the 1930s as the colonial government, under nationalist pressure, gave Indian industries tariff protection. The Depression hit the village far harder than the city.

## After the Second World War: Rebuilding the World

Just twenty-one years after the First World War ended, the **Second World War** broke out (1939-1945). It was, if possible, even more devastating: about **60 million people died** — most of them civilians, not soldiers. Cities across Europe and Asia were reduced to rubble. The Holocaust killed six million Jews. Hiroshima and Nagasaki vanished under atomic bombs.

After this second great catastrophe, world leaders sat down to ask a serious question: **how do we never let this happen again?** Their answer would shape the world we live in today.

### The Bretton Woods System

In **July 1944**, even before the war was over, representatives of 44 nations met at the **Mount Washington Hotel** in **Bretton Woods**, New Hampshire (USA), for the **United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference**. Their goal was to build a stable post-war economic order.

Two lessons had been drawn from the inter-war disaster:

1. **Mass production needs mass consumption.** Mass consumption needs stable incomes. Stable incomes need full employment. And markets alone cannot guarantee full employment — **governments must step in**.
2. **A country cannot manage its economy if global money and goods flow uncontrolled across its borders**. Some international rules were needed.

Out of Bretton Woods came two new institutions, the famous **"Bretton Woods twins"**:

- The **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** — to manage countries' external surpluses and deficits and to lend to countries in balance-of-payments trouble.
- The **International Bank for Reconstruction and Development**, popularly known as the **World Bank** — to finance post-war reconstruction and development.

Both began operations in **1947**. From day one, decision-making in these institutions was dominated by the Western industrial powers; the US has an effective veto on major decisions. India joined both.

The post-war monetary system was based on **fixed exchange rates**. National currencies — including the rupee — were tied to the US dollar at a fixed rate. The dollar itself was tied to gold at \$35 an ounce. This system gave the world economy two decades of remarkable stability.

### The Era of Decolonisation and the G-77

Between roughly 1945 and 1970, most of the world's colonies in Asia and Africa became independent nations. India in 1947. Indonesia in 1949. Ghana in 1957. Algeria in 1962. Kenya in 1963. By 1970, the European empires were mostly gone.

But independence did not mean equality. The Bretton Woods institutions had been designed for the industrial countries, not for newly independent poor countries facing the urgent task of building schools, hospitals, dams, and factories. Many former colonies found that **the former colonial powers still controlled vital resources** — mines, oil fields, large plantations — in their countries. American and European multinational corporations often secured cheap deals to extract minerals and oil.

So in the 1960s, the developing countries organised themselves into a group — the **Group of 77** (or **G-77**) — and demanded a **New International Economic Order (NIEO)**. They wanted:

- Real control over their own natural resources
- More development aid
- Fairer prices for their raw materials
- Better access for their manufactured goods in the rich-country markets

The G-77 did not transform the world, but it gave the developing world a louder, more organised voice — and India was one of its leading members.

### End of Bretton Woods and the Birth of "Globalisation"

The Bretton Woods system itself did not last forever. From the 1960s, the United States ran up huge costs from overseas military involvements (especially the Vietnam War). The dollar weakened. In 1971, the US ended the dollar's link to gold; soon, the fixed-exchange-rate system collapsed and currencies began to **float** — their value set by daily demand and supply in foreign exchange markets.

From the mid-1970s, the international financial system changed in important ways. Developing countries had to borrow from private Western banks, leading to a series of **debt crises** in Africa and Latin America. From the late 1970s, multinational corporations (MNCs) began **shifting production to low-wage Asian countries**.

Then, in the late 1970s and 1980s, two enormous changes happened:

- **China**, cut off from the world economy since its 1949 revolution, began **opening up** its economy.
- The **Soviet Union and Eastern European communist countries collapsed** in 1989-1991, joining the global capitalist system.

Suddenly, a vast pool of low-cost labour was available for global capitalism, and global capitalism rushed in. **China** became "the factory of the world". Most of your phone parts, your toys, your clothes were probably manufactured in China at some stage. The relocation of industry to low-wage countries — including, increasingly, **India**, China, Brazil, and Vietnam — has redrawn the economic map of the world. In 1990, "globalisation" was still a young, slightly intimidating word. By 2020, it was the air we breathed.

## Where India Sits Today

India's place in this story is unusual. We have been:

- A **giver of culture and goods** in the pre-modern era (Buddhism, spices, fine textiles).
- A **victim of colonial extraction** in the nineteenth century (raw materials out, manufactured goods in).
- A **source of indentured labour** for European plantations.
- A **victim of a global crisis** during the Great Depression.
- An **early member** of the Bretton Woods institutions.
- A **leader of the G-77** demand for a fairer global order.
- A **destination** of MNC investment from the 1990s onwards.
- A **major exporter of software and services** in the 21st century.

Indian IT engineers writing code in Bengaluru for an American bank are doing something deeply familiar — connecting India to a global market — but doing it now from a position of relative strength rather than indenture. The arc bends slowly, but it bends.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Three Short Stories About Globalisation in Your Own Life

**Story 1 — The Aloo Paratha.** Imagine your grandmother rolling out an aloo paratha. That dish is a quiet globalisation story. The **wheat** in the paratha is an old crop, but the **potato** inside it travelled from Peru to Europe with Spanish ships in the sixteenth century, then to India with Portuguese and British traders. Before 1600, no Indian had ever tasted a potato. Today it is in every village kitchen. Your aloo paratha contains 500 years of trade history.

**Story 2 — Your Chai.** A cup of Indian masala chai is a layered globalisation story too. The **tea plant** came to India from China. The **plantations** in Assam and Darjeeling were built with British capital in the nineteenth century. The **labour** to clear and pick the plantations was indentured — often Adivasis from central India, paid little, watched closely. The **sugar** has a slave-trade history of its own. Even the **cardamom** and **cinnamon** in your masala travelled along old spice routes. Sipping chai is sipping global history.

**Story 3 — The Bengaluru Coder.** A 24-year-old engineer in Bengaluru writes code at midnight for a New York bank. The code runs on servers in Singapore. The customer is an American shopkeeper. The salary is paid in dollars, then converted to rupees. This is twenty-first century globalisation — Indian brainpower exported the way Indian cotton was once exported, but on much fairer terms.

### DIY Activity: Audit Your Dinner Plate

Tonight at dinner, pick **five items** on the table — vegetables, spices, sauces, drinks, anything. For each one, find out where the plant originally came from. (You can ask family, look it up, or use a school book.) You will be surprised how many of your everyday foods came from somewhere very far away.

Then look around your house and find **five objects** — clothes, electronics, appliances, packets. Read the labels: "Made in …". Make a small list of where each thing was made.

### A Question to Keep Asking

Globalisation has brought potatoes, chai, mobile phones, and English-language jobs into Indian life. It has also brought Walmart, environmental damage, and inequality. As an Indian growing up now, **which parts of globalisation should we welcome, and which parts should we resist?**

There is no single right answer — but it is one of the most important questions of your generation.
