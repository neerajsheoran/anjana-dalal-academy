## In One Paragraph

**Globalisation** is at least 5,000 years old — Indus Valley traders, **silk routes**, and Indian Ocean trade networks linked Asia, Europe and Africa for centuries. After **1492**, Columbus's voyages connected the Americas to the rest of the world, transferring crops like potatoes, chillies, maize, and tomatoes to Europe, Africa and Asia, while European diseases like smallpox killed up to 90% of indigenous Americans and made colonial conquest easier. The **nineteenth century (1815-1914)** saw the world economy transformed by railways, steamships, telegraphs, the **gold standard**, and mass migration — nearly 150 million people left their homes. India became an exporter of raw cotton, jute, opium and tea, and a source of **indentured labour** sent to Mauritius, the Caribbean, and Fiji. In Africa, the **rinderpest** cattle plague (1890s) destroyed African livelihoods and forced Africans into colonial wage-labour. The First World War (1914-18) ended this first wave of globalisation; the **Great Depression** of 1929 shattered the world economy, halved Indian trade, and pushed peasants into ruin, feeding the Civil Disobedience Movement of 1930-31. After the Second World War, the **Bretton Woods conference (1944)** created the **IMF** and **World Bank** and a system of fixed exchange rates. **Decolonisation** brought independence to Asia and Africa, and developing nations organised as the **G-77** to demand a fairer global order. Bretton Woods collapsed in the 1970s; from the 1980s, **MNCs** shifted production to low-wage Asia, China opened up, the Soviet bloc collapsed, and today's globalisation took shape, with India emerging as a major exporter of IT services.

## Key Takeaways

- **Globalisation is ancient, not new** — silk routes, Indian Ocean trade, and cowrie shells linked the world for thousands of years.
- **1492 was a turning point** — Columbus's voyages began the Columbian Exchange: potatoes, chillies, maize, tomatoes and groundnuts to the Old World; horses and wheat to the New; smallpox and other diseases killed 90% of native Americans.
- **The slave trade** ran a triangle: European manufactures to Africa, African slaves to American plantations, sugar and cotton back to Europe.
- **19th-century globalisation (1815-1914)** rested on three flows: **trade, labour, and capital** — multiplied by railways, steamships, telegraphs and the **gold standard**.
- **150 million people** migrated globally in the 19th century, including 50 million Europeans to America and Australia.
- **Indentured labour from India** went to Mauritius, Trinidad, Guyana, Surinam, Fiji, Ceylon, Malaya — a "new system of slavery". Abolished in **1921**.
- **Rinderpest (1890s)** killed 90% of African cattle, destroying African livelihoods and pushing Africans into colonial wage-labour.
- **Colonial India** became an exporter of raw materials (cotton, jute, indigo, opium, tea) and an importer of British manufactured goods; Britain used its **trade surplus with India** to balance deficits with other countries and pay **"home charges"**.
- **First World War (1914-18)** killed 9 million, broke the gold standard, made the US the world's leading creditor, and ended the first wave of globalisation.
- **Henry Ford's assembly line** and the 1920s consumer boom in the US shaped modern mass production and mass consumption.
- **The Great Depression (1929-mid-1930s)** halved India's trade, crashed jute prices by 60%, ruined peasants, and triggered the Civil Disobedience Movement (1930-31). India exported gold to pay its way.
- **Bretton Woods (1944)** created the **IMF** and **World Bank** and a fixed-exchange-rate system; commenced operations in 1947.
- **Decolonisation (1945-1970)** freed most Asian and African colonies; developing countries formed the **G-77** to demand a **New International Economic Order**.
- **End of Bretton Woods (1970s)** brought floating exchange rates; MNCs shifted production to low-wage Asia; China and the former Soviet bloc joined the global capitalist economy in the 1980s-90s; today's globalisation took shape.

## Key Terms

- **Globalisation** — the long-running process by which economies, societies, and cultures across the world become connected through trade, migration, capital flows, technology, and ideas.
- **Silk routes** — pre-modern overland and sea trade routes linking China, India, Central Asia, West Asia, Europe and Africa; thrived from before the Christian era to about the 15th century.
- **Columbian Exchange** — the massive transfer of crops, animals, people and diseases between the Old World (Europe/Asia/Africa) and the New World (Americas) after 1492.
- **Corn Laws** — British laws restricting the import of corn (grain); abolished in 1846, after which Britain became a major food importer.
- **Gold standard** — a 19th-century monetary system in which national currencies were tied to fixed quantities of gold, making exchange rates stable.
- **Indentured labour** — a worker bound by contract ("indenture") to work for an employer for a fixed number of years (usually 5) to pay off the passage to a new country; in 19th-century India, often a "new system of slavery".
- **Rinderpest** — a fast-spreading cattle plague that swept Africa in the 1890s, killing 90% of cattle and reshaping the colonial economy.
- **The Great Depression** — the worldwide economic collapse that began around 1929 and lasted till the mid-1930s, with catastrophic falls in production, employment, prices and trade.
- **Bretton Woods system** — the post-World War II international monetary system (1944) of fixed exchange rates anchored to the US dollar and gold; gave rise to the **IMF** and the **World Bank**.
- **G-77 (Group of 77)** — a group of developing countries formed in the 1960s to demand a fairer global economic order (NIEO) — control of natural resources, fair prices, development aid, market access.
- **MNCs (Multinational Corporations)** — companies operating in several countries at once; expanded enormously after the 1950s and from the 1970s began shifting production to low-wage Asian countries.
- **Hire purchase** — buying goods on monthly or weekly instalments rather than paying full price upfront; central to the American 1920s consumer boom.
