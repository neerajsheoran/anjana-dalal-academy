## When Did "Globalisation" Really Begin?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says potatoes came to India from somewhere else. Then what did people eat with their dal before that?

**Mother:** A very good question. Before the potato came, your great-great-great-grandmothers had quite a different vegetable patch. Aloo, tamatar, mirchi, mooli — many of these are recent additions to the Indian thali.

**Child:** Really? No aloo gobhi? No alu posto?

**Mother:** Imagine it. Bengal without alu posto. North India without aloo paratha. They had **yams, sweet gourds, lauki, kaddu, palak, methi**, mustard greens, brinjal, drumsticks, lots of dals, rice, roti from jowar or bajra. They had spices — but not chilli; the heat came from **pepper, ginger, mustard, asafoetida**. Chilli is American, you know. So is the tomato.

**Child:** So Indian food before Columbus was completely different?

**Mother:** Quite different. And here is the deeper point — what we call "globalisation" did not start with mobile phones or American jeans. It started thousands of years ago, when traders, monks, and travellers carried food, seeds, ideas, and germs across the world. Your aloo paratha is itself a 500-year-old globalisation event. The potato was domesticated by farmers in Peru, taken by Spanish ships to Europe, then carried to India by Portuguese and British traders, then absorbed so deeply into Punjabi cuisine that today no one can imagine an aloo-less paratha.

**Child:** So when did the textbook say globalisation began?

**Mother:** The textbook says it has a "long history". As early as **3000 BCE**, the Indus Valley people were trading with West Asia. Cowrie shells from the Maldives reached China and East Africa as money. Silk from China travelled west; Indian spices travelled east. Buddhist monks walked to China. Arab traders carried Indian cotton to Europe. The pre-modern world was already deeply, slowly connected — through the **silk routes** on land, and through the Indian Ocean on water.

**Child:** So today's globalisation is just faster?

**Mother:** Faster, larger, deeper. But the impulse is the same — humans have always wanted to trade, travel, and connect. What changed in modern times is technology. A silk-route caravan took years to cross Asia. A container ship now does it in weeks. An email does it in seconds. The geography hasn't changed; the clock has.

## Why Did Europeans Conquer the Americas Instead of the Other Way Around?

**Child:** Maa, I always wondered. If America was so rich in gold and silver and crops, why didn't the Americans come and conquer Europe?

**Mother:** That is one of the deepest questions in modern history. There are many answers, but the chapter focuses on a surprising one — **disease**.

**Child:** Disease?

**Mother:** Yes. We sometimes imagine that the Spanish conquered Mexico and Peru because their guns and horses were so much better. But the truth is darker. The Spanish carried **germs** with them — smallpox, measles, influenza — diseases that Europeans had been exposed to for centuries and had built up some immunity to. The native Americans had never met these diseases. When the Spanish arrived, smallpox raced through the population like fire through dry grass. In some regions, **90% of native Americans died** within a few decades.

**Child:** Ninety percent? That's terrible.

**Mother:** It is one of the great catastrophes of human history. The Aztec and Inca empires did not just lose battles — they lost their populations. Once 90% of the people are gone, you cannot defend your land, your culture, your civilisation. The Spanish then poured into the empty spaces.

**Child:** And the same could not happen the other way? If Native Americans had come to Europe?

**Mother:** Not in the same way. The Europeans had lived with cattle, pigs, sheep, horses, chickens for thousands of years. They had picked up many animal-borne diseases and built up resistance. The Americans had domesticated fewer animals, lived in different ways, and were not carrying many killer germs that would have devastated Europe.

**Child:** That feels like luck, not bravery.

**Mother:** Largely it was. Plus the Europeans had ocean-going ships, gunpowder, and aggressive states hungry for gold. But the germs did most of the killing. Historians call it a "biological catastrophe" — and it was one of the foundations of European empire.

**Child:** And then the Africans were brought as slaves?

**Mother:** Yes — the second tragedy. With the native population destroyed, the Europeans needed labour for plantations of sugar, cotton, and tobacco. So from the 1600s onwards, about **12 million Africans** were captured, chained, and shipped across the Atlantic in conditions so cruel that around 2 million died during the voyage itself. Their descendants are the African Americans, the Afro-Caribbeans, the Brazilians of African origin. An entire side of the modern world was built on this terror.

**Child:** And no one stopped it?

**Mother:** For a long time, no. There were brave campaigners — William Wilberforce in Britain, Frederick Douglass in America. Slavery was abolished in Britain in 1833, in the US after the Civil War in 1865. But the damage had been done, and the wealth from slave-plantations had already built much of modern Europe and America. The legacy is still being argued about today.

## Why Did Indian Labourers End Up in So Many Strange Countries?

**Child:** Maa, my friend in school is from Mauritius. Her grandparents speak Bhojpuri. How did they get there?

**Mother:** That is a sad and important story. After slavery was abolished in the British Empire in 1833, the sugar plantations of Mauritius, Trinidad, Guyana, Fiji, and elsewhere suddenly had no workers. The British did not want to pay Africans proper wages, so they invented a new system — **indentured labour**.

**Child:** What does "indentured" mean?

**Mother:** An indentured labourer signs a contract — an "indenture" — to work for a fixed number of years, usually five, in exchange for passage to and from the colony and small wages. On paper, it is a voluntary contract. In practice, in the 19th century, it was, in the words of the time, **"a new system of slavery"**.

**Child:** Where did the Indians who went come from?

**Mother:** Mostly from **eastern Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, central India, and dry districts of Tamil Nadu**. Why these regions? Because in those years, cottage industries had collapsed, land rents had risen, peasants were deep in debt, lands were being cleared for mines and plantations. People were desperate. When recruitment agents came offering work overseas, many signed — sometimes without even being told they would have to cross an ocean. Some were lied to. A few were abducted.

**Child:** And where did they go?

**Mother:** **Mauritius. Trinidad. Guyana. Surinam. Fiji.** Tamils went to **Ceylon and Malaya**. Some went to Natal in South Africa — that is where young Gandhi met them and began his political life. And within India, indentured workers from places like Chhota Nagpur were sent to the **tea plantations of Assam** under similar harsh contracts.

**Child:** Did they ever come back?

**Mother:** Some did. Most stayed. Their descendants are now full citizens of those countries — proudly Mauritian, proudly Trinidadian, proudly Fijian. But they still carry a faint cultural memory of India. They sing Bhojpuri songs. They eat dal and roti. They celebrate Diwali. The cricketer **Shivnarine Chanderpaul** from the West Indies — his ancestors were indentured labourers. **V.S. Naipaul** who won the Nobel Prize for Literature — his ancestors were too.

**Child:** Did Indians complain? Or fight back?

**Mother:** Many did. Workers escaped into forests; some were caught and beaten. They invented new cultural worlds — in Trinidad they turned the Muharram procession into a colourful carnival called **Hosay**, joined by people of all races. Indian rhythms blended with African rhythms to give us **chutney music**. From the 1900s, Indian nationalist leaders — Gandhi himself, Madan Mohan Malaviya — began campaigning hard against indenture as cruel and abusive. It was finally **abolished in 1921**.

**Child:** Where were my own ancestors during all this?

**Mother:** You should ask your dada-dadi this question. Many Indian families have someone who went abroad — to Burma, Singapore, East Africa, Mauritius, the Gulf later. Some of those journeys were indentured; some were trade; some were soldiers in the British Indian Army. Migration is in our blood. Your friend in Mauritius is, in some quiet way, your cousin.

## How Did a Cattle Disease Change a Whole Continent?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter has this strange story about a cow disease changing Africa. Can a disease really change a continent?

**Mother:** It can, and it did. The disease is called **rinderpest** — a fast-spreading cattle plague. In Africa, in the 1890s, it killed about **90% of all cattle**.

**Child:** Where did it come from?

**Mother:** It was brought in, by accident, with infected cattle shipped from British Asia to feed Italian soldiers invading Eritrea in East Africa. From there it spread westwards across the continent — the textbook says "like forest fire" — and reached the Atlantic coast by 1892 and the southern tip of Africa five years later.

**Child:** But what did it do to people?

**Mother:** To understand that, you have to understand what cattle meant to traditional African societies. Cattle was wealth. Cattle was milk, ploughing, hides, status. A man's worth was measured in cattle. With 90% of cattle dead, African societies were ruined overnight.

**Child:** And then?

**Mother:** Here is the cruel part. The Europeans wanted Africans to work for wages on their plantations and mines. But Africans, who had land and cattle, did not particularly want to work for white men's wages. So the Europeans imposed heavy taxes — **paid only in cash**. The only way to get cash was to work for the Europeans. Inheritance laws were changed so that only one son could inherit family land — the rest were pushed into the labour market. Mineworkers were locked into closed compounds. All these methods were used to force Africans into wage labour.

**Child:** And the cattle plague helped them?

**Mother:** It made it easier. With cattle gone, Africans had no fallback. Colonial governments and European companies grabbed the surviving cattle, monopolising what little was left. Now to feed your family, you had no choice but to work in a European mine or plantation. The disease itself was an accident, but European empire converted it into a tool of conquest. A single cattle plague helped subdue an entire continent.

**Child:** That is really sad. Did Africans ever recover?

**Mother:** Africa is still recovering, in some ways. The structures built in that period — borders drawn at Berlin in 1885, economies organised around extracting minerals for Europe, cities built as ports for export — still shape African countries today. When you look at a map of Africa and notice the straight-line borders, remember they were drawn by European powers at a conference, not by Africans themselves.

## Was the Great Depression Felt in Indian Villages Too?

**Child:** Maa, the Great Depression — that happened in America, right? In 1929? Why is it in our Indian history book?

**Mother:** Because by 1929, India was deeply tied to the world economy. When America sneezed, India caught a cold. When America had a heart attack, India fell into a coma.

**Child:** How bad was it for India?

**Mother:** Between 1928 and 1934, India's exports and imports **nearly halved**. Wheat prices in India **fell by 50%**. Prices of jute — Bengal's lifeline — **crashed by more than 60%**. Peasants who had borrowed money to grow more, expecting better prices, suddenly couldn't even cover their debts.

**Child:** What did peasants do?

**Mother:** They suffered terribly. They used up their savings. They mortgaged their lands. They sold their jewellery. There is a famous fact in the textbook — India became a major **exporter of gold** in those depression years. Imagine that: ordinary peasant families parting with their gold bangles and earrings to pay taxes and rent.

**Child:** Did the British government help them?

**Mother:** No. That is the cruellest part. The colonial government **refused to lower its revenue demands**. Even as prices crashed and peasants starved, the government wanted its rupees on time. This is one of the reasons rural India was simmering with anger by 1930. It is no accident that **Mahatma Gandhi launched the Civil Disobedience Movement in 1930-31** — right at the height of the depression. The Salt March drew on that anger.

**Child:** What about our family? Was Punjab affected?

**Mother:** Punjab's peasants suffered badly. Many had taken loans to buy land in the Canal Colonies, expecting wheat and cotton exports to keep paying off the debt. When wheat prices crashed, they were trapped. The Punjab moneylender — the **sahukar** — became a hated figure in those years. There is a lot of Punjabi folk poetry about peasants ruined in this period. Your own great-grandfather's generation would have lived through it.

**Child:** And the rich? Were they hurt too?

**Mother:** This is interesting. The depression hit village India far harder than urban India. Salaried clerks and landlords with fixed rents actually became **better off** because everything was cheaper. Some Indian industries also grew — because the colonial government, under nationalist pressure, gave them tariff protection against foreign competition. Bombay textile mills, Tata Steel — they actually did relatively well. The depression sharpened the gap between town and countryside.

**Child:** So the same crisis hurt different people differently?

**Mother:** Yes — and that is true of every economic crisis, including ones we've seen in our lifetime. A crisis is never equally shared. The poor and rural always feel it most. The educated urban people sometimes don't notice it. That is one of the most important lessons of economic history.

## What's Different About Today's Globalisation?

**Child:** Maa, so if globalisation is thousands of years old, what is new about today?

**Mother:** A few important things. First, the **speed**. A message from Bengaluru to New York used to take six months by ship. Today it takes a fraction of a second. Second, the **scale**. Much more of the world's goods and people now move across borders. Third, the **kinds of things** that move. In the 19th century, what moved was mostly raw materials — cotton, jute, wheat, opium, indentured labourers. Today it is also **services** — software, customer support, finance, design — and **information**.

**Child:** And India? Where are we in this?

**Mother:** India sits at a very interesting place. We have been many things in this long story — a giver of spices and Buddhism in ancient times, a victim of colonial extraction in the 19th century, a source of indentured labour, a victim of the Great Depression, an early member of the Bretton Woods institutions, a leader of the G-77's call for fairness, and now — from the 1990s onwards — a major exporter of **software and IT services**.

**Child:** So the Indian engineer in Bengaluru writing code for an American bank...

**Mother:** ...is doing something deeply familiar. Connecting India to a global market — the way Indian cotton once did, the way indentured Indian labour once did — but doing it from a position of strength rather than indenture. Same impulse, very different terms.

**Child:** Is globalisation good or bad?

**Mother:** It depends who you ask, and when. The factory worker in a Bengaluru garment factory making jeans for the world might feel grateful for a job. The handloom weaver who lost his livelihood to cheap mass-produced cloth might feel ruined. The Indian engineer in San Francisco might feel proud. The American factory worker whose factory closed because production moved to Vietnam might feel angry. The Mauritian schoolgirl proud of her Bhojpuri ancestors might feel one way; the Sudanese farmer ploughing land owned by a Chinese company might feel another.

**Child:** So there is no simple answer?

**Mother:** No simple answer. Globalisation, in any era, brings winners and losers — sometimes from the same family. The question is never "should we be in the global world?" — we already are, and have been for thousands of years. The question is — **on what terms?** Who decides the rules? Who benefits? Who bears the cost? That is the question of your generation.

**Child:** What can we do about it?

**Mother:** You can learn it carefully, think about it honestly, and one day help shape it. There is a long line of Indian leaders — Gandhi, Nehru, Indira Gandhi — who tried to insist that the global system be fairer to India and other former colonies. The G-77 was India's idea, in part. We have a tradition of demanding fair globalisation, not just accepting whatever comes. That tradition is yours to carry forward.

**Child:** I'll think about it, Maa.

**Mother:** That's all anyone can ask. Now go eat your aloo paratha. It travelled 500 years to reach your plate — give it the respect it deserves.
