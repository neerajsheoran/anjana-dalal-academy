## A Painting That Dreamed of a New World

In the year 1848, a French artist named **Frédéric Sorrieu** painted a curious image. He called it *The Dream of Worldwide Democratic and Social Republics*. In it, the peoples of Europe and the Americas — men and women, young and old, rich and poor — march together past a statue of Liberty. She holds the torch of Enlightenment in one hand and the Charter of the Rights of Man in the other. Each nation in the procession is identified by its flag and its costume. At the front walk the United States and Switzerland, already republics by 1848. France, in revolutionary tricolour, has just reached the statue. Behind her come the Germans, Austrians, Italians, Poles, the Irish, the Hungarians — most of them not yet free, not yet united. Christ and the angels watch from the clouds, blessing what looks like a brotherhood of equal nations.

Sorrieu was dreaming. None of those Germans or Italians actually existed as a single people yet. They lived inside other people's empires, spoke many dialects, and answered to many princes. But Sorrieu painted them as if the future had already arrived — as if every people in Europe would soon belong to its own free, equal **nation-state**.

This chapter is the story of how that strange dream — a continent of nation-states — partly came true in the nineteenth century. It is also the story of how the same dream, by the end of the century, turned ugly: how nationalism began with marching peasants and ended with marching armies. Indian students will notice many of the same questions in our own freedom movement. The European nineteenth century is, in a sense, the rehearsal hall for the twentieth century everywhere — including the India of 1947.

## The French Revolution and the Idea of the Nation

The first clear cry of European nationalism came not from a king or a poet but from a revolution. In **1789**, France was a "full" state in the modern sense — one territory, one ruler, one army — but it was the **property of a king**. The French Revolution announced something startling: that sovereignty did not belong to the monarch but to the **people**. From now on, *la patrie* (the fatherland) and *le citoyen* (the citizen) would be the building blocks of public life.

The revolutionaries did everything they could to make the new "nation" feel real:

- A new tricolour flag replaced the royal standard.
- The Estates General was renamed the **National Assembly** and elected by citizens, not summoned by kings.
- New songs, oaths, and martyr-cults were invented around the idea of "France."
- Internal customs duties were abolished, and **uniform weights and measures** replaced a confusing patchwork.
- The dialect of Paris was promoted as the single national language, and regional dialects were quietly discouraged.

If this list reminds you of the early Indian National Congress — its insistence on a common cause across Bengal, Madras, and Bombay; its promotion of Hindustani; its vande mataram and tricolour — that is not a coincidence. Every nationalist movement borrows the same toolkit.

The French went further. They declared it the **mission** of the French nation to liberate other peoples of Europe from despotism. When Napoleon's armies poured into Holland, Switzerland, Belgium, and parts of Italy and Germany in the 1790s, they carried that mission with them — at the point of a bayonet.

## The Napoleonic Code and Its Mixed Legacy

Napoleon Bonaparte ended the French Republic and crowned himself Emperor. So in one sense he killed the revolution. But in the territories he conquered, he kept many of its administrative reforms. His **Civil Code of 1804** — known as the **Napoleonic Code** — became one of the most exported documents in history. It abolished privileges based on birth, established equality before the law, and protected the right to property. Wherever French armies went, the Code went too.

In the Dutch lands, in Switzerland, in Italy, in much of Germany, Napoleon:

- Abolished the **feudal system** and freed peasants from serfdom and manorial dues.
- Removed guild restrictions in the towns.
- Standardised administrative divisions, weights, currency, and transport.
- Made movement of goods and capital easier across regions.

Businessmen and artisans loved this. Imagine, for a moment, a German merchant who in 1830 wanted to carry a cartload of cloth from Hamburg to Nuremberg. He had to pass through **eleven customs barriers** and pay tax at each one. Each region used its own measure of the *elle* (the local yard) — 54.7 cm in Frankfurt, 55.1 cm in Mainz, 65.6 cm in Nuremberg. For such people, a unified Europe under a single rational code felt like a blessing.

But the love did not last. Heavy taxation, censorship, and **forced conscription** into Napoleon's wars made his rule feel like a foreign occupation. The French had marched in as liberators and stayed as masters. A famous German cartoon of the period showed Napoleon as a postman riding home after the battle of Leipzig (1813), with letters falling from his bag — each one marked with the name of a province he had just lost. Compare this with the way British rule in India started as "trade and good administration" and ended in resentment: the same arc of welcome turning into anger.

## Conservatism's Grip After 1815

Napoleon fell in 1815. The kings, princes, and chancellors of Europe gathered in Vienna — hosted by Austria's **Chancellor Metternich** — to undo what the revolution had begun. The result was the **Treaty of Vienna of 1815**. The Bourbons were placed back on the French throne. France lost its conquests. New buffer states were arranged on her borders. Russia got part of Poland; Prussia got part of Saxony; Austria was given northern Italy.

This was the age of **conservatism**. The word does not mean "doing nothing." It meant believing that traditional institutions — monarchy, the Church, the family, the social hierarchy, private property — were the bones of civilisation, and that they should be preserved. But Europe's clever conservatives had learned a lesson from Napoleon: a modern army, an efficient bureaucracy, and an end to old feudal rubbish could *strengthen* a monarchy. They wanted modern tools and an old system.

The settlement was held together by **autocratic regimes** that crushed any newspaper, play, or song that smelled of revolution. A famous caricature from this period, titled *The Club of Thinkers*, joked: "How long will thinking be allowed to us?" One of its rules was that members would be given muzzles upon entering.

Sound familiar? Indian readers will think of the **Vernacular Press Act of 1878** — the same logic of an empire muzzling the language of dissent.

## Revolutionaries and Secret Societies — Enter Mazzini

Liberty driven underground does not die. It hides in cellars and prints in basements. Across post-1815 Europe, **secret societies** sprang up to train revolutionaries: to oppose the Vienna settlement, to fight for constitutions, and — crucially — to build **nation-states** out of empires.

The most famous of these revolutionaries was an Italian, **Giuseppe Mazzini**. Born in Genoa in 1805, he joined the Carbonari (a secret society) and was sent into exile at the age of 26 for plotting an uprising. From exile he founded **Young Italy** in Marseilles, and later **Young Europe** in Berne — bringing together like-minded young men from Poland, France, Italy, and the German states.

Mazzini's central idea was almost religious: God had intended **nations** to be the natural units of mankind. Italy could not remain a "patchwork of small states and kingdoms." It had to become one republic, alongside other free nations. Metternich called Mazzini "the most dangerous enemy of our social order." That is high praise from such a man.

Compare Mazzini's position to that of early Indian nationalists like Surendranath Banerjee or Dadabhai Naoroji — moral, idealistic, articulate, working through speeches and networks rather than through armies. The revolutionary nationalist is a recognisable type across the world.

## The Greek War of Independence — The First National Revolution

The first revolution to actually win a nation-state was Greek. Greece had been ruled by the Ottoman Empire since the fifteenth century. In **1821**, Greek nationalists rose in armed struggle, supported by Greeks living abroad and by sympathetic Europeans who romanticised Greece as the cradle of European civilisation.

Poets and painters joined the cause. The English poet **Lord Byron** raised money for the Greek struggle and went there to fight; he died of fever in 1824. The French painter **Eugène Delacroix** painted *The Massacre at Chios* in 1824, an enormous canvas showing 20,000 Greeks killed by Turkish forces — dramatic, vivid, designed to stir European sympathy.

After eleven years of war, the **Treaty of Constantinople (1832)** recognised Greece as an independent nation. This was the first time in modern Europe that a nation had argued its way into existence partly through art, poetry, and shared cultural pride. The lesson would echo across the century.

## Romantic Nationalism — Language, Music, Folk Culture

Wars and treaties did not by themselves create nations. Culture did much of the quiet, deeper work. The cultural movement called **Romanticism** rejected the cold worship of reason and science. Instead, it celebrated emotion, intuition, folk tradition, and the soul of the common people.

The German philosopher **Johann Gottfried Herder** argued that the true German nation lived not in palaces but in the *das volk* — the common people — and that it spoke through their **folk songs, folk poetry, and folk dances**. To recover the *volksgeist* (the spirit of the people), one had to collect what villagers sang and told. The **Grimm brothers**, Jacob and Wilhelm, spent six years travelling from village to village in Germany, writing down fairy tales handed down through generations. *Grimms' Fairy Tales*, published from 1812, became one of the most important works of cultural nationalism in Europe. To them, fighting French cultural domination meant collecting German folktales.

This pattern repeats everywhere. In **Poland** — which had been wiped off the political map by Russia, Prussia, and Austria — language and music became weapons. The composer Karol Kurpinski turned Polish folk dances like the **polonaise** and **mazurka** into national symbols. When Russia banned the Polish language in schools, Polish priests began using Polish in church and for religious instruction. Many were jailed or sent to Siberia. To speak Polish was to resist.

Indian students will instantly recognise this picture. The **Bengal Renaissance** of the nineteenth century — Bankim writing *Anandamath* and *Vande Mataram*, Tagore composing songs, scholars collecting folk traditions — did for India exactly what Herder and the Grimms did for Germany. Nationalism almost always begins in libraries, theatres, and song before it reaches the streets.

## Hunger, 1848, and the Dream of a Republican Europe

The 1830s and 1840s were years of crushing economic hardship. Europe's population grew faster than its jobs. Migrants poured into the cities and lived in slums. Cheap machine-made cloth from industrialised England crushed small handloom weavers on the continent. Where feudalism still survived, peasants were buckled under dues and labour service. A bad harvest meant widespread **pauperism**.

A grim example was the **Silesian weavers' uprising of 1845**. Contractors had cut their wages drastically. On 4 June, a crowd of weavers marched on the contractor's mansion demanding higher wages. They were "treated with scorn and threats alternately." They smashed the windows. They broke into the storehouse and tore the cloth. The contractor returned with the army, and eleven weavers were shot dead. (Indian students may compare this to the **indigo revolts of Bengal** in 1859 — the same desperate logic, the same use of state force.)

Then came **1848** — the year of revolutions. In February, food shortages and unemployment pushed Paris into the streets. Barricades went up. King Louis Philippe fled. A National Assembly proclaimed a Republic, granted suffrage to all men above 21, and promised the right to work.

The fire spread. In Germany, Italy, Poland, and the Austro-Hungarian Empire, the educated middle classes pushed their demands for **constitutions, freedom of the press, freedom of association**, and a unified nation-state. In Germany, 831 representatives convened the **Frankfurt Parliament** in the Church of St Paul on 18 May 1848. They drafted a constitution for a German nation under a parliament-checked monarchy and offered the crown to Friedrich Wilhelm IV of Prussia. He refused — siding instead with the other monarchs against the elected assembly. The aristocracy and the army gathered against the parliament. The middle classes inside it resisted the workers' demands and lost the streets. Troops were called in. The Frankfurt experiment collapsed.

Women had taken part throughout — founding newspapers, forming associations, marching, debating. Yet women were denied the vote in the Frankfurt elections and admitted only as observers in the visitors' gallery. The liberal **Carl Welcker** argued that nature had made men "stronger, bolder, freer" and women fit for the home — that equality would "destroy the dignity of the family." Against him, **Louise Otto-Peters** founded a women's journal in 1849 with the cry: "Liberty is indivisible! Free men therefore must not tolerate to be surrounded by the unfree."

Though 1848 was crushed, the kings learned a quiet lesson. The cycle of "revolution-and-repression" was exhausting them. In the years that followed, conservative monarchies in Central and Eastern Europe began to grant exactly the changes that Western Europe had already made before 1815: **serfdom was abolished** in the Habsburg lands and in Russia, and Hungary was given more autonomy in 1867. The revolution had failed, and yet it had won half its argument.

## Unifying Germany — Bismarck's Iron and Blood

After 1848, European nationalism changed colour. It moved away from democratic, romantic, popular dreams. Conservatives saw they could use nationalist passion themselves — to expand the power of their states. The clearest example was Germany.

The liberal Frankfurt attempt of 1848 was dead. Now Prussia took the lead — but Prussia was not a liberal kingdom. Its chief minister, **Otto von Bismarck**, was the architect of a unification carried out with the **Prussian army and bureaucracy** behind him. His method was famously called **"blood and iron"** — meaning that nations are made by force, not by speeches.

Bismarck fought **three wars in seven years**:

- Against **Denmark** (1864)
- Against **Austria** (1866) — driving Austria out of German affairs
- Against **France** (1870-71) — uniting the remaining German states behind Prussia in shared victory

On the bitterly cold morning of **18 January 1871**, in the unheated Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles, an assembly of German princes, generals, and Prussian ministers — Bismarck among them — proclaimed the Prussian king **Wilhelm I** as **German Emperor (Kaiser)**.

The new Germany then focused on building modern currency, banking, legal, and judicial systems. Prussian practices became models for the rest. Bismarck's strategy looks, in some lights, like the early debates inside India's freedom movement: the question of whether unity should come through patient negotiation or through a strong centralising hand. Bismarck answered, decisively, "the hand."

## Unifying Italy — Cavour, Garibaldi, and the Red Shirts

Italy followed a different but equally remarkable path. In the mid-nineteenth century, Italy was divided into **seven states**. Only one — **Sardinia-Piedmont** — was ruled by an Italian princely house. Austria's Habsburgs ruled the north. The Pope ruled the centre. The Bourbon kings of Spain ruled the south. Even the Italian language had no single common form. Many princely states — much like India's princely states before 1947 — had to be persuaded, bullied, or annexed into a single whole.

After Mazzini's revolutionary uprisings failed in 1831 and 1848, the project of unification passed to **King Victor Emmanuel II of Sardinia-Piedmont** and his chief minister **Count Camillo de Cavour**. Cavour was no revolutionary. He was a careful, French-speaking, aristocratic diplomat — more comfortable in Paris than in Italian villages. Through a tactful alliance with France, Sardinia-Piedmont defeated the Austrian forces in 1859.

Then came the heart of the story. **Giuseppe Garibaldi**, an Italian sailor who had joined Mazzini's Young Italy in 1833 and lived in exile in South America until 1848, raised a band of armed volunteers. In **1860**, this **Expedition of the Thousand** marched into South Italy and the **Kingdom of the Two Sicilies**. Local peasants joined them. The army swelled to nearly 30,000 — wearing the famous **Red Shirts** that gave them their nickname.

The Thousand drove out the Spanish Bourbons. And then Garibaldi did something astonishing: instead of declaring a republic, he **handed the conquered south over to King Victor Emmanuel II** to complete the unification. A famous English caricature of 1859 showed Garibaldi holding the boot of "Italy" steady while the king from the north pulled it on like a leather shoe.

In **1861**, Victor Emmanuel II was proclaimed king of a united Italy. Rome was added in **1870** when France withdrew her troops from the Papal States during the Franco-Prussian War. The political map was complete — but the cultural map was not. Most ordinary Italians were illiterate and had never heard the word "Italia." The peasants of southern Italy who had supported Garibaldi reportedly believed that "La Talia" was Victor Emmanuel's wife! This unfinished cultural project — uniting the nation in people's minds, not just on maps — is the kind of work that India too undertook through Hindi films, cricket, and the national school curriculum decades after 1947.

## Britain — The Model Nation-State?

Some scholars argue that the model nation-state is **Great Britain** — but built not in a revolution, but over centuries of slow absorption.

There was no "British nation" before the eighteenth century. People on the British Isles were **English, Welsh, Scot, or Irish** — distinct ethnic groups, each with its own traditions. The English gradually grew richer and more powerful. In 1688, the English parliament seized real power from the monarchy. The **Act of Union of 1707** between England and Scotland created the "United Kingdom of Great Britain" — but in practice, it meant English domination of Scottish institutions. Scottish Highland clans that resisted were brutally suppressed. Scottish Highlanders were forbidden to speak **Gaelic** or wear their national dress; many were forced off their lands.

**Ireland** suffered worse. Deeply divided between Catholics and Protestants, the country was held down by an English-backed Protestant ruling class. After the failed revolt of **Wolfe Tone and his United Irishmen (1798)**, Ireland was forcibly incorporated into the United Kingdom in **1801**.

The symbols of the new "British" nation — the **Union Jack** flag, **God Save the King**, the English language — were aggressively promoted. The older nations survived only as junior partners.

There is a lesson here for any student of India. Nation-building always raises a difficult question: how do we make many peoples feel "one" without erasing them? Britain answered partly by absorption and partly by suppression. India after 1947 chose a different answer: **unity in diversity**, recognising many official languages and many religions. The two paths produce very different countries.

## Visualising the Nation — Marianne and Germania

Here is an odd question. How do you draw a nation? You can paint a king — but a nation is an abstract idea. Artists of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries solved this in a curious way: by personifying the nation as a **woman**. The figure did not stand for any real woman. It was an **allegory** — a way of giving an abstract idea a face.

In France, she was named **Marianne** — a popular Christian name that suggested an ordinary "woman of the people." Her attributes were borrowed from images of Liberty and the Republic — the **red cap**, the **tricolour**, the **cockade**. Marianne's statue stood in every public square. Her face was on coins and postage stamps. Whenever a French citizen handled money, the nation looked back at them.

In Germany, she was **Germania**. She wore:

- A **crown of oak leaves** — because the German oak stood for heroism.
- **Broken chains** — symbolising a people being set free.
- A **breastplate with an eagle** — the symbol of the German empire and strength.
- A **sword wrapped with an olive branch** — ready to fight, but willing to make peace.
- The **black, red, and gold tricolour** — the banned flag of the 1848 liberal-nationalists.
- **Rays of the rising sun** — the start of a new age.

The painter Philip Veit hung Germania's image from the ceiling of the Church of St Paul during the Frankfurt parliament of 1848. In an earlier version of 1836, Veit had drawn the Kaiser's crown where the broken chains now hung — a small change of detail that captured the whole 1848 dream of freedom from monarchs.

Indian readers will recognise our **Bharat Mata** as the same artistic idea — an abstract motherland given a human face, painted with attributes of strength, sacrifice, and grace. Every nation needs a face to fall in love with.

## Nationalism Turning Sour — The Balkans and Imperialism

By the last quarter of the nineteenth century, nationalism had changed mood again. The idealistic, brotherhood-of-peoples dream of Sorrieu's 1848 painting was gone. In its place came a **narrow, intolerant, war-ready creed**. Nationalist groups began to hate one another. Major European powers used the nationalist hopes of subject peoples in other empires to pursue their own **imperialist** ambitions.

The most explosive zone was the **Balkans** — modern-day Romania, Bulgaria, Albania, Greece, Macedonia, Croatia, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Slovenia, Serbia, and Montenegro. Most of these peoples were **Slavs**, and most lived under the slowly disintegrating **Ottoman Empire**. As the Ottoman grip weakened, Slavic nationalities one by one broke away. Each of them claimed independence on the grounds of nationality, using history to argue that they had once been free and had been subjugated by foreigners.

But the Balkan states were also bitterly jealous of one another. Each wanted to grab territory at the cost of its neighbours. And the great powers — **Russia, Germany, England, Austro-Hungary** — moved in to use this rivalry. Each power wanted to keep the others out of the Balkans and increase its own control. The result was a series of wars that culminated in the **First World War** in 1914.

Even further away, in the colonies, the picture darkened. European nations were now competing for empire across Africa and Asia. A British map of the period showed Britannia perched triumphantly on the globe with angels holding banners of "freedom" — while tigers, elephants, forests, and "primitive people" marked the colonies. The domination of the world had become the basis of national pride.

And yet — here is the twist — the very colonies that suffered under European imperialism began to fight back. The **anti-imperial movements** that grew up everywhere in the twentieth century were themselves nationalist. They demanded independent nation-states, built on a shared sense of "us" forged in the struggle against the coloniser. India's freedom movement is the largest example. So are Vietnam's, Indonesia's, Egypt's, Ghana's. Europe had given the world the idea of the nation-state — and the colonised used that same idea to throw Europe out.

The dream of Sorrieu's painting — a continent of free, equal nations — was not entirely false. But its journey from 1848 to 1914 to 1947 ran through revolutions, betrayals, world wars, and partitions. Nationalism turned out to be one of the most powerful forces in modern history, with a face that could be liberating one decade and murderous the next. That double face is what every student of the modern world must learn to read.

## Apply What You Have Learned

The fight for nation-states didn't end in 1914. It is still going on around you — in your family stories, your city, and the news you read. Three short stories to ground this chapter in life today.

### Where you see this in real life

**Story 1 — Sardar Patel and the princely states.** When India became independent in 1947, more than 560 princely states had to be persuaded or pressured to join the new Indian Union. Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel did something extraordinarily close to Bismarck's work in Germany and Cavour's work in Italy — except that he tried to do it mostly through negotiation rather than war. The merging of Hyderabad, Junagadh, and Kashmir into India shows how every nation-state, including ours, had to invent itself out of a patchwork. Italy's seven states became one. Germany's 39 became one. India's 565+ princely states became one.

**Story 2 — Partition stories at home.** Many Indian families have a grandparent or great-grandparent who lived through the Partition of 1947 — when the same nationalism that united India also tore it into two new nation-states. The bitter side of nationalism — the side that turned ugly in the Balkans before 1914 — is hidden inside our family albums. The aunts and uncles who came from Lahore, Karachi, or Dhaka carry stories of how a flag can also become a knife.

**Story 3 — Language and the new states of India.** The States Reorganisation Act of 1956 redrew India's internal map along linguistic lines. Andhra, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Gujarat — many of our states are creations of language pride, exactly the kind of pride that Herder spoke about in Germany and Karol Kurpinski sang about in Poland. The Telugu, Marathi, or Tamil identity you may feel inside an Indian identity is a small echo of the same forces that built Europe in the 1800s.

- **Map your mohalla's linguistic diversity.** Walk through your neighbourhood and list every language you hear in shops, on signboards, at home. Compare it to the situation Sorrieu painted in 1848 — many languages inside one space.
- **Research one Indian freedom fighter** who, like Mazzini, Garibaldi, or Bismarck, played a defining role in shaping India as a nation-state. (Try Bhagat Singh, Subhas Chandra Bose, Sarojini Naidu, Maulana Azad, or Sardar Patel.) Write a one-page biography in your own words.
