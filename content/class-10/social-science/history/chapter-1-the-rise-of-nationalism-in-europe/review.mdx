## In One Paragraph

The French Revolution of 1789 first declared that sovereignty belongs to the people, not the king — and that idea ignited a century of national struggles across Europe. Napoleon spread the revolution's administrative reforms (the Napoleonic Code, uniform laws, end of feudalism) but also imposed French rule, mixing welcome with resentment. After his defeat in 1815, the Congress of Vienna restored conservative monarchies under Metternich, but revolutionaries like Mazzini went underground with secret societies (Young Italy, Young Europe). The Greek War of Independence (1821-32) became the first successful national revolution. Romantic poets, the Grimm brothers, and folk musicians built nationalism through language and culture. The hunger years and the revolutions of 1848 demanded constitutions and nation-states; though crushed, they forced kings to abolish serfdom and concede reforms. After 1848, nationalism turned conservative: Bismarck's "blood and iron" forged Germany (1871) through three wars; Cavour's diplomacy and Garibaldi's Red Shirts unified Italy (1861-70) under Victor Emmanuel II. Britain built its nation by slow absorption of Wales, Scotland, and Ireland. By the late 1800s, however, nationalism turned narrow and aggressive, fuelling imperialism and the explosive Balkan crises that detonated into the First World War in 1914 — even as colonised peoples in India and elsewhere used the same idea to demand their own independence.

## Key Takeaways

- **1789 — French Revolution** transferred sovereignty from king to people; introduced *la patrie*, *le citoyen*, the tricolour, the National Assembly, and a uniform French nation.
- **1804 — Napoleonic Code** abolished privileges of birth, established equality before the law, protected private property, and was exported across French-occupied Europe.
- **1814-15 — Congress of Vienna**, hosted by Metternich, restored the Bourbons in France, set up buffer states, and created a conservative European order.
- **1821-32 — Greek War of Independence** mobilised Romantic European sympathy (Lord Byron, Delacroix's *Massacre at Chios*) and ended with the Treaty of Constantinople, 1832.
- **Romantic nationalism** used language, folk songs, fairy tales (the Grimm brothers in Germany), and folk dances (the polonaise and mazurka in Poland) to build a sense of national identity.
- **Mazzini** founded Young Italy (1831) and Young Europe (Berne); Metternich called him "the most dangerous enemy of our social order."
- **1848 — Year of Revolutions** — uprising in Paris ended Louis Philippe; the Frankfurt Parliament drafted a German constitution but failed when Friedrich Wilhelm IV rejected the crown; women were excluded from voting.
- **1834 — Zollverein** — Prussian-led customs union abolished tariffs and unified currencies across German states, preparing economic ground for political unification.
- **1864-71 — German unification** — Bismarck's three wars (Denmark, Austria, France); Wilhelm I proclaimed Kaiser in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles on 18 January 1871.
- **1859-71 — Italian unification** — Cavour's diplomacy defeated Austria; Garibaldi's Expedition of the Thousand (Red Shirts) took the south in 1860; Victor Emmanuel II proclaimed king of Italy in 1861; Rome added in 1870.
- **Britain** — built by Act of Union (1707) with Scotland and forcible incorporation of Ireland (1801); a top-down nation-state through absorption.
- **Marianne** (France) and **Germania** (Germany) were female allegories — abstract nations given a human face, similar to India's Bharat Mata.
- **Late 1800s — Balkans** became Europe's flashpoint as Slavic peoples broke from the Ottoman Empire; great-power rivalry led to the First World War (1914).
- **Anti-imperial nationalism** spread to colonies including India, who used the same idea of the nation-state to demand independence from European empires.

## Key Terms

- **Nationalism** — The belief that a group of people who share language, history, or culture should form their own self-governing nation-state, and that loyalty to the nation comes before loyalty to a king or empire.
- **Nation-state** — A modern state in which the majority of citizens — not only rulers — feel a shared identity, common history, and common destiny within a defined territory.
- **Liberalism** — From the Latin *liber* (free); a political philosophy that demanded individual freedom, equality before the law, government by consent, constitutions, representative parliaments, and (in economics) free trade.
- **Conservatism** — A political philosophy after 1815 that valued tradition, monarchy, the Church, social hierarchy, and family — preferring slow, controlled change to revolution.
- **Absolutism** — A form of monarchy with no checks on royal power — centralised, militarised, and repressive.
- **Utopian** — A vision of a society so perfect that it is unlikely to exist in reality (used to describe Sorrieu's dream).
- **Plebiscite** — A direct vote by which all the people of a region accept or reject a proposal.
- **Suffrage** — The right to vote.
- **Allegory** — When an abstract idea (like Liberty or Justice) is expressed through a person or thing — for example, Marianne for France or Germania for Germany.
- **Zollverein** — The 1834 Prussian-led German customs union that abolished internal tariffs and reduced thirty-plus currencies to two — economic nationalism in action.
- **Junkers** — The powerful, conservative Prussian landowners who supported Bismarck and the monarchy.
- **Ideology** — A system of ideas reflecting a particular social and political vision.
- **Feminist** — A person who believes in the social, economic, and political equality of the genders, and works to secure women's rights.
- **Ethnic** — Relating to a common racial, tribal, or cultural background that a community identifies with.
