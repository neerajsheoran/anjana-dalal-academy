## Why Did So Many Revolutions Happen Around the Same Time?

**Child:** Mother, I read the chapter and I'm confused. 1789 France, 1821 Greece, 1830 Belgium, 1848 everywhere. Why did Europe just keep exploding for sixty years? Were they all copying each other?

**Mother:** Partly yes, partly something deeper, beta. Once France in 1789 said "the king is not the country, the *people* are the country," that idea could not be locked back in a box. Travellers, students, soldiers, books — they all carried it across borders. Metternich himself said: "When France sneezes, the rest of Europe catches cold." But the reason the cold spread so fast was that *the same medicine was missing everywhere*. Every European country had angry middle-class merchants who hated customs duties, hungry peasants crushed by feudal dues, factory workers being paid nothing, and educated young men reading the same books in French and German. When one match was lit in Paris, the whole continent was already soaked in fuel.

**Child:** So it wasn't really copying — it was that the same problems existed everywhere?

**Mother:** Exactly. Think of it like our own 1857 uprising. It looked like one event, but actually sepoys, peasants, and rulers across north India all had separate grievances that suddenly came together. Europe was the same — many small fires that briefly joined into a continental blaze in 1848. The conditions had been building since Napoleon's time. And when monarchs tried to push the lid back down with the Vienna Treaty of 1815, they only built up pressure underneath. Revolutions came in waves because grievances came in waves.

## Was Napoleon a Hero or a Tyrant?

**Child:** This is what I don't understand. The chapter says Napoleon ended democracy in France and crowned himself emperor. So he was a tyrant. But then in Holland, Switzerland, and Italy he freed peasants from feudalism and gave them rule of law. So he was also a liberator? Which is it?

**Mother:** Both. And that is the most important lesson of this chapter — history doesn't give us neat heroes and villains. Napoleon was a *complicated* man who did *both* things. He destroyed political freedom — there was no parliament, no free press, no elections under him. But he also destroyed *feudalism* and old privileges. He abolished serfdom. He gave merchants uniform laws and a single currency in his territories. The Napoleonic Code of 1804 is still partly used in many countries today.

**Child:** So what should I say in an exam?

**Mother:** Say exactly that — Napoleon was a paradox. His Civil Code gave equality before the law, freed peasants, and modernised administration. But his rule also meant heavy taxes, forced conscription into his wars, censorship, and foreign domination. That is why the same towns that welcomed his soldiers as liberators in 1796 were resisting them as occupiers by 1812. It is a pattern Indians know well too — the British also came as "traders bringing rule of law" and ended as a colonial oppressor we had to throw out. The same person, the same empire, can wear two faces depending on whether you live under it or watch it from outside.

**Child:** That's actually a useful way to think about it.

**Mother:** It is the only way to think about history honestly, beta. Heroes have shadows. Villains have moments of light. Your job is to weigh both.

## How Did Bismarck Unite Germany Without Anyone Stopping Him?

**Child:** Mother, this Bismarck part is strange. Three wars in seven years, and he just *won* Germany? Why didn't the other powers — Russia, Britain, France — stop him?

**Mother:** Very good question, beta. Three reasons. First, Bismarck was a very clever diplomat. Before each war he made sure no major power would help his enemy. He went after Denmark first (a small target), then made an alliance with Austria, then turned on Austria itself, then made sure France stood alone when he went after her. He never fought two big enemies at once.

**Child:** So it was more politics than war?

**Mother:** Yes — the wars were short and decisive, but the *diplomacy* before them was everything. Second reason: Bismarck used **nationalism as a weapon**. By the 1860s, German-speaking people in many small states already felt they were one nation in waiting. Bismarck simply gave them the war they were already cheering for. After defeating France in 1870-71, when Prussia called all the German states to proclaim a single Empire, they came willingly. Third reason: the other powers were busy. Britain was focused on her empire. Russia had just lost the Crimean War. France was overconfident and miscalculated. Bismarck knew how to read the room.

**Child:** It sounds like Sardar Patel.

**Mother:** That comparison is sharp, beta. Patel too used a mix of pressure, careful timing, and the *idea* that India was already one waiting to be born. The difference is that Patel did most of his work through negotiation — Bismarck through war. But the strategic logic of "use the moment when no one can stop you, and put the idea before the army" — that is shared. Strong unifiers in history all have this in common.

## Why Did Garibaldi Just Give Italy to a King?

**Child:** Mother, this part really shocked me. Garibaldi marches with a thousand volunteers, conquers all of southern Italy, the peasants love him — and then he just hands the whole thing to King Victor Emmanuel and walks away? Why would a republican do that?

**Mother:** It is one of the most beautiful and frustrating moments in modern history. Garibaldi believed in *Italy* more than in his own ideology. He was a republican — he wanted a free, equal Italy without kings. But he saw that if he tried to create a republic in the south while Cavour and the king ruled the north, Italy would be split forever. So he made a painful choice: a *united Italy under a king* was better than a *divided Italy with one half republican*.

**Child:** That's actually quite noble.

**Mother:** Yes — and quite rare in history. Most revolutionaries hold on to their banner till the end. Garibaldi let his go for a bigger prize. There is a famous English cartoon from 1859 of Garibaldi holding the boot of "Italy" steady while Victor Emmanuel pulls it on like a leather shoe — that captures the relationship perfectly. The radical hero builds the country; the king takes the credit.

**Child:** Did Indian leaders ever do this?

**Mother:** Many times, beta. Think of how the Congress and the princes had to compromise after Independence — radical socialists like Jayaprakash Narayan, Marxists, Gandhians, and conservative industrialists all had to sit at the same table to write our Constitution. They each gave up something. Without that, India would have torn into pieces. Nation-building is, in the end, the art of giving up your perfect vision so that something imperfect can survive. That is what Garibaldi understood.

## When Did Nationalism Stop Being a Good Thing?

**Child:** This is the part that scares me, Mother. The chapter says nationalism started as something noble — Sorrieu's painting, brotherhood of nations, freedom for everyone. And then by 1914 it had become the thing that started the First World War. How did it turn so evil?

**Mother:** That is the deepest question in the whole chapter, beta. And I will give you my honest answer: nationalism became dangerous when it stopped being about *we are equal* and started being about *we are better*.

**Child:** What changed?

**Mother:** Three things. First, after 1848 the educated middle classes who had led democratic nationalism were defeated, and *conservative governments* took over the nationalist project. Bismarck and his kind made nationalism a tool of state power, not popular freedom. Second, **imperialism** entered the picture. As European nations competed for colonies in Africa and Asia, "my nation" came to mean "my empire" — and a great empire requires looking down on other peoples. The British map showing Britannia sitting on the globe with elephants and "primitive people" at her feet is exactly this. Third, in the Balkans, many small nationalisms started clashing with each other. Serbs, Croats, Bulgarians, Greeks — each used their own history to claim land that overlapped. Nationalism that began as *we are not less than you* became *you are not equal to us*.

**Child:** So nationalism is not bad, but it can go bad?

**Mother:** Exactly, beta. Like fire. Fire cooks your food and warms your home. But unwatched, it burns the house down. The same nationalism that freed Greece in 1832 lit the fuse for the First World War in 1914. And after the war, the same idea inspired Gandhi, Bose, and Nehru to throw the British out of India in 1947. The fire is the same. The question is always: who is tending it, and what is it being used for?

## What Should I Take Away From All This?

**Child:** Mother, this is a long chapter with so many dates and names. If I had to remember only the most important thing, what should it be?

**Mother:** Sit down. Let me say it slowly. There are three lessons.

**One — nations are made, not found.** Germany, Italy, Britain, even France were not natural objects sitting in the world waiting to be discovered. They were *built* over decades and centuries through wars, books, songs, customs unions, schools, and shared struggles. India was the same. Nothing about a country is automatic.

**Two — the same force can do good and bad.** Nationalism freed Greece, Italy, Germany, and eventually India. It also caused the First World War, the Holocaust, the Partition, and many tragedies that came later. You will spend your life seeing this force at work. Learn to read it carefully. Ask: is *this* nationalism the kind that lifts people up, or the kind that pushes others down?

**Three — culture matters as much as politics.** Bismarck won wars, but it was Herder and the Grimm brothers who first made Germans feel German. Cavour did diplomacy, but it was Mazzini's writings that made young Italians believe Italy was real. In India, it was Bankim's *Vande Mataram*, Tagore's songs, the freedom movement's tricolour — these did half the work before the political leaders did the other half. So if you ever want to change a country, beta, do not just change its laws. Change what it sings, what it reads, what it celebrates.

**Child:** That is a lot to remember.

**Mother:** Then remember it as a single sentence: **"A nation is what its people are willing to imagine together."** Hold on to that, and the rest of this chapter will stay with you.
