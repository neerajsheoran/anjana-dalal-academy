## In One Paragraph

Industrialisation did not begin with factories. For two centuries before the first chimney was built in Lancashire, European merchants used a **proto-industrial putting-out system**, paying peasant families in the countryside to spin and weave for an international market. The first cotton mill came up in England in the 1730s, but factory production spread slowly — even in 1900 less than 20 per cent of British workers were in advanced industrial sectors, and hand labour remained important because it was cheap, flexible, and skilled. Meanwhile India, the world's biggest textile exporter for centuries, was systematically **deindustrialised** by colonial trade policy. The East India Company crushed Indian merchants, forced weavers to sell at low prices through gomasthas, and then flooded India with cheap Manchester cloth — cotton piece-goods fell from 33% of India's exports in 1811 to just 3% by 1851. From the 1850s, Indian businessmen — China traders like the Tatas, Petits, Birlas — built mills in Bombay and Ahmedabad, helped by the swadeshi movement and the First World War. Small handloom production survived in niches like Banarasi and Kanchipuram and even expanded in the twentieth century with the fly shuttle. The age of industries was thus not a clean story of progress, but a tangle of new factories, surviving crafts, lost livelihoods, and rebuilt industries.

## Key Takeaways

- **Proto-industrialisation** was large-scale rural production for international markets, organised by merchants under the **putting-out system**, long before factories existed
- The first English cotton mill was set up by **Richard Arkwright**; the first symbol of the new industrial era was **cotton**, and the leading sector after the 1840s was **iron and steel**
- Industrial change was much slower than popular myth suggests — even at the end of the nineteenth century **less than 20 per cent** of Britain's workforce was in advanced industrial sectors
- British industrialists often preferred **hand labour** because workers were plentiful and cheap, demand was seasonal, and intricate products (500 kinds of hammers!) needed skilled hands
- Industrial workers faced **low wages, seasonal unemployment, and the threat of new machines** — women weavers famously attacked the spinning jenny when it threatened their livelihoods
- Before the British, **India was the world's leading textile exporter** — Bengal muslin, Coromandel chintz, Patola, Banarasi silk, Kashmir shawls; trade flowed through **Surat, Masulipatam and Hoogly**
- After 1760, the East India Company destroyed the Indian textile order — eliminated rival merchants, used **gomasthas** to control weavers, locked them in with advances, and used sepoys to enforce delivery
- From the early 1800s, **Manchester goods flooded India**, while Indian cloth was kept out of Britain by tariffs — Indian cotton exports collapsed from 33% (1811) to 3% (1851); this is the **deindustrialisation of India**
- The first Indian cotton mill opened in **Bombay in 1854**; the **first Ahmedabad mill in 1861**; most capital came from Indian merchants who had got rich in the **China opium-tea trade** — Tata, Petit, Birla, Hukumchand
- European Managing Agencies (Bird Heiglers, Andrew Yule, Jardine Skinner) dominated Indian industry till the First World War; Indian businessmen were even barred from European chambers of commerce
- Most Indian industrial workers came from villages around the mill cities — over half of Bombay's millworkers in 1911 came from **Ratnagiri district**; jobs were controlled by powerful **jobbers**
- **Small-scale and handloom production never died** — even in 1931 only 10% of industrial workers were in registered factories; handloom production almost trebled between 1900 and 1940 thanks to the **fly shuttle** and specialised weaves
- **Advertising** was born with industrialisation — Manchester used Hindu gods and Indian kings on its labels; Indian mills used the same imagery for the **swadeshi** message of "use Indian goods"

## Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Proto-industrialisation | The phase of large-scale rural production for international markets before the rise of factories |
| Putting-out system | System where merchants gave raw material and advances to peasant families to produce cloth in their homes |
| Spinning Jenny | Machine invented by James Hargreaves in 1764 that let one worker spin several threads at once |
| Gomastha | Paid Company servant who supervised weavers, collected cloth, and checked quality on behalf of the East India Company |
| Deindustrialisation | The decline of an existing industry caused by external pressure — used here for the destruction of Indian textile manufacturing under colonial rule |
| Fly Shuttle | Mechanical weaving device worked by ropes and pulleys, which doubled handloom productivity in the early twentieth century |
| Managing Agency | European trading firm that mobilised capital, set up joint-stock companies, and managed Indian industries till the First World War |
| Jobber | An old, trusted worker hired by factory owners to recruit new hands, settle them in the city, and discipline them — often in exchange for bribes and gifts |
| Swadeshi | Movement that called for the boycott of foreign goods and the use of Indian-made products, beginning around 1905 |
| Piece-goods | Cloth sold in standard-sized pieces — the main form in which both Indian textiles and later Manchester textiles were traded |
