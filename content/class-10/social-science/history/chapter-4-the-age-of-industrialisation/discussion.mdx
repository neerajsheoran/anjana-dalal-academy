chapterRef: Chapter 4 — The Age of Industrialisation

**Child:** Maa, if India was making fine cloth way before England, why didn't WE have the industrial revolution?

**Mother:** That is the most honest question to begin this chapter with. The textbook says India was the world's biggest exporter of fine cloth till the eighteenth century. Bengal muslin was so fine that Mughal poets joked it could pass through a finger ring. And yet the first big mill of the world came up in Lancashire, not Murshidabad. Why? It is worth taking time to answer this properly, because most students learn this chapter as "Britain progressed, India fell behind" — which hides more than it reveals.

## Why Britain First, Not India or China?

**Child:** So why DID Britain get there first?

**Mother:** Historians give several reasons, and no single one is enough.

**One — Britain had cheap energy.** It sat on enormous reserves of **coal** very close to its surface. Coal is what powered the steam engine. India had coal too, but mostly deep, harder to reach, and the colonial administration did not want to invest in mining it for Indian use.

**Two — Britain had captive markets.** A British factory in 1820 could ship its cloth to India, the West Indies, Australia, parts of Africa — and the colonial government made sure local industries could not compete. So a Manchester factory was not just selling to ten million British people. It was selling to half the world, with tariffs protecting it both ways.

**Three — Britain had finance.** London had a banking system, joint-stock companies, insurance. A merchant could raise capital to build a mill. In India, the **hundi system** of bankers was sophisticated, but the colonial government channelled finance towards what served Britain — opium, raw cotton, indigo — not towards Indian manufacturing.

**Four — Britain had political conditions that favoured factories.** No internal customs, no warring kingdoms taking tax on each road, no foreign power skimming profits.

**Five — and this is what hurts to say — India had a hostile colonial state.** When Indian industry might have grown, the East India Company was actively dismantling it. There was no Indian government to protect Indian weavers. There were no protective tariffs for Indian goods. The state worked for Manchester, not for Bengal.

**Child:** So it wasn't that we were "behind." We were held down?

**Mother:** Both, and the first led to the second. When the British arrived, India was already a manufacturing power. Then we were systematically deindustrialised — that is the precise word — and our own industrialisation did not really begin until **1854** in Bombay, almost a century after Arkwright's mill in England.

**Child:** Why didn't China industrialise first either?

**Mother:** A very good question. China had the technology — paper, printing, gunpowder, fine porcelain. But China in the eighteenth century was a stable, inward-looking empire that did not need or want a foreign trade revolution. The Chinese state did not encourage merchants the way Europe did. And then, like India, China was attacked from outside — the Opium Wars in the 1840s opened China up to British commercial pressure. So China's story is different from India's, but the end result was similar — it did not industrialise on its own terms in the nineteenth century.

## Were the First Factories Better Than Cottage Industries?

**Child:** Were the factories actually better than the village cottages? The textbook makes it sound terrible inside the mills.

**Mother:** This is the question that decides how you read the chapter. The honest answer is — for the **factory owner**, factories were enormously better. For the **worker**, they were a mixed bag, and often worse.

Let me give you a picture. Before the factory, a Lancashire weaver worked in his cottage. He owned his loom. He decided when to start work in the morning. If his daughter was sick, he could take her to the doctor. If the harvest was good, he wove less and worked the fields more. He had a measure of dignity. He was poor — but he was not a servant.

After the factory came, that same weaver could not survive on cottage weaving — the mill produced cloth so cheaply that no hand-weaver could match the price. So he had to walk to Manchester, give up his loom, and join the mill. There, he worked twelve to fourteen hours a day. The whistle decided when he ate. He could not visit a sick child. If he was late, he was fined. If he protested, he was sacked. He had a wage — but he had lost control of his time.

Was he better off? In some ways, yes. The wage was steadier than hand-weaving in a bad year. The city had more shops, more entertainment, eventually better schools for his children. But in dignity, in autonomy, in the rhythm of life — he had lost a lot.

**Child:** What about in India?

**Mother:** The Indian story is more straightforward. When the weaver of Bengal was working under the old system — selling to whichever merchant gave the best price — he was poor but free. After the gomastha came, he was poor and a servant. He took an advance, he was tied to the Company, he could not bargain. The textbook describes weavers being beaten by sepoys for delay. There is no version of that story where the worker is better off.

In the Bombay mills of the 1900s, conditions were also harsh — ten-hour shifts ending at 3 am, asthma from cotton dust, child labour. But at least the mills were owned by Indians, the wages stayed in India, and the workers were not under colonial whips.

I am not romanticising the village. Village life had hierarchies, caste violence, debt. But the truth is that **early industrialisation was not a clean upward step for the worker.** It took fifty years of trade unions, factory laws, and political struggle before workers' lives in mill cities became visibly better. That fight is still going on.

## Why Did Indian Weavers Suffer Under British Rule?

**Child:** I keep going back to this. The textbook says the Company appointed gomasthas. But weavers were already selling to merchants before the British came. What changed?

**Mother:** The key word is **competition**. Before the British, a Bengal weaver lived in a village where the French, the Dutch, the Portuguese, the English, and several Indian supply-merchants all came to buy cloth. If one offered a low price, the weaver could refuse and sell to another. That competition kept prices reasonable.

After 1757 — after the Company won political power in Bengal — the Company crushed the competition. The French, Dutch, Portuguese were pushed out by war and treaty. Indian merchants were starved of credit. The Company set itself up as the **only buyer**.

Now imagine you grew vegetables and only ONE buyer in the whole district was allowed to buy them. He sets the price. You cannot refuse. That is exactly what happened to the Bengal weavers.

**Child:** Why did weavers keep taking advances if it was so bad?

**Mother:** Because they had no choice. The advance bought them rice, paid for raw thread, kept the family alive while the cloth was being woven. Once you took an advance, you were locked in. Many weavers tried to refuse and were forced to take advances by the gomastha. Others were beaten when they were slow.

The most heartbreaking part is what happened next. By the 1810s the Company didn't even need Indian cloth — Manchester was producing it cheaper. So the same Bengal weaver was now being **forbidden** to sell to anyone else **and** the Company was buying less and less. He was crushed twice.

**Child:** What happened to those weavers?

**Mother:** Some migrated to other villages where their relatives still had looms. Some moved to the cities. A lot of them — and this is the saddest part — gave up cloth altogether and became **agricultural labourers** on land they did not own. A skilled craftsman whose family had woven for ten generations ended up daily-waging in a field, watching his loom be sold for scrap.

Bihar was particularly hard hit. The Commissioner of Patna reported in the 1830s that the cloth trade of Jahanabad and Behar had "entirely ceased" because of Manchester cloth. Whole towns lost their industry. Some places never recovered.

I think about the families. My great-grandmother on my mother's side came from a weaving family in eastern UP. By her generation they had become small farmers — they remembered being weavers, but no one wove any more. That is a private memory of deindustrialisation.

## How Did Bombay and Ahmedabad Mills Survive?

**Child:** If the British were so against Indian industry, how did Bombay and Ahmedabad mills come up at all?

**Mother:** Through a clever combination of money, strategy, and luck.

The **money** came from one specific place — the **China trade**. From the late eighteenth century, the British were exporting opium grown in India to China and bringing back Chinese tea to England. They needed Indian agents to do the dirty work — financing the opium, shipping it, selling it in Canton. Many Indians became junior partners — Parsis in Bombay, Marwaris in Calcutta, Bohras in Surat.

By the 1830s, some of these men were quite rich. **Dwarkanath Tagore** in Calcutta. **Jamsetjee Jeejeebhoy** in Bombay — a Parsi weaver's son who owned a fleet of ships. **Jamsetjee Tata** later in the century — another Parsi, who took the China-trade fortune and built a steel city, Jamshedpur.

These men could have spent their money on big houses and ended there. Instead they took a risk — they put it into factories. In 1854, the first Bombay cotton mill. In 1861, the first Ahmedabad mill. In 1912, the first Indian steel works at Jamshedpur.

**Child:** Were they patriotic?

**Mother:** Some were, some were just shrewd businessmen. Dwarkanath Tagore explicitly said India would develop through "westernisation and industrialisation." Jamsetjee Tata was a deep nationalist — he sent his son R.D. Tata to negotiate steel deals, he insisted on Indian engineers, he started the Indian Institute of Science in Bangalore to train them. The Tatas in particular saw industry as a national project.

The **strategy** was clever. They didn't compete head-on with Manchester. Manchester was sending **cloth**. The Bombay mills initially made **yarn** — cotton thread, not finished cloth. The yarn was sold to handloom weavers in India and exported to China. Manchester did not bother with yarn, so there was no direct fight.

After 1900, when Chinese mills began producing their own yarn, the Indian industrialists switched to **cloth**. By that time the **swadeshi movement** had begun, and ordinary Indians were boycotting foreign cloth — so Indian mills had a growing home market.

The **luck** came in 1914 — the First World War. British mills stopped sending cloth because they were making war material. Indian mills suddenly had the whole Indian market and big war contracts on top. Production exploded. Many old mills ran multiple shifts. New mills came up. By the time the war ended in 1918, Indian mills were strong enough that Manchester never recovered its grip on the Indian market.

**Child:** So we got our industrial revolution finally?

**Mother:** A partial one. Most heavy industry — railways, locomotives, machine tools, chemicals — was still imported or owned by British managing agencies until Independence. Real industrialisation of heavy goods only began after 1947, with Nehru's planning. But the cotton textile sector, after one of the worst deindustrialisations in history, did rebuild itself. That is not a small achievement. The Tata steel works in Jamshedpur is still running. Many Ahmedabad mill chimneys still stand. The seeds planted in the 1850s grew, slowly, into the industrial India of today.

## What Role Did Children and Women Play in Factories?

**Child:** The chapter mentions women and children working in the mills. Was that normal?

**Mother:** It was completely normal in the nineteenth century — in Britain, in America, in India, everywhere. Factories in their early decades ran on the labour of women and children.

In Britain, when the spinning jenny was introduced, women who had earned their bread by hand-spinning at home were the first to be pushed out — because the jenny did their work faster. That is why the textbook describes women attacking the machines with axes. In 1790 a magistrate wrote about "a lawless Banditti of colliers and their wives" advancing on a mill — the wives were leading because their livelihood was being destroyed.

In factories themselves, women worked in **specific sections** — usually spinning, where the work was steady and required smaller hands. Men did the heavier weaving and the engine-room work. Children — sometimes as young as eight — were employed for jobs like cleaning under running machines, because they were small enough to crawl into the cogs. Injuries were horrific.

**Child:** Why did parents send their children to work?

**Mother:** Because the family could not eat on one wage. A father in a mill earned just enough for two or three people. So the wife went into the mill too. So did the older children. The youngest minded the babies at home. It was a survival arithmetic.

In India, in the Bombay mills of the early 1900s, women were a quarter of the workforce in cotton spinning. Children worked in significant numbers until the **Factories Act of 1881** (and stronger laws in 1891, 1911, 1922) gradually restricted their hours. Even then, enforcement was weak. In Ahmedabad, in the 1920s, **Gandhiji** got involved in supporting textile workers — that movement led to the formation of the **Textile Labour Association** under Anasuya Sarabhai, one of the first big trade unions in India.

**Child:** Did things get better?

**Mother:** Slowly, through three things: **trade unions, factory laws, and political pressure**.

Trade unions fought for shorter hours, better wages, weekly leave. Factory laws made child labour illegal under certain ages and capped working hours. Political movements — like the nationalist movement after 1920 — put pressure on owners and government to treat workers as human beings.

By the 1930s, the ten-hour shifts ending at 3 am that Bhai Bhosle's father suffered were beginning to disappear in the bigger mills. But conditions for the smaller workshops — the ones that employ 90 per cent of Indian industrial workers even today — remained brutal. They still are. Today's brick-kiln workers, today's garment-stitchers in Tiruppur, today's tea-pickers in Assam — many of them work under conditions that the early-twentieth-century mill worker would recognise.

## What Does All This Mean for Today's India?

**Child:** What is the point of learning all this? The mills are gone, the British are gone, even our cotton mills are not as important as they were. Why does it matter?

**Mother:** Three reasons. Big ones.

**One — it changes the way you see "progress."** When the news shows a new highway or a new airport or a new factory, you are taught to feel pride. But ask: who lost what so this could be built? Whose land was acquired? Whose old industry was shut down? Whose hand-work became uneconomical? Every act of "progress" has a cost — and that cost is rarely paid by the ones celebrating the inauguration. The age of industrialisation teaches that.

**Two — it changes the way you see global trade.** Today there is a lot of talk about "free trade" — let goods flow freely between countries, the cheapest producer will win, this is fair. The nineteenth century was a free trade age too. Manchester cloth flowed freely into India and broke Indian weavers. India "freely" exported raw cotton and "freely" imported finished cloth. Free trade between unequal partners is not really free. It transfers wealth from the weak to the strong. That lesson is exactly why India today has tariffs and "Make in India" — to protect domestic industry the way Britain protected its own in 1820. It is also why countries argue so bitterly at the World Trade Organisation. Once you have read this chapter, those arguments make sense.

**Three — it changes the way you see your own choices.** Every time you buy something, you are part of a supply chain. The cotton in your shirt may have been grown in Telangana, spun in Tirupur, stitched in Bangladesh, branded in Mumbai, sold in Delhi. Each step earns somebody. The most exploited person in that chain is usually the one who actually grew the cotton or stitched the shirt. The least exploited is the brand. That is industrialisation in its modern form.

Khadi is not just a cloth — it is a statement. It says: I want the value to stay with the spinner, with the weaver, with the village. When Gandhiji asked Indians to spin and wear khadi in the 1920s, he was not being romantic. He was making a precise economic argument — the deindustrialisation of India can only be reversed if the value of cloth goes back to Indian hands.

You don't have to wear only khadi. But every time you buy any product, think for a moment: who actually got the money from this transaction? And what would it cost me to choose differently?

**Child:** That feels like a lot of weight on a t-shirt, Maa.

**Mother:** Yes, but only because the t-shirt actually carries that weight. The history of industrialisation is the history of where the money went, who lost their craft, who got the factory, who was forced into the mine. Once you see it, you cannot unsee it. The point of learning the chapter is to get that vision — and then to use it for the rest of your life.

The age of industries is not over, beta. It is being rewritten every day in Bengaluru's IT parks, in Gurgaon's car plants, in Tiruppur's garment sheds, in the agarbatti workshops of Bengaluru. The actors have changed. The questions are the same. Who controls the work? Who keeps the value? Who decides whose craft survives? That is what every generation has to answer for itself.
