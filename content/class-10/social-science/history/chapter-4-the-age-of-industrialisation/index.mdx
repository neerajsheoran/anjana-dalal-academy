## The Picture We Have Been Sold

Open almost any school textbook on modern history and you will meet the same image: a tall brick chimney, smoke rising into a grey English sky, a goddess of progress floating above with the words "1900 — Dawn of the Century" inscribed on her banner. Around her swirl the symbols of a new age — railway engines, cameras, printing presses, factories.

That picture tells a story. The story says: **industrialisation is progress. Factories are modern. Hand-work is the past. The West invented this future, and everyone else slowly caught up.**

It is a flattering story. It is also misleading. The real history of industrialisation is messier, more interesting, and — for us in India — far more painful. Britain did not "discover" the factory in some clean moment of genius. Factories grew slowly, fitfully, alongside cottage industries that survived for over a century. India was not "backward" before the British arrived — Indian weavers were supplying the finest cloth in the world. And the rise of Manchester mills did not just modernise Britain; it **deindustrialised India**, throwing lakhs of Bengali, Bihari and Tamil weavers out of work.

This chapter follows that fuller story. We begin in the European countryside, long before the first chimney was built. We watch the factory come up in Lancashire. We then sail east to Surat and Hoogly, watch the old Indian textile trade collapse, see the gomastha march into a village with sepoys, and finally meet the Parsi and Marwari businessmen who built Bombay and Ahmedabad mills against heavy odds.

By the end you should be able to answer, in your own words, a hard question: **if industrialisation is supposed to be progress, why did so many Indians experience it as ruin?**

## Before the Factory — Proto-Industrialisation

A common assumption is that before factories, people made things only for their own village — a tailor stitching for the neighbours, a potter selling pots at the local mela. Factory production then arrived and suddenly people produced for the world.

This is wrong. **Long before factories existed, there was already large-scale industrial production for an international market.** Historians call this earlier phase **proto-industrialisation** — "proto" meaning "the first or early form of something."

Here is how it worked in seventeenth and eighteenth century Europe. Merchants in towns wanted to produce more cloth, more rope, more nails, because demand was rising as European empires expanded across the world. But they could not expand production inside the towns. Why? Because **urban craft guilds** — associations of craftsmen — strictly controlled who could enter a trade, who could employ how many apprentices, what prices could be charged. Guilds had monopoly rights granted by kings. A new merchant could not simply open a workshop in town.

So merchants went to the **countryside**. There the rules of the guilds did not apply. There, poor peasants and cottagers — people who had lost access to the common village lands as fields were enclosed by landlords — were desperate for additional income. A small plot could not feed a whole family.

The system that grew up was elegant. The merchant advanced money or raw material to a peasant household. The wife and daughters spun yarn. The husband wove cloth. The merchant then collected the cloth and moved it through a chain — a wool stapler sorted the wool, fullers thickened the cloth, dyers coloured it, finishers in London polished it for export. This is called the **putting-out system**. Production stayed in the village, but the merchant controlled the supply chain.

At each stage about twenty to twenty-five workers handled the goods of one merchant. A single clothier could therefore command the labour of several hundred families across many villages. The village and the town were not separate worlds — they were tied by an invisible thread of credit, cloth, and commerce.

This was already industrialisation. It was just not yet **factory** industrialisation.

## The Coming of the Factory

The earliest factories in England appeared in the 1730s, but they remained a curiosity for half a century. It was only in the late eighteenth century that the **factory** became the dominant symbol of industrial life. The first symbol of the new era was a humble thread — **cotton**.

In 1760 Britain imported 2.5 million pounds of raw cotton to feed its weavers. By 1787, just twenty-seven years later, that figure had jumped to 22 million pounds — a nine-fold rise. What changed?

A series of inventions in the eighteenth century — the spinning jenny by James Hargreaves, the water frame, the mule, the power loom — sped up each step of cotton manufacture. Then **Richard Arkwright** put all these expensive machines under one roof and set up the first true **cotton mill**. Within the mill all the processes — carding, spinning, weaving — happened in one building, watched by one manager, powered by one source of water or steam.

This was revolutionary not just because of speed, but because of **control**. In the village, a merchant could not really know if a weaver was using good yarn, working full hours, hiding part of the cloth, or lacing his thread with mud to add weight. In the mill, every worker was visible. Quality could be checked. Labour could be disciplined. Output could be predicted.

By the early nineteenth century, factories had become "intimate parts of the English landscape," as one historian put it. Tall chimneys belching black smoke became the new skyline of Manchester, Leeds, Birmingham. Visitors were dazzled. So dazzled, in fact, that they almost forgot the thousands of small workshops and rural cottages where production still continued.

## The Real Pace of Change

You might imagine that, once the factory came, everything changed overnight. Reality was slower.

**First**, only two industries truly led the revolution: **cotton** (the leading sector till the 1840s) and **iron and steel** (taking over after the 1840s, especially as railways spread from England in the 1840s and into the colonies from the 1860s). By 1873 Britain was exporting iron and steel worth £77 million — twice the value of its cotton exports.

**Second**, the new factory industries could not easily displace traditional ones. Even at the end of the nineteenth century, **less than 20 per cent of the British workforce was employed in advanced industrial sectors**. Most workers still worked in workshops, on farms, in small trades.

**Third**, even the "traditional" sectors were not standing still. Food processing, building, pottery, glass-work, leather, furniture — these absorbed small, quiet innovations and kept growing. The brilliance of the steam mill grabbed historians' attention; the slow improvements in pottery did not. But the pottery sector employed more people.

**Fourth**, technology spread slowly because it was expensive and unreliable. James Watt patented his improved steam engine in 1781. His friend Matthew Boulton manufactured it. Yet for years they found no buyers. At the start of the nineteenth century — **twenty years after Watt's invention** — all of England had only 321 steam engines in operation. Eighty in cotton mills, nine in wool mills, the rest scattered in mines and ironworks. The "most powerful new technology" of the age was, in practice, slow to spread.

The honest picture is this: the typical worker in the mid-nineteenth century was not a machine operator. He was a craftsperson or a labourer working with his hands.

## Hand Labour and Steam Power

If machines were so wonderful, why did so many British industrialists keep using hand labour?

The answer is simple — **Britain had no shortage of human hands**. Poor peasants and vagrants were pouring into the cities from the countryside. When labour is plentiful, wages stay low. Why invest in an expensive machine that might break down, when you could hire ten women for the same monthly cost?

There was also the matter of **seasonality**. Gas works needed more workers in winter. Bookbinders rushed before Christmas. Shipyards repaired vessels in the cold months. For these uneven, seasonal demands, hand labour was perfect — hire them when needed, let them go when not.

And then there was **quality and variety**. Machines were good at producing standardised, identical goods — uniforms, sacks, plain cloth, simple plates. But the market also wanted intricate, varied things. Mid-nineteenth century Britain produced **500 varieties of hammers and 45 kinds of axes**. No machine could do that. Only a skilled hand could.

Among the British upper classes — the aristocrats, the bourgeoisie — handmade goods were actually preferred. They were better finished, individually designed, a mark of refinement. Machine-made goods were considered cheap. They were sent off for **export to the colonies**.

This is worth holding on to. The richest people in industrial Britain quietly chose hand-made for themselves and shipped machine-made to India and Africa.

## Life in the New Industrial City

For the worker, the new industrial city was a hard place.

The countryside had emptied as enclosures pushed peasants out. Hundreds tramped to Manchester, London, Glasgow each year. Whether you got a job depended on who you knew — a brother in a factory, a cousin in a workshop. Without connections you waited under bridges, slept in night shelters, or queued for a place in the **Casual Wards** maintained by the Poor Law authorities — a humiliating system where the destitute were stripped, examined for disease, washed down, and made to do hard labour in exchange for one night's bed.

Wages did rise gradually through the early nineteenth century. But the headline figure hid two cruel realities. First, during the long Napoleonic Wars prices rose so sharply that real wages — what a coin could actually buy — fell. Second, **periods of employment** mattered more than wage rates. A worker with a high daily wage but only ninety days of work in a year was poorer than a worker on lower wages but full-year employment. In the slump of the 1830s, unemployment shot up to between 35 and 75 per cent in some industrial regions.

The **fear of unemployment** also poisoned the relationship between workers and machines. When the spinning jenny was introduced in the woollen industry, women who had survived by hand-spinning saw their livelihood vanish. Many of them attacked the new machines with axes. A magistrate's report from 1790 described "a lawless Banditti of colliers and their wives" advancing on a manufacturer's mill to cut his spinning engines to pieces. The men, the magistrate noted with relief, were "more open to conviction." The women were not.

After the 1840s, the construction boom in cities began to soak up labour. Roads were widened, drains laid, railway stations built. The number of workers in the transport industry doubled in the 1840s, then doubled again in the next thirty years. The Industrial Revolution was, in the end, not just a story of factory chimneys — it was also a story of brick-layers, navvies, and tunnel-diggers.

## India Before the British — A Textile Superpower

Now sail east. While Britain was building its first mills, India had already been the world's greatest textile exporter for **at least four hundred years**.

The roll-call of Indian cloth was legendary. **Bengal muslin** — so fine that Mughal poets said a length of forty yards could pass through a finger ring. **Coromandel chintz** — block-printed cotton from the south, prized in European drawing rooms. **Patola** from Gujarat. **Kashmir shawls**. **Daccai jamdani**. **Banarasi silk brocade**. **Madras kerchiefs** and **lungis**, which kept their reputation right into the twentieth century.

This was not a backward "cottage economy." It was a sophisticated commercial system. Armenian and Persian merchants carried bales of Punjab cotton on camel-back across the Hindu Kush, through Kabul, to Central Asia and Persia. By sea, **Surat on the Gujarat coast** linked India to the Gulf and the Red Sea. **Masulipatam** on the Coromandel coast and **Hoogly in Bengal** had trade routes to Southeast Asia — Java, Burma, the Malay peninsula.

The trade was financed and managed almost entirely by **Indian merchants and bankers**. Supply merchants advanced money to weaver families. Brokers at the ports negotiated prices with foreign buyers. **Hundis** — bills of exchange — moved money safely across thousands of kilometres. In the late seventeenth century, the gross value of trade passing through Surat alone was about **Rs 16 million** a year.

Now hold that picture in your mind — Surat in the year 1700, ships from twenty countries in its harbour, Indian bankers writing hundis, weaver families in Bengal working looms that produced cloth so fine no European mill could match it. Then ask: how did this whole world collapse?

## What Happened to the Weavers

The answer, in one word, is **colonialism**. But colonialism worked through specific, traceable steps — not magic.

**Step one: undercutting Indian merchants.** From the 1750s onwards, European trading companies — especially the **English East India Company** — gradually won concessions from local rulers, then monopoly rights to trade. The old ports of Surat and Hoogly slowly decayed because Indian merchants could no longer freely operate from them. Trade shifted to **Bombay and Calcutta**, which were controlled by the Company. By the 1740s, Surat's annual trade had crashed from Rs 16 million to Rs 3 million — an 80 per cent collapse in two generations.

**Step two: direct control over weavers.** After the East India Company won political power in Bengal (1757) and the Carnatic (1760s), it could **assert monopoly rights** in a way it never could before. Earlier, French, Dutch, Portuguese and local traders had competed for cloth in the same villages, and the weaver could play one against the other to get a better price. The Company ended this competition by force.

It appointed a paid servant called the **gomastha** to supervise the weavers, collect cloth, and check quality. Then it locked the weaver in with **advances**. A weaver who took an advance from the Company had to sell only to the Company. He could not bargain. He could not refuse. The price the Company paid was miserably low.

Older supply merchants had lived inside weaving villages, lent money in emergencies, married into local families. The new gomasthas were outsiders. They marched in with **sepoys and peons**. They beat weavers who delayed delivery. They flogged those who tried to sell elsewhere.

By the 1770s and 1780s, in district after district of Bengal and Carnatic, weavers were **deserting their villages**. Some migrated to villages where their relatives still had looms. Others gave up cloth altogether and became agricultural labourers. In some places, weavers along with village traders rose up in armed revolt against the Company.

This was not industrialisation. It was the **destruction of an existing industry by force**.

## Manchester Comes to India

The worst was yet to come.

Up to about 1800, India was still exporting cloth — the Company's officials in London were still keen to keep the trade going. A Company official named Henry Patullo confidently wrote in 1772 that demand for Indian cloth "could never reduce, since no other nation produced goods of the same quality."

He was wrong by exactly one human lifetime.

As cotton mills came up in Manchester, English industrialists demanded **two things** from their government. First, **import duties on Indian cloth coming into Britain** — so Manchester goods could rule the British home market. Second, **free entry for Manchester cloth into India** — no protection for Indian weavers in their own home market.

The colonial government granted both.

The numbers tell the story. In 1811-12, cotton piece-goods made up **33 per cent** of India's exports — India was still a major cloth exporter. By 1850-51, that figure had collapsed to **3 per cent**. Meanwhile, imports of British cotton cloth into India rose from almost nothing in 1800 to **31 per cent of all Indian imports by 1850**, and over **50 per cent by the 1870s**.

A district report from Bihar around 1830 captures the mood: "It appears that twenty years ago, a brisk trade was carried on in the manufacture of cloth at Jahanabad and Behar, which has in the former place entirely ceased, while in the latter the amount of manufacture is very limited, in consequence of the cheap and durable goods from Manchester with which the Native manufactures are unable to compete." A Census Report on the **Koshtis** — a weaving community of the Central Provinces — reported in 1872 that they had "fallen upon evil times" and were emigrating in great numbers to become day labourers in Berar.

Then came a final blow. The American Civil War (1861-65) cut off raw cotton supplies from the southern American plantations to Britain. Britain turned to India. Indian raw cotton prices shot up. Indian weavers, already pressed by Manchester imports, now had to pay exorbitant prices for their own raw material. Weaving stopped being viable in district after district.

This destruction of the world's most sophisticated textile economy by colonial trade policy is what historians call the **deindustrialisation of India**.

## The Indian Factories Strike Back

And yet, the story does not end in defeat.

In **1854**, the first Indian cotton mill opened in Bombay. By **1862** there were four working mills in the city, with 94,000 spindles and 2,150 looms. Around the same time **jute mills** sprang up in Bengal. In **1862** the first mill of **Kanpur** (the Elgin Mill) appeared in the north. A year later, the first cotton mill of **Ahmedabad**. By 1874 the first cotton mill of Madras.

Who built these mills? Where did the money come from?

The capital, almost entirely, came from **Indian merchants who had grown rich in the China trade**. From the late eighteenth century, the British had been exporting Indian opium to China and bringing Chinese tea to England. Many Indians became junior partners in this triangular trade — financing it, supplying it, shipping for it. They watched their British partners get richer and richer and slowly decided to do the same.

In Bengal, **Dwarkanath Tagore** (grandfather of the poet Rabindranath) made his fortune in the China trade and set up six joint-stock companies in the 1830s and 1840s. In Bombay, Parsi merchants like **Dinshaw Petit** and **Jamsetjee Nusserwanjee Tata** — yes, the founder of Tata — accumulated their initial wealth from China exports and raw cotton shipments to England, then rolled it into mills, steel works, and shipping. **Seth Hukumchand**, a Marwari businessman, set up the first Indian-owned jute mill in Calcutta in 1917. **G.D. Birla's** father and grandfather were also China traders.

But these new Indian industrialists were not given a free run. The colonial state quietly steered the economy to serve British interests. Indian merchants were **barred from trading with Europe in manufactured goods** — they could export only raw materials (raw cotton, opium, wheat, indigo). They were edged out of the shipping business. Until the First World War, **European Managing Agencies** — Bird Heiglers, Andrew Yule, Jardine Skinner — controlled the largest share of industries in India. Indian financiers put up the capital; European agents made the business decisions. Indian businessmen were not even allowed to join the European-only chambers of commerce.

Despite all this, the Indian mills grew. In **1901** there were 584,000 workers in Indian factories. By **1946** the number had risen to over **24 lakh**.

## The Workers in the Indian Mills

Where did those workers come from?

Most came from villages in the surrounding districts. Over half of the workers in Bombay's cotton mills in 1911 came from a single neighbouring district — **Ratnagiri** in the Konkan. Kanpur's mills drew their hands from the villages around Kanpur. Workers did not abandon their villages — they shuttled between mill and field, returning home for harvests, for sowing, for weddings, for the village festival.

A retired Bombay millworker recalled in his memoir: "The mill worker was closely associated with his village, physically and emotionally. He would go home to cut the harvest and for sowing. The Konkani would go home to cut the paddy and the Ghati, the sugarcane. It was an accepted practice for which the mills granted leave."

Yet even when mills multiplied, jobs were never enough. Entry was controlled by a powerful figure — the **jobber**. The jobber was usually an old, trusted worker who recruited fresh hands from his village, helped them find lodging, advanced them money. In return he demanded gifts, favours, sometimes even a cut of their wages. Vasant Parkar, a former Bombay millworker, remembered: "The workers would pay the jobbers money to get their sons work in the mill."

Conditions inside were brutal. Bhai Bhosle, a trade unionist of Bombay, recalled his father's life in the 1930s and 1940s: "In those days, the shift was 10 hours — from 5 pm to 3 am — terrible working hours. My father worked for 35 years; he got the asthma-like disease and could not work any more... Then my father went back to village."

Women worked mostly in the spinning departments — the lowest-paid sections. Children worked too — some as young as eight or nine, employed because their small fingers could reach into machine rollers without injury. (Or so it was claimed; in fact, child injuries were horrific.)

## Why Small Industries Survived

A surprising fact: even as factories grew, **small workshops and household production remained the larger part of the Indian economy**.

In 1911, only **5 per cent** of India's industrial workforce was in registered factories. By 1931 it had crept up to 10 per cent. The remaining 90 per cent worked in tiny workshops, weaver-sheds, household units — often in the bylanes invisible to passers-by.

In fact, in some cases handicrafts production actually **expanded** in the twentieth century. **Handloom cloth production almost trebled between 1900 and 1940.** How?

Three reasons. **First**, small technological improvements. By the 1920s, weavers had adopted the **fly shuttle** — a mechanical device worked with ropes and pulleys that doubled the speed of weaving. By 1941 over 35 per cent of all handlooms in India had fly shuttles; in regions like Travancore and Mysore the figure was 70 to 80 per cent.

**Second**, weavers stayed in the niches the mills could not reach. Mills could not imitate the **specialised weaves** — the gold-thread borders of Banarasi saris, the double-ikat patterns of Patola, the famous **lungis and handkerchiefs of Madras**, the Baluchari pallu. Coarse plain cloth went to the mills; fine craft cloth stayed with the weavers.

**Third**, the rich kept buying handlooms. Demand for finer cloth among the wealthy was stable even when demand for coarse cloth crashed in famine years. A Banarasi wedding sari sold in 1860 and in 1940 and still sells today.

Weavers did not prosper. They worked long hours; every member of the family — including children — was involved. Their lives were hard. But they survived, and their craft survived. They were not "remnants of a dying age." They were a living part of how Indian industry actually worked.

## Selling the Goods — The Birth of Advertising

When a new product is made, people must be persuaded to buy it. That persuasion is called **advertising** — and it was born together with industrialisation.

When Manchester industrialists first sold cloth in India, they began by stamping **labels on the cloth bundles**. The label said "**MADE IN MANCHESTER**" — a guarantee of quality, a way to make a foreign factory's product feel reliable. Labels also carried beautiful illustrations.

The British learned quickly that the surest way to sell foreign goods to Indian buyers was to make the goods feel **Indian**. So British cloth labels were printed with images of **Krishna, Lakshmi, Saraswati, Kartika** — Hindu gods and goddesses giving "divine approval" to a product made in Lancashire. Maharaja Ranjit Singh appeared on cloth labels. Mughal emperors decorated calendars. The message: this foreign cloth is worthy of your gods and kings.

By the late nineteenth century, manufacturers had discovered **calendars** as an even more powerful tool. Unlike newspapers, calendars did not need readers. They could hang in tea-shops, in farmers' huts, in middle-class drawing rooms, in offices — for an entire year, the image was seen every day. Calendars of Krishna selling Sunlight soap, of Lakshmi blessing Gripe Water, of Vishnu approving baby food — all became part of Indian visual culture.

Then Indian manufacturers took the same tool and **flipped it**. When the **swadeshi movement** began in 1905 — first as a protest against the partition of Bengal, then as a national call to boycott foreign cloth and use Indian — Indian mills printed labels with goddesses offering "Ahmedabad cloth" and asking people to "use things made in India." The same gods that had blessed Manchester now blessed Bombay. Advertising became a battlefield of nationalism.

The First World War accelerated all of this. With British mills busy producing for the war, Manchester imports into India collapsed. Indian mills suddenly had the home market to themselves and also had massive war contracts — jute bags, army cloth, tents, leather boots, saddles. New factories were set up. Old ones ran multiple shifts. Production boomed.

After the war, Manchester never recovered its old grip on the Indian market. By the 1930s, the swadeshi movement and protective tariffs were enough to push Indian industrialists into a dominant home position. The deindustrialisation of the nineteenth century was slowly, partially, being reversed.

## Why This Story Matters

Step back. What is the lesson of all this?

The industrial age is told to us as a story of triumph — chimneys, machines, progress. The honest story is far more complicated. Industrialisation in Britain was **slow**, fitful, and deeply unequal. Most workers were still craftspeople, not machine operators. Most production was still in small workshops, not big mills. The new wealth flowed unevenly — towards factory owners, then towards skilled workers, last of all and least of all towards the unskilled.

In India the story was sharper. Indian industry was not "absent" before the British arrived — it was the largest textile exporter in the world. Colonial policy did not just **fail to industrialise India**; it actively **deindustrialised** India by destroying Indian weavers and flooding the market with Manchester cloth. The Indian factories that finally emerged in the second half of the nineteenth century were built by Indian capital, fought for against active colonial resistance, and stayed concentrated in two cities — Bombay and Ahmedabad — for decades.

The story matters because it changes how we see the modern world. Britain did not become rich because of some natural genius for invention. It became rich, in part, by smashing other people's industries. India did not stay poor because of some natural backwardness. It was made poor, in part, by losing a textile industry it had built over a thousand years.

When you next see a Manchester chimney in a textbook, look beyond the chimney to the Banarasi weaver still pulling silk through her loom, the Surat banker who lost his trade, the Bombay mill girl spinning yarn for export to China. Their stories are the other half of the age of industries.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Story 1 — The Mill Chimneys of Mumbai

Walk through Parel or Lower Parel in Mumbai today and you will see, between glass office towers, a few **tall brick chimneys** rising up without any factory around them. These are the last bones of Bombay's cotton mills. Between the 1850s and the 1980s, this part of central Mumbai was one of the densest concentrations of textile mills in the world. The mills shut down in the 1980s and 1990s after a long strike and economic shifts. The land was sold. Malls and offices rose. The chimneys — built so solid they were not worth demolishing — remain. They are now the silent monuments of an industrial age that began with Dwarkanath Tagore and Jamsetjee Tata, fed a city of millions, and quietly ended within living memory. Look at them differently next time.

### Story 2 — The Weavers Who Never Stopped

In **Varanasi**, in the bylanes of Madanpura and Alaipura, you can still hear the soft clack-clack of the handloom at six in the morning. A weaver works fourteen hours to produce one and a half feet of a Banarasi sari. In **Kanchipuram**, generations of weavers still hand-knot the temple-border zari that no machine has ever copied. In **Sualkuchi** in Assam, in **Bhujodi** in Kachchh, in **Maheshwar** on the Narmada — handloom families have survived two centuries of competition from Manchester, from Bombay mills, and now from Bangladeshi power-looms. They are not relics. They are the unbroken thread that runs from the Bengal muslin of 1700 to the GI-tagged saris of today. Every handloom you see is a small refusal to be deindustrialised.

### Story 3 — The Krishna on the Soap Box

Walk into any small kirana shop in any small Indian town. Look closely at the packaging. **Hanuman** on an incense pack. **Lakshmi** on a packet of rice. **Krishna** on a sweet box. **A temple gopuram** on a textile label. The marketing trick that British manufacturers used in 1890 — using Hindu gods to make a foreign product feel familiar — has been thoroughly adopted by Indian manufacturers themselves. Look at the front of any agarbatti box and you are looking at a hundred and thirty years of unbroken commercial history. The packaging trick survived empire.

### Do It Yourself

Pick **one** of these three. Do it within a week.

**Option A — Visit a working mill or factory.** It does not need to be a big one — a rice mill, a flour mill, a small printing press, a roadside garment workshop. Note who works there, what the hours are, how the product moves from raw material to finished good. Compare with what we read about Bombay mills in 1911.

**Option B — Find a weaver shop.** Many cities still have small handloom emporiums — Khadi Gramodyog, state-run handloom houses (Cauvery, Lepakshi, Gangotri, Tantuja). Ask the shopkeeper where the cloth comes from, what village, what cluster. Take photos.

**Option C — Track ten household items.** Pick ten things in your kitchen, study room, or wardrobe. Read the back of each. Where was it **made** — China, India, Bangladesh, Vietnam? Where was the raw material from? Are any of the "Made in India" items actually from registered factories or from small workshops?

#### A Question to Keep Asking

Every time you buy a piece of cloth, a piece of furniture, a packet of biscuits — ask: **who made this, where, in what conditions, and what did they earn for it?**

That single question, asked often enough, is what historians of industrialisation have been asking for two hundred years. It is also what every conscious citizen of an industrial society should ask. The answer reveals more about your country than any newspaper headline.
