**Child:** Maa, why was Gandhi's salt march such a big deal? It was just salt.

**Mother:** *(laughs)* That is exactly what makes it brilliant, beta. Sit down. Let me tell you the way your great-grandfather told it to me.

## Why Did Gandhi Choose Salt?

**Mother:** Think about our kitchen. What do we put in *every* dish we cook?

**Child:** Salt.

**Mother:** Roti, dal, sabzi, even pickles — salt goes into everything. Rich people eat salt. Poor people eat salt. A king's banquet has salt. A labourer's roti and onion has salt. There is no Indian who can go through the day without salt.

Now, the British had put a **tax on salt**. They also had a **monopoly** — meaning only the government could legally make salt. A poor villager living on the coast, with seawater right there at his doorstep, was not allowed to boil seawater and make his own salt. He had to buy taxed salt from the government.

**Child:** That sounds so unfair.

**Mother:** It was. And Gandhi understood something very deep about politics: if you want a *whole country* to wake up together, you cannot choose a symbol that only touches one group. A textile worker cares about cotton. A farmer cares about land revenue. A lawyer cares about civil rights. But salt? Salt touches *everyone*.

When Gandhi walked 240 miles and picked up a fistful of salty earth from the beach at Dandi, every single Indian household watched and understood. Suddenly the British Raj was not a distant idea — it was sitting in the salt-jar in your kitchen.

**Child:** So the salt wasn't really about salt.

**Mother:** Exactly. The salt was about *us*. About showing crores of people, at the same time, that they could break an unjust law without raising a single weapon. And the British could not arrest a whole country at once.

## Why Did Some Leaders Disagree with Gandhi?

**Child:** Did everyone love Gandhi? Or were there leaders who thought he was wrong?

**Mother:** Many leaders disagreed with Gandhi, beta. Some respected him deeply but still disagreed. Disagreement is how a real movement grows.

Take **Subhas Chandra Bose**. He admired Gandhi but felt that non-violence alone could not push the British out. He believed India needed an army. Eventually he formed the **Indian National Army (Azad Hind Fauj)** and tried to fight the British with Japanese support in the Second World War.

Take **Bhagat Singh**, who was hanged at 23. He once said something striking — that the bomb and pistol were not the goal, the goal was revolution in *society*. He felt Gandhi's method was too slow, that British power would never be moved by moral appeal alone. He chose dramatic action — throwing a bomb in the Assembly, refusing to defend himself in court, walking to the gallows shouting **"Inquilab Zindabad!"**

Then there was **Dr B.R. Ambedkar**. He did not disagree about non-violence — he disagreed about caste. He believed Gandhi was too soft on the upper-caste Hindus who would never give up untouchability voluntarily. Dalits needed *political power* of their own, not just sympathy.

Even **Jawaharlal Nehru**, who loved Gandhi like a father, sometimes burst out in frustration — especially when Gandhi withdrew Non-Cooperation in 1922.

**Child:** So how did the movement hold together with so many disagreements?

**Mother:** That is the real question, beta. It held together because Gandhi was unusually skilled at *holding*. He listened. He bent where he could. He stood firm where he had to. He brought industrialists and peasants into the same Congress without letting either one drive the other away — most of the time. But sometimes the cracks broke open. And those cracks would matter, especially in 1947.

## What Did Swaraj Mean to a Peasant vs. a Mill Worker vs. Ambedkar?

**Child:** This part confused me. Everyone was shouting "swaraj, swaraj" — but the book says they all meant different things. How is that possible?

**Mother:** Imagine our family is going on a trip. Your dadi says "let's go on a trip" — she means a temple pilgrimage. You say "let's go on a trip" — you mean an adventure park. I say "let's go on a trip" — I mean a quiet hill station. *Same word. Different journeys.*

The word **swaraj** worked like that across India.

For a **Patidar peasant in Gujarat** during the Depression, with falling crop prices and a tax bill he could not pay — swaraj meant *lower revenue*. End the British land tax. That was the dream.

For a **mill worker in Bombay or Sholapur** earning a few rupees a week and living in a one-room chawl — swaraj meant *higher wages, shorter hours, the right to form a union*. It was a workers' freedom, not just a country's freedom.

For an **Awadh tenant** under Baba Ramchandra, beaten by the talukdar's men and forced to do **begar** without pay — swaraj meant *no more landlord oppression*. He didn't care so much who sat in Delhi; he wanted the man with the whip to stop coming to his hut.

For a **Gudem tribal in Andhra**, whose forests had been fenced off by colonial laws — swaraj meant *the forest given back*. Freedom to gather fruit, graze cattle, cut firewood.

For an **Assam tea-plantation worker** locked into a plantation by the Inland Emigration Act — swaraj meant the *right to leave, to go home to his village*.

For **Ambedkar** and the Dalits — swaraj had a sharper edge. He asked: "*Whose* freedom? If the British leave but I am still not allowed to drink water from the village well, what has changed for me?" For him, swaraj had to include *the end of untouchability and political power for Dalits*.

**Child:** So when crores of people marched together for swaraj, they were marching for crores of different futures?

**Mother:** Yes. That is the beauty of Indian nationalism — and also its difficulty. It was a coalition of dreams. As long as the British were the common enemy, the coalition held. After Independence, India had to figure out how to deliver on so many different versions of swaraj. We are still figuring it out.

## Was the Chauri Chaura Decision Right?

**Child:** I want to ask about something I really don't understand. Gandhi called off Non-Cooperation in 1922 after Chauri Chaura — when twenty-two policemen were burnt. Why? The movement was at its peak. Wasn't that the wrong moment to stop?

**Mother:** This question, beta, historians still argue about. Let me give you both sides honestly.

**Gandhi's view:** He believed deeply that *how* you win matters as much as *what* you win. If India became independent through violence, then violence would become the language of Indian politics forever. Independence would be hollow. Bullies would replace the British. He felt the people in many areas were not yet trained — that they would attack landlords and burn police stations the moment authority weakened. If Non-Cooperation continued, he feared Chauri Chaura would repeat itself in twenty places. So he stopped.

**The critics' view — including young Nehru and others:** They felt this was a betrayal of the moment. The British were rattled. The whole country was moving. Calling off the movement let the British recover, jail the leaders, and stretch their rule by another quarter-century. To them, twenty-two policemen at Chauri Chaura, painful as that was, was not a reason to stop a movement that could have shortened British rule by twenty years and saved many more lives.

**Child:** What do *you* think, Maa?

**Mother:** Honestly? I am not sure even today. I see Gandhi's wisdom — he was building a *culture* of non-violence, not just winning one campaign. He knew a country freed by violence would torture itself afterwards, the way some countries did. And he was right that disciplined non-violence is rare and precious; it must be protected like a flame.

But I also see the critics' anger. He stopped a movement at its peak. That is a thing leaders rarely recover from politically. And the people at Chauri Chaura were retaliating against police firing — were they really wrong to fight back?

What I would say to you is this: at Chauri Chaura, Gandhi was choosing the *long road* over the *short road*. The short road might have got independence faster. The long road tried to shape what *kind* of independent India we would become. Whether the price was worth it depends on what you believe an independent India should look like.

**Child:** That's not an easy answer.

**Mother:** Real history rarely is, beta.

## How Did Women and Dalits Shape the Movement?

**Child:** When the book talks about Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience, mostly it's male leaders. Where were the women? Where were the Dalits?

**Mother:** This is one of the most important questions you can ask. Let me tell you what your great-grandmother once told me about her aunt — who walked out of her home in 1930 to picket a foreign-cloth shop in Surat.

**The women of 1930:** When Gandhi started the Salt March, something happened that no one fully predicted. Women came out — *thousands* of them. From rich peasant homes in villages. From high-caste families in cities. They had spent their whole lives in courtyards. Now they marched in processions. They made salt by boiling seawater. They sat down outside cloth shops and refused to move. They went to jail.

Your great-grandmother's aunt was one of them. When she walked back home after her first day of picketing, her father-in-law refused to let her in. Her husband stood up to him and said — *she is doing what the nation is asking her to do*. Many homes were turned upside down by this kind of small revolution.

**Child:** So women's lives changed?

**Mother:** Their *public lives* changed. They were now on the streets, in jail, in the newspapers. That was new and powerful.

But Gandhi himself remained quite traditional. He praised women's role in the movement but he also wrote — many times — that women's true work was to be **good mothers and good wives**. The Congress was reluctant to give women real positions of authority. They wanted women's symbolic presence, not full leadership. So the public face of nationalism opened to women, but the social order at home did not change as much. Women like **Sarojini Naidu** and **Aruna Asaf Ali** broke through — but they had to fight on two fronts: against the British, and against their own movement's blind spots.

**The Dalit question:** Now this is harder, beta. Gandhi cared about untouchability — genuinely. He cleaned toilets to honour the work of sweepers. He led satyagrahas to open temples. He insisted that swaraj was meaningless if untouchability survived.

But Dr **Ambedkar** said something Gandhi could not hear easily: *moral appeals to upper-caste hearts are not enough*. The Dalit needs *power*. Education seats. Government jobs. Reserved political representation. Real institutional protection, not just sympathy.

When Ambedkar demanded **separate electorates** for Dalits at the Second Round Table Conference, Gandhi went on a **fast unto death**. The whole country was paralysed. Eventually Ambedkar agreed to the **Poona Pact** of 1932 — reserved seats, but elected by a joint electorate. He agreed because if Gandhi had died, Dalits would have been blamed and lynched.

**Child:** That sounds like Ambedkar was forced.

**Mother:** It was a kind of moral coercion, yes. Many Dalit thinkers still feel the Poona Pact was a setback. They feel separate electorates would have given Dalits *independent* political voice — not Dalit MLAs who depend on upper-caste votes to win.

So the movement opened doors to Dalits — but not on Dalit terms. The question of whose voice gets heard in our democracy is something Ambedkar would later answer through the Constitution itself. He understood: laws can do what the heart will not.

## What Should I Take Away From This?

**Child:** Maa, this whole chapter feels so big. What is the one thing I should remember?

**Mother:** Let me sit close. The one thing, beta, is this:

**Indian nationalism was a coalition of many freedoms held inside one word.**

When you say "freedom" today — say in a Republic Day speech — it sounds simple. *One word. One country. One victory.* But it was never that.

Freedom for the peasant meant ending the landlord's whip. Freedom for the mill worker meant a fair wage. Freedom for the woman meant walking out of her courtyard. Freedom for the Dalit meant being treated as fully human. Freedom for the Muslim was, increasingly, the right to a political identity that would not be drowned by a Hindu majority. Freedom for the businessman meant ending British control over trade. Freedom for Bhagat Singh meant a revolution in society, not just a flag-change.

The miracle of the freedom movement was that all these freedoms walked together, more or less, for two and a half decades. The tragedy was that some of them got dropped on the way — and on 15 August 1947, when the new flag went up, Partition was also happening, and millions were dying.

So when you think about the freedom struggle, do not think of it as one thing. Think of it as **a question**. *Whose freedom won? Whose freedom is still waiting?* That is the question every Indian generation has to ask all over again — including yours.

And one more thing.

**Child:** Yes, Maa?

**Mother:** The freedom struggle was not made by leaders alone. It was made by lakhs of ordinary people whose names we will never know. The girl who refused to go to a government school in 1921. The peasant in Bardoli who hid his cattle from the revenue officer. The Khan Saheb's grandmother who fed the Pathan satyagrahis in Peshawar. The Bombay woman who burnt her silk sari and started wearing khadi. The Bengali boatman who ferried Subhas Babu in disguise. They are not in your textbook. But they are in your blood. Remember them.

**Child:** *(quietly)* I will, Maa.

**Mother:** Good. Now go finish your homework. The freedom they won is yours to keep — and to extend.
