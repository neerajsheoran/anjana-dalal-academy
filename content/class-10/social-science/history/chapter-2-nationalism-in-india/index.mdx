## A Story Told From Many Villages

When Indians today say "we won our freedom," it sounds like one single sentence — one nation, one struggle, one victory. But that simple sentence hides something much more interesting. The freedom struggle was not one movement. It was hundreds of movements, in hundreds of villages and towns, joined together by leaders who somehow held them all in a single thread.

A **mill worker** in Ahmedabad wanted higher wages. A **peasant** in Awadh wanted his rent reduced. A **tribal hunter** in the Andhra hills wanted his forest back. A **lawyer** in Calcutta wanted a Constitution. A **woman** in a Bombay household wanted to walk out of her courtyard and into a public square. A **Dalit** organiser in Maharashtra wanted the temple gates opened. A **revolutionary** in Lahore wanted to throw a bomb in the Assembly. Each of them used the word **swaraj** — self-rule — but each of them meant something different by it.

The genius of Indian nationalism between 1919 and 1947 was that it was big enough to hold all these meanings together. The cracks were real. Sometimes the cracks broke open. But for the first time, a sense of being **Indian** — of belonging to one large country with one shared destiny — became powerful enough to bring crores of ordinary people out of their homes and onto the streets. This chapter is the story of how that happened.

## The World War That Lit the Fuse

Modern Indian nationalism did not begin in 1919. It had been growing through the late nineteenth century, with the founding of the Indian National Congress in 1885, the partition of Bengal in 1905, and the Swadeshi movement. But the **First World War (1914–1918)** changed its temperature completely.

War is always expensive. Britain financed its war partly out of Indian pockets. Defence spending shot up. **Customs duties** were raised. A new **income tax** was introduced. To pay for it all, prices doubled between 1913 and 1918 — and when prices double, the poor suffer most. A farmer who used to buy a *seer* of oil could now afford only half. A weaver's daily earnings bought less rice than before.

Then came **forced recruitment**. The British army needed soldiers. Villages, especially in Punjab, were told how many young men they had to "give" to the army. Many never came home. Those who did brought back wounds and stories of trenches in France.

In 1918–19 and again in 1920–21, the rains failed. Crops withered. The 1918 **influenza epidemic** swept across the country. According to the 1921 census, around **12 to 13 million Indians** died from famine and disease in those few years. Imagine — that was more than the entire population of many countries.

People had hoped that the war would end and life would get easier. It did not. Anger was everywhere. All the country needed was someone who could turn that anger into a movement.

## Gandhi Arrives — And Brings a New Weapon

**Mahatma Gandhi** returned to India from South Africa in January 1915. He had spent two decades there fighting racist laws, and he had developed a method of struggle he called **satyagraha** — literally "holding on to truth."

Satyagraha was not passive. Gandhi was firm about that. It was a form of **active resistance**, but without violence. The idea ran like this: if your cause is true, if you are fighting injustice, then you do not need to pick up a weapon. You need to refuse to cooperate with the wrong. You need to be willing to suffer the consequences yourself. By doing so, you appeal to the conscience of the wrongdoer. Truth, Gandhi believed, eventually wins.

This was a strange and radical idea in a world that respected armies and guns. But Gandhi tested it quickly on Indian soil.

- In **1917**, he went to **Champaran** in Bihar, where indigo planters were squeezing peasants under an oppressive contract system. He stood with the peasants and forced an inquiry.
- In **1918**, he organised the **cotton mill workers of Ahmedabad** in a strike for higher wages — going on fast himself when the strike weakened.
- Later in **1918**, he supported the **Kheda peasants** of Gujarat, who could not pay revenue after crop failure and plague.

Each of these was small. But each showed something new: that ordinary Indians — peasants, weavers, mill workers — could be organised, could be disciplined, and could face down the colonial state without violence.

## Rowlatt, Jallianwala Bagh, and a Country in Shock

In 1919, the British passed the **Rowlatt Act** — a law that let the government detain any Indian without trial for up to two years on mere suspicion. The Indian members of the legislative council had voted unanimously against it. They were ignored.

Gandhi called for a nationwide **hartal** (strike) on 6 April 1919. Cities shut down. Workers walked out. Shops closed. The colonial state was alarmed. In Amritsar, local leaders were arrested. On 10 April, police fired on a peaceful procession. Crowds attacked banks and railway stations. **Martial law** was imposed. **General Dyer** took charge.

On **13 April 1919** — Baisakhi day — thousands of Punjabis gathered at **Jallianwala Bagh**, an enclosed garden with only one narrow exit. Many were villagers there for the fair, knowing nothing about martial law. Some had come to protest the new laws. Dyer marched in with his troops, blocked the exit, and ordered his men to open fire. They fired for nearly ten minutes. Hundreds were killed. Many jumped into a well to escape and died there. Dyer later said his purpose was to "produce a moral effect" — to terrorise.

What followed was even more humiliating. Indians in Punjab were forced to crawl on their stomachs through certain streets where a British woman had once been attacked. They were made to **salaam** every British officer they passed. Villages near Gujranwala were bombed from the air.

When the news spread, an entire generation turned against British rule. The poet **Rabindranath Tagore** returned his knighthood. Young men who had been loyal to the empire suddenly saw it for what it was. Gandhi himself called off the satyagraha when he saw violence spreading. But the wound was permanent. India was no longer the same country it had been on 12 April 1919.

## The Khilafat Bridge

Gandhi understood something crucial: no mass movement in India could succeed unless **Hindus and Muslims stood together**. After the war, Muslims in India were upset about a different issue — the harsh treatment of the **Ottoman Sultan**, who was the **Khalifa**, the spiritual head of the Islamic world. A **Khilafat Committee** had been formed in Bombay in March 1919. The young Ali brothers — **Muhammad Ali** and **Shaukat Ali** — were leading it.

Gandhi saw a chance. If Hindu and Muslim politics could be brought under one umbrella, the freedom movement would become genuinely national. At the **Calcutta session** of the Congress in September 1920, he convinced his colleagues. The Congress would support the Khilafat cause. In return, the Khilafat leaders would support a new movement — **Non-Cooperation** — for swaraj.

It was a bold political bargain. For a moment, India looked like one body.

## Non-Cooperation (1920–1922): Refuse to Help Them Rule

In his 1909 book **Hind Swaraj**, Gandhi had made a striking claim: British rule survived in India only because Indians cooperated with it. Indians worked in British offices, studied in British schools, argued in British courts, bought British cloth. *Withdraw all this cooperation* — Gandhi argued — *and the British Raj would collapse within a year*.

The **Non-Cooperation Movement** unfolded in stages. Indians were asked to:

- **Surrender titles** the British government had given them
- **Boycott** government schools, colleges, law courts, legislative councils, and elections
- **Boycott foreign cloth** and use only **khadi** (hand-spun Indian cloth)
- **Resign from government jobs** in the army, police, and civil service

It was adopted at the **Nagpur Congress** in December 1920 and launched in January 1921. The country lit up.

**In the cities:** Thousands of students walked out of government schools. Lawyers like **Motilal Nehru** and **C.R. Das** gave up their lucrative practices. Foreign cloth was burnt in huge bonfires — the import of foreign cloth fell from Rs 102 crore to Rs 57 crore in just one year. Liquor shops were picketed. Indian textile mills and handlooms started producing more.

But the city movement slowly weakened. Khadi was expensive — a poor man could not afford it. Alternative Indian schools and courts were slow to come up. Some students drifted back to government schools. Lawyers returned to courts.

**In the countryside:** This is where things became wild and complicated.

In **Awadh** (Uttar Pradesh), peasants were already organising under **Baba Ramchandra**, a sanyasi who had once been an indentured labourer in Fiji. They were furious with the **talukdars** — landlords who charged crippling rents and forced peasants to do **begar** (unpaid labour). The peasants demanded rent reduction, an end to begar, and a social boycott of oppressive landlords. Village panchayats organised **nai-dhobi bandhs** — refusing barbers and washermen to landlords. In June 1920, a young **Jawaharlal Nehru** toured the Awadh villages, listening to grievances. By October, the **Oudh Kisan Sabha** had been founded — 300 branches in just a month.

But the Awadh peasant movement quickly went past the Congress programme. Houses of landlords were attacked. Bazaars were looted. Grain hoards were seized. In many villages, peasants were told that **Gandhiji himself had said no taxes were to be paid and that land would be redistributed**. Gandhi's name was being invoked for things Gandhi had not said.

In the **Gudem Hills of Andhra Pradesh**, tribal people had been deprived of their forests by colonial laws. They could not graze cattle, gather fuelwood, or collect fruit in their own forests. When the government also forced them into begar for road building, they revolted. Their leader was **Alluri Sitaram Raju** — a charismatic figure who claimed astrological powers, said he could survive bullets, and was revered as an incarnation of God. Raju talked admiringly of Gandhi, told his people to wear khadi and give up drinking — but he also believed India could only be freed by force. The Gudem rebels attacked police stations and ambushed British officials. Raju was captured and executed in 1924. He became a folk hero.

In **Assam's tea plantations**, workers heard about Non-Cooperation and believed that **Gandhi Raj** was coming and everyone would get land in their home villages. They walked off the plantations in thousands. They never reached home. A railway and steamer strike stranded them, and the police beat them brutally.

Each group — peasants, tribals, plantation workers — used the language of swaraj. But each meant something different. For the Awadh peasant, swaraj meant **no begar, no landlord oppression**. For the Gudem tribal, it meant **the forest returned**. For the plantation worker, it meant **the right to leave**. The Congress was struggling to hold all these meanings together inside one movement.

## Chauri Chaura: A Painful Brake

On **5 February 1922**, in a small place called **Chauri Chaura** in Gorakhpur (Uttar Pradesh), a Congress procession was confronted by a few policemen. There was a clash. The crowd, enraged, set fire to the police station. **Twenty-two policemen died** in the flames.

When the news reached Gandhi, he was devastated. He believed the people were not yet trained for disciplined non-violence. If he continued the movement, he feared more such incidents — and a violent freedom struggle, he insisted, would just replace one tyranny with another. On **12 February 1922**, against the angry protests of many Congress leaders, he **withdrew the Non-Cooperation Movement**.

Many younger nationalists — Jawaharlal Nehru among them — found the decision crushing. How could you call off a national movement at its peak? But Gandhi held firm. For him, the *means* mattered as much as the *end*. **Was it right to call off Chauri Chaura?** It is one of the great debates in Indian history — and we will return to it.

## The Long In-Between (1922–1929)

After 1922, the Congress fractured. **C.R. Das** and **Motilal Nehru** formed the **Swaraj Party** within the Congress, arguing that Indians should fight from inside the legislative councils rather than from outside. Younger leaders like **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Subhas Chandra Bose** wanted more radical mass action and full independence.

Two factors then pushed politics forward again in the late 1920s:

**The Great Depression (1929).** Worldwide agricultural prices collapsed from 1930. Indian peasants who grew commercial crops — cotton, jute, sugarcane — could not sell their harvests. They could not pay revenue. The countryside burned with anger.

**The Simon Commission (1928).** Britain set up a commission under **Sir John Simon** to recommend constitutional reforms — but did not appoint a single Indian member. The Congress, the Muslim League, and almost every other political group joined the boycott. **"Go back, Simon!"** rang in every city. **Lala Lajpat Rai** was beaten by police during the Lahore protest and died of his injuries.

To win Indians over, Viceroy Lord Irwin offered vague talk of **dominion status** and a **Round Table Conference**. Congress radicals were not satisfied. At the **Lahore session** in December 1929, under Jawaharlal Nehru's presidency, the Congress declared **Purna Swaraj** — complete independence — as its goal. **26 January 1930** was fixed as **Independence Day**.

But Independence Day passed quietly. The slogan was abstract. Gandhi knew the country needed something concrete — something every household could feel.

## The Salt March (1930): Genius in a Pinch of Salt

On **31 January 1930**, Gandhi sent Viceroy Irwin an extraordinary letter with **eleven demands**. Some were political. Some were for industrialists. Some were for peasants. The most surprising demand of all? **Abolish the salt tax.**

To outsiders, the salt tax sounded trivial. But Gandhi had seen what others had missed. Salt is consumed by every Indian — rich and poor, Hindu and Muslim, peasant and prince. The British monopoly on salt and the tax on it pressed on every kitchen in the country. By targeting salt, Gandhi found a symbol that bound the rich industrialist and the landless labourer into one cause.

If the demands were not met by 11 March, Gandhi warned, he would break the salt law.

Irwin did not respond. On **12 March 1930**, Gandhi set out from his **Sabarmati Ashram** with **78 volunteers**, walking towards the Gujarati coastal town of **Dandi**. The march covered **240 miles** in **24 days**. Crowds gathered in every village along the way. Gandhi spoke about swaraj. People joined the procession. On **6 April 1930**, Gandhi reached Dandi, picked up a fistful of salty earth, and boiled it into salt. The law was broken.

It was a small gesture. The salt he made was not worth much. But the symbolism was thunderous. Across the country, Indians broke the salt law — manufacturing salt on beaches, demonstrating outside government salt depots. Foreign cloth was boycotted again. Liquor shops were picketed. Peasants refused to pay **chaukidari** taxes. In the forests, people walked back in to graze cattle and gather wood — defying colonial forest laws.

This was the start of the **Civil Disobedience Movement**. Unlike Non-Cooperation, which asked Indians to merely *refuse* to cooperate, Civil Disobedience asked Indians to *actively break* unjust laws.

The British struck back. When **Abdul Ghaffar Khan** — Gandhi's loyal Pashtun follower, the "Frontier Gandhi" — was arrested in April 1930, crowds in Peshawar faced armoured cars and gunfire. When Gandhi himself was arrested a month later, industrial workers in **Sholapur** attacked every building that symbolised British rule — police posts, courts, railway stations. Around **100,000 Indians** were arrested. Peaceful satyagrahis were beaten. Women and children were assaulted.

In March 1931, Gandhi signed the **Gandhi-Irwin Pact**, agreeing to attend the **Second Round Table Conference** in London. He came back disappointed — the British were not willing to grant real power. The movement was relaunched, but by 1934 it had lost its steam.

## Who Joined? Different Groups, Different Dreams

The Civil Disobedience Movement, like Non-Cooperation before it, was a mosaic of different groups with different hopes.

**Rich peasants** like the **Patidars of Gujarat** and the **Jats of UP** were producers of commercial crops. The Depression had crushed their incomes. They could not pay revenue. They became enthusiastic supporters of the movement — because for them, swaraj meant **lower revenue**. When the movement was called off in 1931 without revenue reduction, they were bitterly disappointed and refused to rejoin in 1932.

**Poor peasants** were tenants who could not pay rent to their landlords. They wanted unpaid rents to be forgiven. But the Congress, careful not to upset the rich peasants and landlords who supported it, refused to take up "no-rent" demands. So poor peasants drifted toward **socialist** and **communist** movements instead.

**Business classes** had grown rich during the First World War. They wanted **protection** against foreign goods and a friendlier rupee-sterling exchange rate. They formed organisations like the **Indian Industrial and Commercial Congress (1920)** and **FICCI (1927)**. Leaders like **Purshottamdas Thakurdas** and **G.D. Birla** supported Civil Disobedience financially. For them, swaraj meant **a free hand to do business**. But after 1931, with militant activity rising and socialism spreading in the Congress, they grew cautious.

**Industrial workers** mostly did not join in big numbers — except in Nagpur. The Congress, eager to keep industrialists on board, would not endorse workers' demands. Still, railway workers struck in 1930. Dock workers struck in 1932. In Chotanagpur's tin mines, thousands of workers wore Gandhi caps and joined boycotts.

**Women** participated in this movement on a scale never seen before. During the salt march, thousands of women had come out to listen to Gandhi. Now they led processions, manufactured salt, picketed cloth shops and liquor stores. They went to jail in numbers. In cities, they came from high-caste families. In villages, from rich peasant households. Yet — and this is important — Gandhi himself remained traditional about women's roles. He believed women's duty was to be **good mothers and good wives**. The Congress was reluctant to give women real positions of authority — only symbolic presence. The freedom struggle changed women's public lives, but it stopped short of changing women's status.

## Ambedkar, the Dalits, and the Poona Pact

For India's **Dalits** — the so-called "untouchables" — swaraj raised a sharper question. If British rule ended, would they still face untouchability under their own people? Many Dalit leaders believed the answer was yes.

Gandhi argued passionately against untouchability. He called Dalits **harijans** ("children of God"). He led satyagrahas to open temples, wells, and roads to them. He himself cleaned toilets to honour the work of the **bhangis** (sweepers). He told upper-caste Hindus: "Swaraj will not come for a hundred years if untouchability is not eliminated."

But **Dr B.R. Ambedkar** — born into a Dalit family, a brilliant scholar who had studied at Columbia and London — believed that moral appeal was not enough. Dalits needed **political power** of their own. In 1930, he founded the **Depressed Classes Association**. At the **Second Round Table Conference** in 1931, he demanded **separate electorates** for Dalits — meaning Dalit voters would elect Dalit representatives separately from caste Hindus.

Gandhi was alarmed. He believed separate electorates would permanently divide Dalits from the rest of Hindu society. He began a **fast unto death** in Yerawada prison in September 1932. The pressure on Ambedkar was enormous — if Gandhi died, Dalits would be blamed.

Ambedkar finally agreed to a compromise: the **Poona Pact (September 1932)**. Dalits would get **reserved seats** in provincial and central legislatures, but they would be elected by the general electorate, not a separate Dalit one. The Dalit community accepted the pact, but Ambedkar always remained sceptical of the Congress-led national movement. The unease between Ambedkar and Gandhi never fully went away — and Dr Ambedkar would later play a defining role as the architect of India's Constitution.

## The Muslim League, the Communal Question, and Iqbal

After the Khilafat movement faded, many Muslims drifted away from the Congress. From the mid-1920s, the Congress was seen as increasingly close to openly Hindu groups like the **Hindu Mahasabha**. Religious processions became more militant. Riots broke out in city after city. Every riot deepened mistrust.

In **1927**, the Congress and the **Muslim League** nearly reached a deal. **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** offered to drop the demand for separate electorates if Muslims got reserved seats in the Central Assembly and proportional representation in Muslim-majority provinces (Bengal and Punjab). But at the **All Parties Conference of 1928**, the Hindu Mahasabha leader **M.R. Jayakar** torpedoed the compromise.

By 1930, **Sir Muhammad Iqbal** — the poet and philosopher who is celebrated for *Saare Jahaan Se Achchha* — was president of the Muslim League. He argued that Muslims needed separate electorates as a permanent safeguard, and that Indian Muslims had a right to develop along the lines of their own culture in their own homelands. Some historians see this as one of the intellectual seeds of the later demand for **Pakistan**. The fracture that would erupt in 1947 was already visible.

## Bhagat Singh and the Revolutionary Stream

Not every Indian believed Gandhi's path of non-violence was the only way. A parallel current — the **revolutionary stream** — believed the British could only be driven out by force.

In **1928**, the **Hindustan Socialist Republican Army (HSRA)** was founded in Delhi. Its leaders included **Bhagat Singh**, **Jatin Das**, and **Ajoy Ghosh**. In **April 1929**, Bhagat Singh and **Batukeswar Dutta** threw a bomb in the Central Legislative Assembly — not to kill but to "make the deaf hear." They allowed themselves to be arrested. Bhagat Singh was tried, refused to defend himself, and was executed in **1931 at the age of 23**.

At his trial Bhagat Singh said: "I do not wish to glorify the cult of the bomb and pistol. I want a revolution in society… To the altar of this revolution we have brought our youth as incense, for no sacrifice is too great for so magnificent a cause." His final cry — **"Inquilab Zindabad!"** — became the rallying call of an entire generation. Even those who disagreed with violence respected Bhagat Singh's sacrifice.

## How Indians Saw Themselves As Indians

A freedom struggle is not just about laws and protests. It is also about a country **imagining itself**. How did people in a Punjabi village come to feel they belonged to the same nation as a Tamil fisherman or a Bengali poet?

**Bharat Mata.** In the 1870s, the Bengali writer **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay** wrote a hymn called **"Vande Mataram"** as part of his novel *Anandamath*. The hymn portrayed India as a mother — a divine figure to be worshipped. The image of **Bharat Mata** entered popular imagination. The artist **Abanindranath Tagore** painted her in 1905 as a calm, ascetic figure giving learning, food, and clothing to her children. Later artists painted her with a **trishul** beside a lion and elephant — fiercer, more militant. Each Bharat Mata image carried a different idea of India.

**Folklore.** Nationalists travelled to villages to collect folk tales, songs, and ballads. **Rabindranath Tagore** collected Bengali folk songs. In Madras, **Natesa Sastri** compiled a four-volume *Folklore of Southern India*. They believed folk traditions held the "real" Indian voice — uncorrupted by British rule.

**Flags.** During the Swadeshi movement in Bengal, a tricolour flag (red, green, yellow) was designed with eight lotuses for British India's eight provinces and a crescent moon for Hindu-Muslim unity. By 1921, Gandhi had designed a new **Swaraj flag** — red, green, and white, with a **spinning wheel** in the centre representing self-reliance. To unfurl this flag in defiance of British orders was a powerful act.

**Reinterpreting history.** Indian historians began writing about the golden age of ancient India — its mathematics, art, architecture, philosophy — to counter British claims that Indians were backward and incapable of self-rule. But there was a problem: when the past being glorified was Hindu, when the icons were drawn from Hindu mythology, Muslims and other communities felt **excluded**. This tension — between the desire to unify and the way unifying symbols sometimes divided — was already visible in the nationalist movement.

## Quit India (1942): The Final Push

When the **Second World War** broke out, India was again pulled into a British war without consent. The **Cripps Mission** of 1942 failed to offer Indians any real share of power. On **8 August 1942**, the Congress passed the **Quit India** resolution in Bombay. Gandhi delivered his famous **"Do or Die"** speech.

By morning, almost the entire Congress leadership was in jail. With the top leaders gone, the movement was led from below — by students, workers, peasants, and a new generation of leaders: **Jayaprakash Narayan, Aruna Asaf Ali, Ram Manohar Lohia**. Women like **Matangini Hazra** in Bengal, **Kanaklata Barua** in Assam, and **Rama Devi** in Odisha led local actions and were killed by British bullets.

For months, large parts of India were ungovernable. Telegraph lines were cut. Railway stations were attacked. Parallel governments were set up in places like Satara and Tamluk. The British responded with massive force — but it took them over a year to fully suppress the movement. The Quit India Movement was the last great mass uprising before independence. It proved to the British what they had perhaps refused to believe: their time in India was finally over.

## What Was Emerging

By 1945, India was a nation. Not a uniform one — never that — but a country whose many voices had learned to act together against a common opponent. The peasant in Awadh, the Dalit organiser in Maharashtra, the woman who walked out of her courtyard to picket a cloth shop, the schoolboy who burnt his English textbook, the mill worker who joined a strike — all of them had, in their own way, become part of a single story.

Independence would come on **15 August 1947**, but it would come along with the tragedy of Partition. The unity that Indian nationalism had built was real but also fragile. Holding it together — making sense of the many meanings of swaraj — would be the work of every generation that followed, including yours.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Story 1: Tilak's Ganesh Utsav — Religion as Political Tool

In the 1890s, **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** transformed Ganesh Chaturthi from a private home festival into a huge **public Ganesh Utsav** in Pune. Why? The British had banned political meetings. But they could not ban religious processions. By moving the festival into public streets, Tilak created a space where thousands could gather, listen to nationalist speeches, sing songs of swaraj — under the cover of devotion to Ganpati. Across India today, the giant pandals during Ganesh Utsav still carry that double inheritance: religious festival and public assembly.

### Story 2: Khadi Still Carries a Charge

Walk into the Parliament of India today. Look at the politicians on television in election season. White khadi is everywhere — the *kurta*, the *Gandhi topi*, the *bandi*. Khadi was never just cloth. In 1921, it was a declaration that you did not need British mills. In 2026, it is still a political signal — of being close to the people, of respecting Gandhi's legacy, of claiming a piece of India's freedom struggle. The shirt you wear can be a political argument.

### Story 3: The Salt March Route Today

In Gujarat, the 240-mile path from Sabarmati Ashram to Dandi is now a **heritage trail**. The route is marked with monuments. Schoolchildren walk parts of it during anniversaries. The original Sabarmati Ashram still stands on the river — quiet, full of khadi looms, attended by visitors from around the world. Some scholars walk the entire route as a kind of pilgrimage. The salt march has become a piece of moving geography — a path that crosses villages, asking each one to remember what was carried along it in 1930.

**Option B — Visit a freedom monument.** Walk through a freedom-fighter memorial in your city — a martyrs' park, a statue, a museum. Find out who is honoured there. Try to find a name no one in your school has heard of, and learn their story.
