## A World Without Printed Books

Try to imagine a day in which nothing you read was printed. No textbook in your school bag. No newspaper at the breakfast table. No street signboard. No movie poster. No notebook with a publisher's logo. No exam question paper. No comic. No WhatsApp forward — because there is no phone, but also because there is no font, no template, no shared script that millions can read at once.

For most of human history, this was reality. A book was a treasure. To own one was to be wealthy. To make one took months of a skilled scribe's life. Knowledge moved slowly, copied letter by letter by tired hands, and when it moved it was usually controlled — by temples, monasteries, royal courts, scholars who guarded what they knew.

Then, gradually, printing arrived. And the world we live in — full of newspapers, posters, novels, pamphlets, exam guides, election manifestos, religious tracts, comic books, chemistry textbooks — became possible.

This chapter is the story of that transformation. It is also the story of how the printed word changed India.

## The First Print: China

The story of print begins in **China**, not Europe. By around **594 CE**, Chinese craftsmen were rubbing inked **wooden blocks** against sheets of paper to produce text. The process was simple in idea but exquisite in skill: carve a mirror-image of the characters onto a smooth block of wood, ink the raised surface, press paper against it, peel off the printed sheet. The Chinese language, with its thousands of distinct characters, fit this method well — each block could carry an entire page of text.

For centuries the imperial state was the chief printer. China's vast **bureaucracy** required hundreds of thousands of educated officials, and these officials were chosen through gruelling **civil service examinations**. The examinations needed textbooks; the textbooks were printed in huge numbers. The imperial press at Beijing churned out so many copies of Confucian classics that they became cheap and common across the empire.

By the **seventeenth century**, as China's cities grew rich, print spilled out beyond officialdom. Merchants, traders, scholars, women in elite homes — all began to read. There were fictional romances, plays, poetry collections, biographies of famous women, advice books on etiquette, books on medicine and herbs, and a flood of cheap entertainment. Reading became a leisurely social activity. A scholar's study with its scrolls and printed volumes was a status symbol.

Western missionaries and traders arrived in China during the sixteenth century and were astonished. Europe at that point had no such culture of reading.

## Print Travels to Japan and Korea

Buddhist missionaries from China carried hand-printing to **Korea** and **Japan** around **768–770 CE**. The oldest surviving printed book in the world is the Buddhist **Diamond Sutra**, printed in **868 CE** — six wooden blocks for the text and a beautifully detailed cover illustration, all on a long paper scroll.

In Japan, printing spread alongside Buddhism. Temples printed religious texts; later, in cities like **Edo** (today's Tokyo), illustrated collections — paintings of urban life, courtesans, kabuki actors, landscapes, comic stories — became immensely popular. The Japanese artist **Kitagawa Utamaro** (1753–1806) and his peers produced the woodblock prints known as **ukiyo-e**, "pictures of the floating world," which would later astonish European painters like Van Gogh.

For more than seven hundred years, then, East Asia had an active, sophisticated, popular print culture — long before Europe even imagined one.

## Print Reaches Europe

Europeans first met paper, the cheap surface that makes books affordable, through trade routes from China via the Arab world. Paper made the laborious **manuscript** culture — texts copied by hand on expensive parchment or vellum — slightly more affordable. But books were still painfully slow to make.

In the early fifteenth century, European craftsmen began experimenting with **woodblock printing** in the Chinese style — first for textiles, playing cards, religious pictures with short captions. Booksellers in cities like **Venice, Paris, and Bruges** employed scribes to copy books for the wealthy. By the 1430s, demand for books was outstripping the supply that hand-copying could meet. A new technology was needed.

It came from a German goldsmith named **Johann Gutenberg**.

## Gutenberg and the Printing Press

**Johann Gutenberg** (c. 1400–1468), born in Mainz, was the son of a merchant who had grown up around vineyards, metalworking, and goldsmithing. Gutenberg combined ideas already floating around Europe — the wine-press for squeezing grapes, the goldsmith's skill in making fine metal moulds, the oil-based ink used by printers — into one revolutionary machine.

His central innovation was **movable metal type**: each letter cast as a separate piece of hard metal, which could be assembled into words, lines, and pages, then disassembled and reused. Gutenberg perfected the casting of these tiny letters around **1448**.

His first major book was the **Bible**, printed around **1454–55**. About **180 copies** were produced — small by today's standards, gigantic by the standards of the manuscript age, which would have needed years of scribes' labour for the same output. Gutenberg printed in black-letter Gothic type and left blank spaces where rubricators (specialists) added decorations in colour by hand, to make the book resemble luxurious manuscripts.

Within fifty years, **printing presses had spread across Europe**. Printers set up shop in Italy (Venice especially), France, the Netherlands, England, Spain, Bohemia. Historians estimate that by **1500**, around twenty million printed volumes were circulating in Europe. By **1600**, the figure was about **200 million**.

A revolution was underway, and not just in book-making.

## The Print Revolution: A New Reading Public

What did print really change?

First, the **cost of books collapsed**. A printed book in 1500 cost a fraction of a hand-copied manuscript. Affordable books meant more readers; more readers meant a market for more books. A **virtuous cycle** had begun.

Second, a new **reading public** emerged. Earlier, books had been the property of clerics and aristocrats. Now, merchants, lawyers, apothecaries, schoolteachers — even some artisans — owned books. Literacy spread. But there was a problem: many people, especially the poor, could not read. So print culture invented ways to reach them too. **Ballads** — songs telling popular stories — were printed and sung aloud. **Folk tales** were illustrated with woodcuts so even the unlettered could follow the pictures while someone read the words. The boundary between "reading" and "listening" blurred. A village blacksmith might gather his neighbours at the tavern and a school-leaver would read aloud while the rest commented.

Third, ideas now traveled at unimaginable speed. A book published in Wittenberg could reach Paris in weeks, Rome in months. Reformers, scientists, philosophers found that their thoughts could be **broadcast** — a word that originally meant scattering seeds across a field.

Not everyone was happy.

Many in authority — rulers, priests, the wealthy — feared what print might do. If ordinary people could read freely, they might question those above them. If forbidden ideas spread, what would happen to obedience, to faith, to social order? These fears would prove well-founded.

## Print and the Protestant Reformation

In **1517**, a German monk named **Martin Luther** nailed (or perhaps mailed) ninety-five complaints — his **Ninety-Five Theses** — against the corruption of the Roman Catholic Church to a chapel door in Wittenberg. He attacked the sale of "indulgences" (paper certificates promising forgiveness of sins, for a fee), the worldliness of bishops, the gap between gospel and church practice.

In an earlier century, Luther's protest would have died in a few villages. But this was 1517. Within fifteen days, his theses had been printed and read across Germany. Within months, all of Europe was discussing them. Luther himself wrote a stream of pamphlets — sharp, simple, often crude — that printers eagerly produced. He translated the Bible into **German** so that ordinary believers could read scripture for themselves, rather than depend on Latin-speaking priests.

The **Protestant Reformation** that followed split European Christendom into Catholic and Protestant branches. Print had not caused the split alone — there were old religious tensions — but print made the split spread faster, deeper, and irreversibly. Luther himself said: *"Printing is the ultimate gift of God and the greatest one."*

The Catholic Church responded by setting up an **Index of Prohibited Books** in 1558 — a list of forbidden titles, which printers were not to publish and readers were not to read. The very existence of such an index admits the threat. Print had outgrown the ability of authority to control it.

## Reading Manias and Religious Doubts

By the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, literacy rates in parts of Europe climbed dramatically. In some Protestant regions of Germany, Sweden, and Scotland, almost everyone could read by 1800. Schools sprang up. Sunday schools taught children to read scripture. Cheap printed material — chapbooks, almanacs, ballads, religious tracts — was peddled by traveling salesmen called **chapmen** in England and **colporteurs** in France.

A new literary genre flourished: the **periodical**, which carried news, gossip, advice, criticism, advertisements. Coffee houses in London and salons in Paris became spaces where men gathered to read newspapers aloud and argue.

This intoxication with print also produced doubt. In sixteenth-century Italy, an unusual case appears in the records. A miller named **Menocchio**, born around 1532, started reading the few cheap books he could lay his hands on and concluded that the world had not been created the way the Church taught — instead, he said, it had grown out of decay like cheese, and worms had appeared in it. The Roman Catholic **Inquisition** found his ideas dangerous, summoned him twice, and finally executed him in 1599. A village miller had become a heretic because of his reading. Print could free minds; authority would resist that freedom; sometimes lives were lost in the contest.

## Print and the French Revolution

A few historians have argued — not all agree — that print **prepared the soil** for the **French Revolution** of 1789. The argument goes like this.

Through the eighteenth century, the writings of the **Enlightenment** thinkers — **Voltaire, Rousseau, Diderot, Montesquieu** — circulated through France in expensive books and cheap pamphlets, smuggled past censors, sold in plain wrappers, discussed in salons. These writers attacked the privileges of the Church, the corruption of the monarchy, the unjust distribution of wealth. They popularised ideas of **reason, liberty, equality, and the social contract**.

Cheap **pamphlets, cartoons, and posters** mocked Queen Marie Antoinette and her court — sometimes obscenely. The aura of royalty, the sense that the king was a holy figure chosen by God, was steadily eroded in the public mind. By the time the **Bastille was stormed on 14 July 1789**, French people had been reading anti-royal literature for decades.

Of course, this argument can be over-stated. The Revolution had many causes: starvation, taxation, the bankruptcy of the state, the resentment of the middle class. Print did not by itself cause revolt. But print **shifted what people thought was possible**. It made them imagine a world without kings.

## The Nineteenth Century: A Mass Market

Through the nineteenth century, three big changes pushed print culture from elite hobby to mass market.

(1) **Primary education** spread. Britain, France, Germany, and the United States expanded schools. By 1900, most children in industrialising Europe could read.

(2) **Children's literature** became a separate publishing genre. **Grimm Brothers** in Germany collected folk tales and published them as books for children (1812 onwards). Authors like **Charles Dickens** wrote books that were serialised in cheap weekly magazines — so even working-class families could afford them, instalment by instalment.

(3) **Women** began reading and writing in large numbers. Novelists like **Jane Austen, the Brontë sisters, and George Eliot** in Britain wrote about marriage, money, women's inner lives. Women's magazines flourished, filled with fiction, fashion, advice, and serialised stories.

By the end of the nineteenth century, the **novel** had become Europe's most influential literary form. People read about characters who looked like themselves — clerks, governesses, factory workers — and saw their own lives reflected in print for the first time.

## Print Comes to India

For centuries before printing, India had a magnificent **manuscript** culture. **Sanskrit, Tamil, Persian, Arabic, Bengali, Marathi** — and dozens of other languages — were written on **palm leaves**, on **handmade paper**, bound with strings or wooden covers, copied by professional scribes (**kayasthas, kayakaars**) for temples, courts, scholars. Manuscripts were beautifully illuminated. But they were expensive, fragile, and limited to a tiny educated elite.

The first **printing press** in India arrived with **Portuguese missionaries** at **Goa in 1556**. The Jesuits printed religious tracts in Konkani and Tamil. Through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, missionaries printed Bibles and religious literature in many south Indian and west Indian languages.

But it was under **British rule** in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries that print exploded in India. The **East India Company** brought in English presses. **Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras** became major centres of printing. From the 1780s, newspapers, journals, books, and pamphlets began to appear in growing numbers — first in **English**, then in **Bengali, Hindi, Urdu, Marathi, Gujarati, Tamil**, and other languages.

The first weekly Indian newspaper was **James Augustus Hicky's Bengal Gazette**, launched in **1780**. Hicky criticised colonial officials, was repeatedly threatened, and finally jailed. But he had shown that newspapers could be a tool of public criticism. Within decades, Indian-edited papers were following his example.

## Religious Reform and Public Debate

By the early nineteenth century, print had become the battleground for India's most heated religious and social debates.

**Raja Rammohan Roy** (1772–1833) of Bengal published the journal **Sambad Kaumudi** in 1821, arguing against **sati** (widow burning), child marriage, caste, and idolatry. His opponents responded with their own paper, **Samachar Chandrika**, defending tradition. Newspapers became weapons.

In the 1860s, religious reformers and their critics filled Indian presses with debate. **Swami Dayanand Saraswati**, founder of the **Arya Samaj**, used print to argue for a "purified" Hinduism returned to the Vedas. Muslim reformers — the **Deoband school**, the **Aligarh movement** under **Sir Syed Ahmed Khan** — published religious tracts, Quran translations, and educational textbooks. Even ordinary people, men with little formal education, set up small presses to argue with each other in Hindi, Urdu, Bengali, and Marathi.

Print made religion **public**. Earlier, debates about scripture had been confined to scholars. Now, a railway clerk in Patna could read an Urdu pamphlet about the proper interpretation of the Quran, or a Hindi tract about Vedic ritual, and form his own opinion.

## New Forms of Print: Religious Texts, Newspapers, Cheap Books

By the 1870s, an extraordinary explosion of cheap print was underway across India.

**Religious texts** were among the bestsellers. The **Ramcharitmanas of Tulsidas** — the Hindi retelling of the Ramayana — was printed in countless cheap editions and reached villages where literacy was just spreading. Suddenly, a peasant who could read a little could own his own Ramayana, rather than depend on a Brahmin's recitation. The **Quran**, the **Bhagavad Gita**, **Sikh scriptures**, **devotional songs of the Bhakti and Sufi poets** — all became cheaply available.

**Newspapers and magazines** multiplied. By 1900 there were several hundred Indian-language newspapers across the country. They circulated through small towns, were read aloud in tea shops, and shaped public opinion.

**Cheap novels and stories** — sometimes called **battala** literature in Bengal, after the Calcutta neighbourhood where many small presses clustered — flooded the market. Detective tales, romantic stories, satirical sketches, ghost stories, tales of saints and sinners — all printed on rough paper, sold for a few paise, devoured by clerks and students and housewives.

The visual side of print mattered too. **Mass-produced printed images** — calendars, posters, cheap chromolithographs of gods and goddesses — found their way into millions of Indian homes. The Maharashtrian painter **Raja Ravi Varma** (1848–1906) set up a printing press to make affordable reproductions of his oil paintings of Hindu deities. His images of Lakshmi, Saraswati, and other gods, printed in colour, hung on the walls of Indian homes from Kerala to Punjab. They shaped how millions imagined the divine.

## Women and Print

The arrival of cheap print transformed the **lives of Indian women** — slowly, partially, but unmistakably.

In nineteenth-century India, most women received no formal education. But mothers, sisters, and daughters in middle-class homes began to read and, increasingly, to write. Reformers like **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar** in Bengal pushed for widow remarriage and women's education through pamphlets and newspapers. Magazines aimed at women — discussing marriage, child-rearing, housekeeping, religion, fiction — proliferated in Bengali, Marathi, Hindi, and Tamil.

Some remarkable women became writers themselves. **Rashsundari Debi**, born around 1809, secretly taught herself to read while running a Bengali household and in the 1860s wrote **Amar Jiban** — the first full autobiography written by an Indian woman. **Kailashbashini Debi** wrote about the suffering of upper-caste women. **Tarabai Shinde** in Maharashtra and **Pandita Ramabai** wrote scorching critiques of upper-caste patriarchy. **Begum Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain**'s **Sultana's Dream** (1905) imagined a feminist utopia where women ruled and men were kept in seclusion — a stunning early science fiction.

Conservative voices fought back. Pamphlets warned that educated women would neglect their husbands and bring shame to families. Yet the writing did not stop. The very ability to argue back, in print, was a quiet revolution.

## Print and the Lower Castes

For the masses of **lower-caste Indians**, print became a tool of self-assertion against centuries of exclusion from learning.

**Jyotiba Phule** (1827–1890) in Maharashtra wrote searing critiques of the caste system — **Gulamgiri (Slavery)** in 1873, comparing the condition of lower castes in India to that of African Americans in the United States. He and his wife **Savitribai Phule** ran schools for lower-caste girls and used pamphlets to spread the cause.

**E. V. Ramasamy Periyar** in Madras Presidency edited papers like **Kudi Arasu** that attacked Brahmin domination, the caste system, and religious superstition. His "**Self-Respect Movement**" used print as its main weapon.

In 1920s Maharashtra, **Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar** founded papers like **Mooknayak (Leader of the Voiceless)** in 1920 and **Bahishkrit Bharat (Excluded India)** in 1927 to argue for Dalit rights. His writings — eventually including the magisterial **Annihilation of Caste** (1936) — would become foundational texts of the Dalit movement.

Print finally allowed those who had been forbidden to read Sanskrit, forbidden to sit in school next to Brahmin children, forbidden to speak in public assemblies, to **write their own version of India's story**.

## Print and the National Movement

By the late nineteenth century, print had become the **central infrastructure of the freedom struggle**.

The **Indian National Congress** (founded 1885) communicated through newspapers and pamphlets. **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** edited **Kesari** in Marathi and **Mahratta** in English, writing fiery articles attacking colonial policy. He was twice tried for sedition and jailed. His famous declaration — *"Swaraj is my birthright, and I shall have it"* — reached millions through print.

**Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi** edited **Young India** and **Harijan** in English, **Navajivan** in Gujarati and Hindi. His writings were a steady stream of arguments for non-violence, swaraj, village industries, religious harmony. When he was jailed, his words still circulated.

Posters and cartoons mocked British officials. Pamphlets explained how to participate in **Satyagraha**. Newspapers carried details of meetings, marches, and arrests. After the **Jallianwala Bagh massacre** of 1919, prints of the dead and wounded circulated across India, fuelling outrage. Censorship laws — the **Vernacular Press Act of 1878**, various sedition provisions — tried to silence Indian-language papers. Editors went to prison repeatedly. But the press never quite went silent.

By the time independence came in **1947**, India had one of the most diverse, multilingual, and politically committed press cultures in the world.

## The Print Heritage We Live In

The chapter we have just walked through ends in 1947, but its consequences continue. India today has tens of thousands of newspapers in dozens of languages. We read on screens as much as on paper, but the **format** of what we read — headlines, captions, paragraphs, columns, footnotes — was invented during the print age. Public opinion, mass politics, mass religion, mass education, women's voices, lower-caste voices — all of them entered the world fully through the door that Gutenberg, Hicky, Phule, Tilak, Roy, Ambedkar, and a thousand unnamed printers opened.

The next time you handle a school textbook, remember: behind every page, there is a five-hundred-year-old revolution. And behind every WhatsApp forward, perhaps, a faint echo of the same.

## Apply

### Three Stories from the Real World

**The Vernacular Press That Refuses to Die.** In most rich countries, newspapers are shrinking. Major American and European dailies have closed or cut their staff drastically. But in India, **regional-language newspapers** — *Eenadu* in Telugu, *Dinamalar* in Tamil, *Lokmat* in Marathi, *Malayala Manorama* in Malayalam — are still profitable, still expanding, still adding new editions. Why? Because literacy is still spreading, because many Indians prefer their own language to English, because reading a paper at a tea-stall is a ritual that has not died. The chapter you read is not a museum piece; the print revolution is still completing itself.

**WhatsApp Forwards and Nineteenth-Century Pamphlets.** When a religious or political message goes viral on WhatsApp, it spreads in ways uncannily similar to nineteenth-century pamphlets: short, punchy, emotionally charged, hard to disprove, passed hand to hand. A nineteenth-century Hindi pamphlet defending or attacking some religious practice would be read aloud in a tea shop and pass to twenty more readers. A WhatsApp forward today does the same, faster. The technology has changed; the underlying social logic — cheap, fast, emotionally loaded mass communication — has not.

**The Bestselling Author Your Daadi Reads.** Walk into any railway station bookstall in north India and you will find Hindi novelists like **Surender Mohan Pathak** (detective stories), **Munshi Premchand** (still selling, decades after his death), and stacks of cheap religious literature — pocket Hanuman Chalisas, Bhagavad Gitas, Quran translations, Sikh devotional collections. Your grandmother, who may never have finished school, may own and read several of these. She is the inheritor of a reading public that Indian print created in the nineteenth century. She does not always read English bestsellers. She reads what her language and her tradition has produced for her — exactly the kind of cheap, accessible, vernacular literature that print made possible.

### DIY Activity

(1) **The Newspaper Audit.** Visit a roadside newspaper vendor in your town or city. Photograph or list **five newspapers in five different languages** that he sells. Look at: their price, their size, their cover stories, whether they are local or national, whether they have photographs in colour or black-and-white. Try to figure out who their readers are.

(2) **The Printing Press Visit.** Find a **local printing press** in your town. Many small towns still have small offset or digital printing units that print invitation cards, school magazines, election posters, brochures. Visit one. Ask the owner: when was the press set up? What does he mostly print? Has his business changed in the last twenty years?

(3) **The Old Book Hunt.** Find the **oldest book in your home** — perhaps a religious text, a school textbook of your grandparent, a worn paperback novel, a song collection. Note its date of publication, its publisher, its language, its condition. Ask the family member who owned it what the book meant to them.

### A Question to Keep Asking

In a country like India, where a billion+ people are still becoming readers, where new languages are still being given printed dictionaries, where a vernacular newspaper opens a new edition every few months — **what does it mean to belong to a print culture that is still expanding?** Are screens replacing print, or quietly joining it?
