## Did Books Always Look Like This?

**Child:** Maa, was Gutenberg actually the first to print? You said grandma's old religious book was very old too...

**Mother:** Good catch. Gutenberg was not the first to print at all. The Chinese were printing books at least **eight hundred years** before Gutenberg picked up a goldsmith's tool in Germany. By the year **594 CE** — around the time Indians were composing some of our oldest copper-plate inscriptions — Chinese craftsmen were already rubbing inked wooden blocks against paper. The oldest surviving printed book in the world is a **Buddhist Diamond Sutra from 868 CE**, found in a cave on the Silk Road. It is more than a thousand years old.

**Child:** So why does everyone talk about Gutenberg?

**Mother:** Because of one very clever idea. The Chinese carved each page on a single block of wood. A whole book took hundreds of blocks. If even one character was wrong, the entire block had to be re-carved. Gutenberg's breakthrough — around **1448** — was **movable type**: each letter cast as a tiny separate metal piece. You could line up letters to form words, words to form lines, lines to form a page, print the page, then **pull the letters apart and use them again** for the next book. The same set of letters could print a Bible, then a school grammar, then a poem, then a notice from the king.

**Child:** Like Lego bricks for letters.

**Mother:** Almost exactly. And once you had that, books could be made fast and cheap. Within fifty years of Gutenberg's first Bible, Europe had something like **twenty million** printed books in circulation. The world before Gutenberg was a world where most people had probably never *touched* a book in their lives. The world after Gutenberg was a world where common people could begin to own one.

**Child:** And dadi's old religious book?

**Mother:** That book is probably from the late nineteenth century — by which time print had reached India in full force. Look at it carefully sometime. Each page will have a publisher's name, a place of printing (perhaps Lucknow, Banaras, or Calcutta), maybe even a price stamped in old currency. It is a direct descendant of the same revolution Gutenberg started.

## How Did Cheap Print Change What Ordinary People Believed?

**Child:** The textbook keeps saying print spread the Protestant Reformation. But Martin Luther was just one monk. How did one monk start a religious revolution?

**Mother:** This is one of the most important questions in the chapter. Let me tell you the story properly.

In **1517**, Martin Luther was a German monk angry about something specific — the **selling of indulgences**. Indulgences were paper certificates that the Catholic Church sold; the buyer was told his sins (or his dead relatives' sins) would be forgiven. To Luther this was scandal. Christ had not died on the cross so that the Church could collect money for forgiveness. He wrote up his protest in **Ninety-Five Theses** and either nailed them to a church door or sent them around. We are not sure which.

**Child:** So what happened next?

**Mother:** Without printing, Luther's protest would have remained a quarrel among monks. With printing, his theses were copied, printed, and read across Germany within **fifteen days**. Within a few months, all of Europe was discussing them. Luther himself wrote pamphlets — short, sharp, often funny, sometimes vulgar — and printers grabbed them up. He translated the Bible into German so ordinary people could read it without depending on a Latin-speaking priest.

**Child:** So the Bible used to be in Latin only?

**Mother:** Yes. And only priests could read Latin. Imagine that. The entire sacred text of the religion was in a language ordinary believers could not understand. The priest stood between them and God. Luther's German Bible smashed that wall. Once Germans could read scripture in their own language, they could form their own opinions about what their religion really required. Many concluded the Catholic Church had drifted far from the gospel. Soon, half of northern Europe broke away into what we call **Protestant churches**.

**Child:** Could the Church not stop the books?

**Mother:** It tried. In **1558**, the Roman Catholic Church set up the **Index of Prohibited Books** — a list of forbidden titles. Printers were not to print them; Catholics were not to read them. In some countries the **Inquisition** burned forbidden books and sometimes their owners. There is even a famous case — a sixteenth-century Italian miller named **Menocchio** — who read a few cheap books on his own, came up with strange ideas about how the world was created (he thought it had grown out of decay like cheese), and was executed by the Inquisition in 1599. One village miller. Killed because of his reading.

**Child:** That is terrifying.

**Mother:** Yes. But notice what it also tells us. Authority was afraid of print **because print worked**. Once ordinary people could read for themselves, they could no longer be told what to believe. The Reformation, the Enlightenment, the French Revolution, the women's rights movement, the freedom struggle in India — every one of them ran on the printed word. Print did not give people their ideas. It gave them the chance to **circulate** ideas, to **argue**, to **disagree publicly** with their elders and rulers. That was new in human history.

## Why Were Indian Reformers So Obsessed with Newspapers?

**Child:** I get why Tilak ran a newspaper — he was fighting the British. But why did people like Rammohan Roy and Dayanand Saraswati and Sir Syed Ahmed Khan, who were not really fighting the British, also start newspapers? They were religious reformers, no?

**Mother:** Very good question. And the answer reveals something important about how Indian society works. In India in the early nineteenth century, **religion was the most important public conversation**. Should widows be allowed to remarry? Should child marriage be ended? Was sati a holy duty or a cruel murder? Were Brahmins really superior? What did the Quran actually say about women's rights? Was idol worship pure Hinduism or had it drifted from the Vedas?

These were not abstract questions. They were burning, family-dividing, life-changing questions. And before the printing press, you could only argue about them in person — in a few learned gatherings, in temple courts, in private letters between scholars.

**Child:** And once there were newspapers?

**Mother:** Once there were newspapers, you could argue with **thousands of strangers at once**. Rammohan Roy launched his Bengali paper **Sambad Kaumudi** in 1821 to attack sati and idolatry. His conservative opponents started **Samachar Chandrika** to defend tradition. The two papers fought each other week after week, and every literate Bengali was reading the argument. That had never happened before in Indian history.

**Child:** So newspapers were like… the social media of the time?

**Mother:** Closer than you think. A Hindi pamphlet defending a particular form of Vedic ritual might be printed in Banaras, sold in Patna for one paisa, read aloud in a tea-shop, passed to four more readers, and discussed in five villages — all within a month. That was breathtaking speed.

And it was not only the famous reformers. Ordinary clerks, school teachers, small zamindars, lawyers — all started their own little papers, often badly written, often short-lived, but full of arguments. People learned to **disagree in public**. They learned that there was such a thing as **public opinion**, and that you could try to shape it.

**Child:** Did this make society better?

**Mother:** It made it noisier. And on the whole, yes — better. Sati became indefensible in public, and was banned in 1829. Widow remarriage gained support, helped by **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar**'s pamphlets. Female education spread. Many cruelties of the old order were dragged into the open and shamed. Not because print magically convinced everyone, but because in print, the people defending the old order had to write out their reasons — and often, their reasons looked thin in black and white.

## What Did Women Do with the New Reading Material?

**Child:** What about women, Maa? The chapter says lots of new books reached women too.

**Mother:** It did. And this is one of the most interesting parts of the story, because women were **not supposed to be reading** in nineteenth-century India. In many households, teaching a girl to read was considered dangerous — it would supposedly distract her from housework, make her independent, expose her to corrupting novels, and (one truly absurd belief) **shorten her husband's life**.

**Child:** Wait — what?

**Mother:** Yes, some pamphlets seriously argued that a girl who learned to read would become a widow. So most girls were kept away from books.

But once cheap print arrived, many women found ways. Mothers and grandmothers, who themselves could not read, would sometimes secretly teach their daughters. Some women learned by listening to their brothers' lessons through the wall. There is a beautiful early autobiography by a Bengali woman named **Rashsundari Debi**, born around 1809. She wanted to read scripture but it was forbidden for her gender. So she secretly memorised the shape of letters she saw on torn book pages, then practiced writing them with a stick on the kitchen floor. By the time she was an old woman, she had taught herself completely. In the **1860s**, she published **Amar Jiban** — *My Life* — the first full autobiography written by an Indian woman.

**Child:** That is amazing.

**Mother:** And she was just one. **Tarabai Shinde** in Maharashtra wrote a fierce essay called *Stripurush Tulana* — *A Comparison Between Women and Men* — attacking the double standards of upper-caste society. **Pandita Ramabai** wrote about the misery of Hindu widows in her travel book in English. And in 1905, an extraordinary Bengali Muslim woman named **Begum Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain** wrote a short story called **Sultana's Dream** — a feminist science fiction in which women ruled an ideal city and men were kept in seclusion. It was published in an English magazine in Madras. A hundred years later it is still in print.

**Child:** Did women's magazines come up?

**Mother:** Many. In Bengali, Marathi, Hindi, Tamil — all major Indian languages got their women's magazines by the late nineteenth and early twentieth century. They carried fiction, advice on running a household, recipes, articles on women's education, sometimes brave editorials on widow remarriage and child marriage. Conservative voices fought back with their own pamphlets warning that "modern women" would destroy the family. But the writing did not stop. Once women had a printed page on which to argue, they did not give it up.

**Child:** Could most women read, though?

**Mother:** No. Female literacy was very low — under 5% in the late nineteenth century. But a small minority of educated women, supported by reform-minded fathers and husbands, used the printing press to argue for change. That small minority shaped a much larger conversation. Indian feminism, in the way we know it today, was largely **born in print**.

## Were Caste-Reform Writers as Important as Bestseller Novelists?

**Child:** The textbook gives equal space to people like Phule and Ambedkar and to popular novelists. Were the caste reformers really as important as novelists who sold lakhs of copies?

**Mother:** That is a sharp question. Let me answer it carefully.

In **commercial terms** — copies sold — popular novelists like Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay in Bengali, Premchand in Hindi, and many others probably out-sold the caste reformers many times over. Their novels were entertainment. They were the Netflix of the time. Every literate Bengali wanted to read Bankim's *Anandamath*; every literate Hindi speaker wanted to read Premchand's *Godan* or *Gaban*.

But you have to think about what each kind of book **did to the reader**. A novel might move you, entertain you, teach you something. A pamphlet by **Jyotiba Phule** — like his 1873 book **Gulamgiri**, meaning *Slavery* — might **change your conception of yourself**. Phule was a low-caste Maharashtrian who compared the condition of lower-caste Indians under Brahmin domination to the condition of African Americans under slavery in the United States. For a low-caste reader who had been told all his life that his low status was god-given and deserved, reading Phule was earthquake.

**Child:** And Ambedkar?

**Mother:** Ambedkar founded papers like **Mooknayak** — *Leader of the Voiceless* — in 1920, and **Bahishkrit Bharat** — *Excluded India* — in 1927. He wrote books, the most famous of which is **Annihilation of Caste** in 1936. Through these writings he gave Indian Dalits something they had never been given by the upper-caste-dominated public sphere: a coherent, intellectual, fully argued case for their dignity, equality, and rights. He used the same printing presses that the Brahmin elite used. He could not be shouted down or excluded from the public square because the public square was now made of paper.

**Child:** So the bestsellers reached more people, but the caste reformers changed more lives?

**Mother:** I would say each shaped India differently. Bestseller novelists shaped what people felt and dreamed. Caste reformers shaped what people **politically demanded**. The Constitution of India that protects every Dalit child's right to education — that constitution was largely drafted by Ambedkar. Without his decades of writing and arguing in print, the political case for reservations, anti-untouchability laws, and constitutional protection would have been much weaker. Print gave Dalit voices a place to stand. From there, they reshaped the republic.

**Child:** And Periyar?

**Mother:** **E. V. Ramasamy Periyar** in Tamil Nadu edited a paper called **Kudi Arasu** that attacked Brahmin domination, religious superstition, and caste pride. His Self-Respect Movement still shapes Tamil politics today. Tamil political identity, which has produced such powerful parties as the DMK, has roots directly in the printed pamphlets and papers Periyar produced. Print built movements.

## What Does Print Mean in the Age of WhatsApp?

**Child:** Maa, one last thing. When my friends say "no one reads anymore," is it true? Is print dying?

**Mother:** It is changing, not dying. And in India it is changing in very specific ways.

In **rich Western countries** — America, Britain, Western Europe — newspaper readership has fallen sharply, magazines have closed, even bookshops are shrinking. Most reading there has shifted to screens.

In **India**, the picture is more complex. **Regional-language newspapers** are still profitable and growing. *Malayala Manorama* in Malayalam, *Eenadu* in Telugu, *Dinamalar* in Tamil, *Lokmat* in Marathi, *Dainik Bhaskar* in Hindi — these papers have huge circulation, often larger than top English papers. New editions are still being opened in smaller towns. Why? Because literacy is still spreading in India — every year, several million more Indians become readers. And many readers still prefer the morning ritual of a paper at the tea stall in their own language.

**Child:** And books?

**Mother:** Hindi pulp fiction at railway station bookstalls, cheap religious books at every temple, school textbooks and exam guides — these are massive markets. Your daadi reads cheap printed religious books. Your cousins read Hindi crime fiction by writers like Surender Mohan Pathak. India probably has more booksellers per square kilometre than most countries.

**Child:** What about WhatsApp forwards?

**Mother:** Here is the irony. WhatsApp forwards mimic the **nineteenth-century pamphlet** more closely than they mimic the modern news article. Short. Punchy. Emotionally charged. Often anonymous. Often unreliable. Read aloud and passed to twenty more readers. A Hindi pamphlet defending or attacking some religious cause in 1875 would have spread through tea shops the same way a WhatsApp forward spreads through family groups in 2025. The technology is new; the social logic is very old.

**Child:** So the print revolution is still going on?

**Mother:** In a sense, yes — and it has also evolved. The basic transformation Gutenberg started was: **make ideas cheap, fast, and unstoppable**. Television, internet, social media, smartphones — all of these are children of that original revolution. They all follow the same logic: more information, faster, to more people, for less money. The press changed everything in 1500. The smartphone is changing everything now. The story of print is, in some ways, the story of our own time.

**Child:** That actually makes me look at my phone differently.

**Mother:** Good. Read your chapter once more with that thought in mind. Every time you scroll a feed, you are using the same revolution that Gutenberg, Hicky, Rammohan Roy, Rashsundari Debi, Tilak, Phule, Ambedkar, and a thousand small printers helped set in motion. You are part of that story.
