## In One Paragraph

The story of print is the story of how words and images broke out of the hands of scribes, priests, and kings and reached ordinary people. It began in China around the seventh century with hand-printed wooden blocks, spread to Korea and Japan with Buddhism, and entered Europe through paper from the Arab world. In the 1450s, Johann Gutenberg's movable-type press made books cheap enough that millions of copies could be printed within decades. Print spread Martin Luther's Protestant Reformation, fed the Enlightenment that prepared the French Revolution, and gave Europe its mass-reading public. From the late eighteenth century, print arrived in colonial India in force. Reformers like Rammohan Roy, novelists, religious writers, women like Rashsundari Debi and Begum Rokeya, lower-caste leaders like Phule, Periyar, and Ambedkar, and nationalists like Tilak and Gandhi all used the printed word to argue, organise, and reshape Indian society. Print did not just record the modern world — it helped make it.

## Key Takeaways

- Hand-printing began in **China around 594 CE** using carved wooden blocks; the world's oldest surviving printed book is the Buddhist **Diamond Sutra (868 CE)**.
- China had a sophisticated print culture for centuries before Europe, driven by imperial civil-service exams and urban readers.
- **Johann Gutenberg** invented **movable metal type** around 1448; his **Bible (c. 1454–55)** launched Europe's print revolution.
- By 1500, around **20 million** printed books were circulating in Europe; by 1600, around **200 million**.
- Cheap print created a new **reading public** that included merchants, artisans, women, and eventually working people — though many still listened to books read aloud.
- **Martin Luther's** Reformation (1517) was spread by the printing of his pamphlets and German Bible; print made splits in religion and politics faster and harder to reverse.
- Enlightenment writers like **Voltaire, Rousseau, and Diderot** circulated ideas that helped prepare the ground for the **French Revolution** of 1789.
- In **nineteenth-century Europe**, primary education, children's books, novels, and women writers (Austen, the Brontës, Eliot) made print a part of everyday life.
- Print came to **India** in **1556** with Portuguese missionaries in Goa; under the British, it exploded from the 1780s onward, beginning with **Hicky's Bengal Gazette**.
- **Religious and social reform** in India was waged largely in print — **Rammohan Roy, Dayanand Saraswati, Sir Syed Ahmed Khan** all used newspapers and pamphlets to argue their cases.
- **Women writers** like **Rashsundari Debi, Pandita Ramabai, Tarabai Shinde, and Begum Rokeya** used print to voice and challenge their conditions.
- **Lower-caste leaders** — **Jyotiba Phule, Periyar, and B. R. Ambedkar** — used newspapers, pamphlets, and books to articulate Dalit and anti-caste perspectives.
- **Nationalist leaders** — **Tilak's Kesari, Gandhi's Young India, Harijan, Navajivan** — turned the press into a major tool of the freedom struggle, surviving repeated colonial censorship and arrests.

## Key Terms

- **Movable type**: Individual metal letters that can be arranged and rearranged to print any text — Gutenberg's central innovation.
- **Manuscript**: A book or document written by hand, the dominant form before printing.
- **Vellum / Parchment**: Animal skin used as a writing surface before paper became affordable.
- **Reformation**: The sixteenth-century religious movement, led by Luther and others, that split Western Christianity into Catholic and Protestant churches.
- **Enlightenment**: An eighteenth-century European intellectual movement that emphasised reason, liberty, and questioning of authority.
- **Chapbooks / Ballads**: Cheap printed pamphlets and songs that brought stories and news to ordinary readers in Europe.
- **Vernacular Press Act (1878)**: A colonial law that empowered British authorities to censor or shut down Indian-language newspapers.
- **Battala**: A district of Calcutta famous for cheap printed Bengali novels, almanacs, and devotional books in the nineteenth century.
- **Ukiyo-e**: Japanese woodblock prints — "pictures of the floating world" — that captured urban life and entertainment.
- **Inquisition / Index of Prohibited Books**: Catholic Church institutions that censored books deemed dangerous to faith and authority.
