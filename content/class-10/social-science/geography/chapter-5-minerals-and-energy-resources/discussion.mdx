## What Happens When the Coal Runs Out?

**Child:** Maa, my friend said one day all the coal will run out. Then what? Will our fans and trains stop?

**Mother:** It is a real question, beta — not a silly one. Coal *will* run out. India has roughly 300 billion tonnes of total coal reserves, of which around 100 billion tonnes are proven and extractable. At our current rate of consumption — about a billion tonnes a year — that gives us roughly a hundred years of coal at the proven level, give or take. So you and I will be gone before the last coal mine closes, but your grandchildren may see it.

**Child:** Hundred years sounds like a long time.

**Mother:** It is, and it isn't. Two world wars happened in less than a hundred years. Cars went from a curiosity to filling our streets in less than a hundred years. The internet did it in twenty. So a hundred years is the kind of time we have to plan for, not feel comfortable about. And remember — the problem isn't only running out. The problem is *climate*. Even if we had a thousand years of coal, burning it all would cook the planet. The country that figures out clean energy first will rule the next century — the way the country that figured out coal-fired steam ruled the nineteenth century.

**Child:** So who will figure it out?

**Mother:** Right now China is way ahead — they make most of the world's solar panels and electric cars. But India is moving fast. The Bhadla Solar Park in Rajasthan, Pavagada in Karnataka, the offshore wind plans in Gujarat and Tamil Nadu — these are all serious investments. The fans and trains will not stop, beta. They will just run on a different fuel. But the country that takes its time will pay more for that switch than the country that runs.

## Why Do Some Indian States Have More Mineral Wealth?

**Child:** Maa, why do Jharkhand, Odisha, and Chhattisgarh have so many minerals — and Punjab and Kerala have almost none?

**Mother:** Geology, beta. Pure geology. The minerals in a state were put there by the rocks below the soil, and the rocks were formed by processes that played out over hundreds of millions of years — long before any state boundaries existed. The Chota Nagpur plateau, which sits under Jharkhand, parts of Odisha, parts of West Bengal, and parts of Chhattisgarh, is one of the oldest geological formations in India — part of what geologists call the Indian Shield. It went through periods of volcanic activity and sedimentation that left it rich in iron ore, coal, copper, mica, and bauxite.

**Child:** And Punjab?

**Mother:** Punjab sits on alluvial soil — the soil deposited by the Indus and its tributaries over the last ten thousand years. That alluvium is wonderful for farming, but it has no metallic minerals. The same is true for the Gangetic plains. So Bihar's farmland is brilliant, but Bihar's hills (in what is now Jharkhand) hold the minerals — and that is why Jharkhand was separated from Bihar in 2000. Geography decides what the land *gives*. Politics tries to decide what is *done* with what the land gives.

**Child:** And Kerala?

**Mother:** Kerala has a different geology — coastal sand and laterite, with some heavy minerals in its beach sands (the famous **Monazite sands**, rich in thorium). So Kerala does have a strategic mineral — thorium — but no coal, no iron ore, no copper. Different geology, different gifts.

## How Are Minerals Mined? Is It Dangerous?

**Child:** Maa, I have seen pictures of mines on TV. They look terrifying. People are working far underground. Why do they do it?

**Mother:** Two reasons, beta. Either they have no other job — or the pay is much better than other jobs in the area, even if the work is brutal. Most mining is dangerous in three ways.

**Child:** Three ways?

**Mother:** First, **physical danger**. Underground mines can collapse, flood, or fill with poisonous gases. Open-cast mines have falling rocks and heavy machinery. In the Jharia coalfield, parts of which have been on fire underground for nearly a hundred years, the ground itself can subside without warning. Second, **health damage**. Coal dust causes a lung disease called **pneumoconiosis** — sometimes called "black lung." Mica miners suffer from silicosis. The diseases show up years later, when the worker is too old to work and too poor to fight a court case. Third, **environmental damage** that spreads beyond the mine. Water turns acidic. Rivers run red with iron oxide. Forests are cleared. Wildlife is pushed out. The people who live downstream from the mine — who never asked for it — pay the cost.

**Child:** Then why is it allowed?

**Mother:** Because we *need* the minerals to run the country. Your school's steel staircase, your phone's copper wiring, the cement of your wall — they all come from a mine somewhere. The question is not whether to mine but **how** to mine, and **who benefits** from the mining. A well-regulated mine with safety gear, fair wages, environmental restoration after the mine closes, and a share of royalty going to local communities is very different from an illegal night-time mine that strips the hill and runs. India has both kinds. The fight is to make all our mines look like the first.

## Why Are States Like Jharkhand Rich in Minerals But Poor in People?

**Child:** Maa, this is the part that confuses me most. Jharkhand has the most coal, the best mica, plenty of iron ore — but the people there are very poor. How can a state be rich in resources but poor in life?

**Mother:** It is called the **resource curse**, beta. Economists noticed it long ago. Countries and states with abundant natural resources often grow *slower* and become *more* unequal than countries with no resources. Look at Africa — countries like Congo are sitting on cobalt, copper, gold — and they are some of the poorest places on earth. The same logic applies inside India.

**Child:** But how does that happen?

**Mother:** Three reasons. **One — the wealth leaves the place where it is dug.** When iron ore is mined in Singhbhum and exported to Japan, the iron and the money both leave Jharkhand. The factories that turn iron into steel, the engineers, the office workers, the tax base — they are somewhere else. The hole in the ground stays in Jharkhand. **Two — the mining companies often don't pay enough royalty to local communities, and what they do pay often doesn't reach the village.** Corruption eats much of it. **Three — when a state has easy mineral money, the government doesn't try as hard to build schools, hospitals, and industries.** Why bother, when royalty cheques arrive every quarter? Norway is famous for *avoiding* the resource curse — it puts its oil money into a sovereign fund for the future generation. India is slowly trying to do the same with district mineral funds, but it has taken decades.

**Child:** So the minerals end up hurting the people who live on top of them?

**Mother:** Often yes. Unless someone deliberately fights for them. The tribal communities of central India — Santals, Mundas, Hos, Gonds — have been fighting for centuries to keep the value of their land. The Niyamgiri victory of the Dongria Kondh is one of those rare moments when they won. Most of the time, they lose.

## What's the Future of Indian Energy?

**Child:** Maa, if you had to bet, what will India's energy look like when I am your age?

**Mother:** I will give you my honest guess, beta. Coal will still be there — probably 30 to 40 per cent of our electricity, down from over half today. **Solar will be the giant** — by 2050, it could be 40 per cent or more of our electricity. **Wind will be the second giant** — maybe 15 per cent. Nuclear will grow slowly to perhaps 10 per cent. Petroleum will still power most cars and trucks for a while, but electric vehicles will take over the cities first. And there will be one new player you should watch — **green hydrogen**.

**Child:** Green what?

**Mother:** Hydrogen made by splitting water using solar electricity. Burn it, and it gives only water back. The Indian government has a National Green Hydrogen Mission. If it succeeds, hydrogen will power trucks, ships, and possibly aeroplanes. That is the dream.

**Child:** Will the air get cleaner?

**Mother:** Eventually, yes. But the transition is messy. We will have years of overlap — old coal plants still running while new solar plants come up. Cities like Delhi will struggle with air quality for another decade at least. Your generation has to push for the switch faster than mine did.

## Why Is Solar Energy a Big Deal for India?

**Child:** Maa, every other country talks about wind or nuclear. Why does India keep talking about solar?

**Mother:** Three simple reasons, beta. **One — geography.** India lies in the tropical belt, between roughly 8 degrees and 36 degrees north. We get over 300 sunny days a year across most of the country. The Thar desert in Rajasthan gets over 5.5 kWh of solar energy per square metre per day — among the highest in the world. We have what the energy people call **a free sunlight subsidy from nature**.

**Child:** Two?

**Mother:** **Two — economics.** Solar panel prices have fallen by about 90 per cent in the last fifteen years. In 2010, a unit of solar electricity cost more than coal. Today, in the Indian market, a unit of solar electricity is *cheaper* than a unit of coal electricity. That changes everything. Solar is no longer the green choice — it is the cheap choice.

**Child:** Three?

**Mother:** **Three — energy security.** India imports about 80 per cent of its petroleum. Every time the Russia-Ukraine war or a tension in the Middle East pushes oil prices up, our rupee falls and our import bill explodes. Solar is *Indian* — the sunlight falls on Bhadla and Pavagada, not in Saudi Arabia. The more we run on solar, the less the world's politics can hurt our economy.

**Child:** But solar only works in the day. What about night?

**Mother:** Excellent point, beta. That is the **storage** problem — and the biggest engineering challenge of this decade. **Batteries** are getting cheaper, but they are still expensive at the scale of a country. **Pumped hydro storage** — using cheap daytime solar to pump water up to a reservoir, then letting it fall through turbines at night — is one solution. **Green hydrogen** is another. Until storage becomes cheap, we will still need coal and nuclear for night-time power. After storage becomes cheap, the game changes completely.

## What Should I Take From This?

**Child:** Maa, this chapter has so many states, so many minerals, so many places. If I had to remember the most important thing, what would it be?

**Mother:** Three things, beta. Listen carefully.

**One — minerals are finite, energy choices are not.** We cannot create iron ore or coal — what is in the ground is all we will ever have. But we can choose, every year, what energy mix we want to live with. That choice is not made by ministers alone. It is made by every household that decides to install a solar water heater, every commuter who takes the metro instead of a scooter, every factory that switches to LED lights. Energy is a *political* choice as much as a technical one.

**Two — the place where minerals come from is rarely the place where they are used.** Iron from Singhbhum becomes steel in Pohang, Korea. Bauxite from Niyamgiri would have become aluminium in foreign smelters. The unfairness of this gap is one of the great moral questions of modern geography. It is why tribal villages in central India are right to ask for a fair share of what their land gives.

**Three — the future is going to be solar.** Not because the government says so, not because the climate activists say so — because the *price* says so. The cheapest electricity an Indian factory can buy today is solar. The cheapest electricity an Indian household can buy in ten years will almost certainly be rooftop solar plus a battery. The question isn't *whether* we will switch. It is *who* will switch first — and *who* will be left holding stranded coal mines and unpaid bills.

**Child:** That last part scares me a little.

**Mother:** It should scare you a healthy amount, beta. Big transitions create big winners and big losers. The kid who studies physics today and works in solar storage in 2040 will be a winner. The coal-mining town that doesn't plan for the transition will be a loser. Your job, while you are still in school, is to *understand* this change. Then, when you are older, you can decide which side of it you want to stand on.
