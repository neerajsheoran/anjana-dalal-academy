## A Country Sitting on Treasure — and Yet

If you draw a map of India and shade the districts that hold rich deposits of iron ore, coal, copper, bauxite, and manganese, you will find that the shaded patches cover roughly the same belt — a wide diagonal from Jharkhand and Odisha through Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh into eastern Maharashtra and northern Karnataka. That belt is one of the most mineral-rich tracts on Earth. India produces about 95 different minerals — 4 fuel, 10 metallic, 23 non-metallic, 3 atomic, and 55 minor minerals. Steel mills in Jamshedpur, aluminium smelters in Korba, and copper refineries in Ghatsila were built on top of this belt.

And yet, the districts that hold the minerals are often the **poorest** districts in the country. Jharkhand has India's largest reserves of coal and mica — and one of the lowest per-capita incomes. The mining villages of Keonjhar and Singhbhum live without piped water while their iron ore feeds the smelters of South Korea and Japan. Why does mineral wealth not translate into national wealth? That is the puzzle at the heart of this chapter — and the reason geography matters to every Indian.

This chapter walks through what minerals are, how they are classified, where the major ones are found in India, what is happening to them, and the slow but real shift toward **renewable energy**. The two stories — minerals running out, and renewables stepping in — are the two halves of India's energy future.

## What Exactly Is a Mineral?

A **mineral** is a naturally occurring **homogeneous substance** with a definable internal structure. Most rocks are made up of minerals, the way a wall is made up of bricks. Geologists have identified over 2,000 minerals; only about 100 of them have any real economic importance, and only about a dozen are the workhorses of modern industry.

Minerals occur in different kinds of formations:

- **In igneous and metamorphic rocks**, minerals often crystallise inside cracks, joints, faults, and fissures as molten rock cools. The smaller cracks are called **veins** and the larger ones are called **lodes**. Tin, copper, zinc, and lead are usually found in this form, often deep underground.
- **In sedimentary rocks**, minerals are deposited in layers as the result of long deposition, accumulation, and concentration in horizontal beds. **Coal, iron ore, gypsum, potash, and sodium salt** are typical sedimentary minerals. (Coal and petroleum, you may remember, are also formed from the fossilised remains of plants and animals.)
- **By the decomposition of surface rocks**, leaving a residual mass of weathered material. **Bauxite** — our main aluminium ore — is formed this way.
- **As alluvial deposits in sands of valley floors and at the base of hills**. These are called **placer deposits** and usually contain minerals not corroded by water — like gold, silver, tin, and platinum.
- **In ocean waters**. Common salt, magnesium, and bromine are obtained from sea water; manganese nodules sit on the ocean bed.

Knowing where minerals form helps us know where to look for them. That is why geology and geography sit so close together in this chapter.

## Classifying Minerals — A Quick Map

A class 10 student should be able to draw the mineral classification chart from memory. Minerals are first split into **metallic** and **non-metallic** (and then we add energy-producing minerals like coal and petroleum).

- **Metallic minerals** are further split into:
  - **Ferrous** — those that contain iron. Examples: **iron ore, manganese, nickel, cobalt**.
  - **Non-ferrous** — those without iron. Examples: **copper, lead, tin, bauxite, zinc**.
- **Non-metallic minerals** include **mica, limestone, salt, gypsum, dolomite**.
- **Energy minerals** — also called **mineral fuels** — include **coal, petroleum, natural gas, and uranium**. (Uranium technically sits in the atomic minerals box.)

The split matters because the **uses** of each group are very different. Ferrous minerals feed our steel industry. Non-ferrous minerals feed electrical, chemical, and aerospace industries. Non-metallic minerals feed construction and chemicals. Energy minerals fuel everything.

## Ferrous Minerals — The Iron That Built Modern India

### Iron Ore

Iron is the backbone of industrialisation. Steel is "the cement of the modern world." India is fortunate to have some of the finest reserves anywhere — particularly of **haematite** (the most important industrial iron ore, with up to 70 per cent iron content) and **magnetite** (the finest iron ore, with very high iron content and excellent magnetic qualities, valuable in the electrical industry).

India's iron ore is concentrated in four major belts:

1. **Odisha–Jharkhand belt** — the richest. The mines of **Badampahar** in the Mayurbhanj and Kendujhar districts of Odisha and **Gua and Noamundi** in Singhbhum district of Jharkhand produce some of the highest-grade haematite anywhere on Earth.
2. **Durg–Bastar–Chandrapur belt** — running through Chhattisgarh and into Maharashtra. The famous **Bailadila** range in Bastar has 14 deposits of high-grade haematite, much of which is exported to Japan and South Korea through the port of **Visakhapatnam**.
3. **Ballari–Chitradurga–Chikkamagaluru–Tumakuru belt** — in Karnataka. The **Kudremukh** mines, once the largest single iron ore producer in the country, supplied a slurry pipeline that carried ore all the way to Mangaluru port. The Kudremukh story (which we will return to) became a lesson in mining-vs-environment.
4. **Maharashtra–Goa belt** — including the deposits of Ratnagiri in Maharashtra and the Goa mining belt. Goa's ore is somewhat lower-grade but very cheap to extract; it is exported through Marmagao port.

Read those four names like a chant. They cover almost all of India's iron ore production.

### Manganese

Manganese is the **second** ferrous mineral — and a vital alloying agent. It is used to make steel hard and corrosion-resistant: nearly **10 kg of manganese is required to manufacture one tonne of steel**. It is also used to make ferro-manganese alloys, bleaching powder, insecticides, and paints. **Odisha is the largest producer of manganese ore in India** (roughly one-third of total production), followed by Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, and Andhra Pradesh.

## Non-Ferrous Minerals — Copper, Bauxite, and the Modern Economy

### Copper

Copper is **malleable, ductile, and a great conductor of electricity** — which is why nearly all electrical wiring, electronics, and electrical equipment depend on it. India is critically short of copper. Major reserves are concentrated in three pockets:

- The **Balaghat mines in Madhya Pradesh** — currently the country's leading producer.
- The **Khetri belt in Rajasthan**, where the Khetri Copper Complex has been a Hindustan Copper Limited operation for decades.
- The **Singhbhum district of Jharkhand**, where Ghatsila has been a major centre of copper smelting since the 1920s.

If you trace the cables behind your refrigerator and your inverter, the chances are good that some of the copper inside them passed through one of these three mines.

### Bauxite

**Bauxite** is the ore from which **aluminium** is extracted. Aluminium combines the strength of metals with the lightness of plastics — and is therefore a core material for the aviation, railway, electrical, and packaging industries. India's bauxite deposits are concentrated mainly in the **Amarkantak plateau, the Maikal hills, and the plateau region of Bilaspur–Katni**. Odisha is again the **largest bauxite-producing state in India**, accounting for nearly 45 per cent of total production. The **Panchpatmali deposits in the Koraput district of Odisha** are the largest in the country.

The story of NALCO (National Aluminium Company) in Odisha — and the resistance of the **Dongria Kondh** tribe to the Vedanta company's bauxite-mining proposal on the **Niyamgiri hills** — is one of the most famous mining-vs-environment battles India has seen. We will come back to it in the Apply section.

## Non-Metallic Minerals — The Quiet Backbone

### Mica

Mica is a mineral made up of a series of plates or leaves that can split easily into very thin sheets. These sheets can be so thin that a thousand of them stacked together make up just a few centimetres of mica. Mica is **transparent**, **resistant to high voltage**, **has low power loss** — making it one of the most indispensable minerals in the electrical and electronics industry.

The major mica-producing belts are:

- The **Chota Nagpur plateau** — especially the **Koderma–Gaya–Hazaribagh belt** in Jharkhand, which is the leading producer.
- The **Ajmer area** in Rajasthan.
- The **Nellore mica belt** in Andhra Pradesh, which produces the best quality mica in the world.

Indian mica goes into laptop chips, military electronics, and high-voltage insulators around the world. The dark side of the story is that much of the Jharkhand mica is mined by **children in informal pits**, with no safety equipment and almost no wages. The next time you see a phone advertisement, remember the small hands that may have dug for the shine.

### Limestone

Limestone is found in association with rocks composed of calcium carbonates or calcium-magnesium carbonates. It is the **basic raw material for the cement industry** and is also essential for **smelting iron ore in the blast furnace**. India produces large amounts of limestone — Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Andhra Pradesh, and Gujarat are among the leading producers. Every bag of cement in your house began as a slab of limestone in one of these states.

### Salt

Common salt — sodium chloride — comes to us from two main sources: **sea water** (evaporation in salt pans along the Gujarat, Maharashtra, and Tamil Nadu coasts) and **rock salt** (mined in small quantities in Mandi, Himachal Pradesh). India is one of the world's leading salt-producing countries; Gujarat alone produces roughly three-quarters of India's salt. The Little Rann of Kachchh — a vast salt desert — turns into the world's busiest salt pan in the dry months, with the Agariya community working the brine fields in punishing heat.

## Conservation — Because Minerals Don't Grow Back

Minerals are **finite, non-renewable resources**. The geological processes that create them take millions of years; the rate at which we extract them is thousands of times faster than the rate of replenishment. Worse, the **richest ores are usually used up first**, leaving us to mine lower-grade ores that are more expensive and more polluting to process.

This raises a hard question: if our great-grandchildren are to have any iron ore left, what should we do today?

A few principles guide mineral conservation:

- **Use minerals in a planned and sustainable manner**. Industries must measure consumption and target reductions.
- **Recycle scrap metal**. Roughly **two-thirds of the world's aluminium is now produced from recycled scrap** — at a tiny fraction of the energy cost of mining new bauxite. India is far behind on this.
- **Substitute with cheaper or more abundant materials** wherever possible — for example, fibre-reinforced plastic where iron and steel are not strictly needed.
- **Use better mining technology** that reduces wastage. The mine wastage in many Indian iron ore mines is still around 60 per cent — a number that would horrify a Norwegian or Korean engineer.
- **Mind the environment** while we are at it. Mining pollutes water, contaminates soil, removes forest cover, and displaces tribal communities. Conservation that ignores ecology is no conservation at all.

## Energy Resources — The Engine of Modern Life

Without energy, no factory runs, no truck moves, no fridge cools. Energy resources are split into two big families:

- **Conventional sources** — firewood, cattle dung cake, coal, petroleum, natural gas, hydroelectricity.
- **Non-conventional sources** — solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, biogas, and atomic energy.

India is the **third-largest consumer of energy in the world**, after China and the United States. We still depend on conventional sources for most of our electricity. The shift to non-conventional sources is happening — but slowly.

## Coal — India's Workhorse

Coal is by far the **most abundantly available fossil fuel in India**. Roughly **55 per cent of India's commercial energy** still comes from coal. Coal is used in power generation, in supplying energy to industry as well as for domestic needs, and as a feedstock in metallurgical industries.

In India, coal occurs in rock sequences of two main geological ages — **Gondwana** (about 200 million years old) and **tertiary** (about 55 million years old). Coal is classified by its carbon content:

- **Anthracite** — the highest quality, with very high carbon content. Found in very small quantities in Jammu & Kashmir.
- **Bituminous** — the most popular type for commercial use; used for generating electricity and metallurgical coke. The Jharia coalfields are famous for **metallurgical coal** of this kind.
- **Lignite** — a low-grade, brown coal with high moisture content. **Neyveli in Tamil Nadu** is the main lignite producer and runs a thermal power station on it.
- **Peat** — the lowest-grade coal, with low carbon and high moisture. Largely a curiosity in India.

The major coal-bearing regions are:

- **Damodar valley** — the heart of Indian coal mining. The **Jharia, Raniganj, and Bokaro** coalfields here produce the country's best metallurgical coal.
- **Godavari, Mahanadi, Son, and Wardha valleys** — also containing significant Gondwana coal reserves.
- **Talcher in Odisha and Korba in Chhattisgarh** — both giant open-cast operations.
- **Singrauli (MP), Karanpura (Jharkhand), Singareni (Telangana)** — important regional fields.
- Tertiary coal in **Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, Meghalaya, and Nagaland** — much smaller quantities, mostly lignite.

When you see a thermal power plant from a train window in central or eastern India, you are almost always looking at a coal-fired plant — its cooling towers steaming day and night.

## Petroleum — The Black Gold That Refused to Stay Underground

Petroleum is India's **second most important source of energy** after coal. It also supplies the raw material for an entire family of products — synthetic textiles, fertilisers, plastics, lubricants, and innumerable chemicals — through the **petrochemical industry**. Petroleum refineries act as nodal industries around which large industrial complexes grow.

Most petroleum occurrences in India are associated with **anticlines and fault traps in the rock formations of the tertiary age**. About 63 per cent of India's petroleum comes from offshore fields, mostly from the **Mumbai High** complex in the Arabian Sea — one of India's largest oil fields. Other significant on-shore producing fields include:

- **Ankleshwar and Kalol** in Gujarat.
- **Digboi, Naharkatiya, and Moran-Hugrijan** in Assam — Digboi being the oldest oil-producing site in India (since the 1890s).
- The **Krishna-Godavari basin** in Andhra Pradesh — a newer producer.

India is still a **net importer of petroleum**. We meet only about a fifth of our oil demand from domestic sources. The rest comes from the Middle East, Russia, and increasingly from the United States. Every time the global oil price rises, the Indian rupee feels the squeeze at the petrol pump.

## Natural Gas — Cleaner Cousin of Coal

**Natural gas** is found alongside or near petroleum deposits and is now considered an environment-friendlier fuel. It is the cleanest of the fossil fuels — burning natural gas releases roughly half the carbon dioxide that burning coal does, and very little particulate matter.

India's major natural gas reserves are concentrated in:

- The **Krishna-Godavari basin** (offshore, off the coast of Andhra Pradesh).
- The **Mumbai High** and adjoining offshore fields in the western coast.
- **Gulf of Khambhat** in Gujarat.
- The **Andaman and Nicobar Islands**.

The **Hazira–Vijaipur–Jagdishpur (HVJ) pipeline** is the country's energy spine — a 1,750-km cross-country gas pipeline that links the western fields to the fertiliser, power, and industrial complexes of central India. Compressed Natural Gas (CNG) is now used in city buses, taxis, and three-wheelers across Delhi, Mumbai, and many other Indian cities to reduce urban air pollution.

## Hydroelectricity — Falling Water as Spinning Turbines

**Hydroelectricity** is one of the oldest non-conventional… actually one of the oldest *conventional* electricity sources. Multi-purpose river valley projects like the **Bhakra Nangal, Damodar Valley, Hirakud, and Tehri** dams generate hydroelectricity along with providing irrigation, flood control, and drinking water. India is well-endowed with hydro potential; northern Himalayan rivers and the peninsular rivers alike turn turbines.

But large dams come with large costs — displacement of communities, drowning of forests and farmland, and disruption of river ecosystems. The Narmada Bachao Andolan, led by Medha Patkar, raised exactly this question for the Sardar Sarovar Dam in Gujarat. Today, India increasingly looks at **small and run-of-the-river hydro projects** that have a lighter ecological footprint.

## Non-Conventional Sources — Where the Future Lives

### Solar Energy

India lies in the **tropical belt** and receives over **300 sunny days a year** across most of its territory. The potential is enormous. **Photovoltaic (PV) technology** converts sunlight directly into electricity. **Solar thermal** plants use sunlight to heat fluids that drive turbines. The Pavagada Solar Park in Karnataka, Bhadla Solar Park in Rajasthan, and Kurnool Ultra Mega Solar Park in Andhra Pradesh are among the largest solar installations in the world. The government's **International Solar Alliance**, headquartered in Gurugram, brings together 100+ countries to scale up solar.

Solar is also being used at the household scale — rooftop panels, solar water heaters, solar cookers, and solar lanterns. In many off-grid villages of Rajasthan and Ladakh, a small solar panel is the only source of light after sunset.

### Wind Power

India is now the **fourth-largest wind power producer in the world**. The largest wind farm cluster is located in **Tamil Nadu**, from Nagercoil to Madurai. Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Gujarat, Kerala, and Maharashtra are also major contributors. If you have driven through the Western Ghats or along the Coromandel coast, you will have seen the slow turn of giant white turbines — quiet, clean, infinite.

### Biogas

Shrubs, farm waste, animal and human waste are decomposed in biogas plants to produce gas — a mixture of methane and carbon dioxide — for domestic consumption in rural areas. **Cattle dung is used in most rural biogas plants** in India, where they are popularly known as **gobar gas plants**. They give the farmer **two benefits at once** — clean cooking fuel and improved-quality manure. Biogas is also one of the most efficient ways to use cattle dung — burning cow-dung cakes wastes about 70 per cent of the energy.

### Nuclear (Atomic) Energy

Atomic energy is obtained by altering the structure of atoms — nuclear fission. The heat thus generated is used to produce electricity. **Uranium and thorium** are the key minerals. Uranium deposits are found in the Singhbhum district of Jharkhand and in the Rajasthan thrust belt; thorium is found in the sands of Kerala (Monazite sands of the Kerala coast contain rich thorium reserves). India's nuclear power stations — **Kalpakkam (Tamil Nadu), Tarapur (Maharashtra), Rawatbhata (Rajasthan), Narora (UP), Kakrapar (Gujarat), and Kaiga (Karnataka)** — together generate about 3 per cent of total power. Nuclear is small but strategic.

### Geothermal Energy

Where the inside of the earth is unusually hot near the surface, the heat can be used to generate electricity. India has two experimental geothermal projects — one in the **Parvati valley near Manikaran in Himachal Pradesh** and one in the **Puga valley in Ladakh**. The hot springs of Manikaran have been a Sikh and Hindu pilgrimage site for centuries; the same hot water now drives experimental turbines.

### Tidal Energy

Oceanic tides can be used to generate electricity. Floodgate dams are built across the inlets of a bay or estuary. During high tide, water flows into the inlet; when the tide falls, the gates are closed and water flows out through a turbine. **The Gulf of Kachchh in Gujarat** provides ideal conditions for utilising tidal energy. The Sundarbans in West Bengal and the Gulf of Khambhat are other potential sites.

## Conservation of Energy Resources — Switching Off, Switching Wisely

Energy is the basic requirement for economic development. Energy resources, like minerals, are finite. The lifestyle of the average urban Indian uses far more energy than that of the average rural Indian — and far less than that of the average American. Where will we sit on that ladder ten years from now?

Some practical steps:

- **Use public transport** instead of individual vehicles. One bus full of people uses far less energy per person than the same people in 40 cars.
- **Switch off electrical devices** when not in use. A simple ceiling fan left running in an empty room for a single day uses about a kilowatt-hour.
- **Use power-saving devices** — LED bulbs, BEE 5-star appliances, and proper insulation.
- **Use non-conventional sources of energy** at home — solar water heaters, rooftop solar panels, solar cookers in summer.

These are not lifestyle suggestions for a poster on the wall. They are the small daily decisions that, multiplied across a billion Indians, decide whether 2050 is a comfortable year or a desperate one.

## What This Chapter Really Says

Behind all the maps and statistics, this chapter says three things.

**One — minerals are not the same as wealth.** A district can sit on top of a billion tonnes of iron ore and still be poor. What turns minerals into wealth is industry, infrastructure, schools, hospitals, and honest governance. Without those, the ore goes out by train and the poverty stays put.

**Two — the cheapest energy is the energy we don't use.** Conservation buys us more time than any new oil field.

**Three — the future is going to be solar, wind, and a little nuclear.** Coal will still be around for a while, but it cannot grow. The kid in Rajasthan whose grandfather walked miles for kerosene now has a solar panel charging a phone on the rooftop. That is the direction of travel. The question is how fast we get there.

## Apply What You Have Learned

Minerals and energy are not abstract topics. They sit inside your phone, your fan, your fridge, your bus, your scooter, and the cement of your school wall. Three short stories to ground this chapter in life today.

### Where you see this in real life

**Story 1 — The Karnataka Iron Ore Scandal.** Between 2006 and 2010, illegal mining tore through the iron ore belts of Ballari, Tumakuru, and Chitradurga in Karnataka. Trucks moved by night without permits; forest cover was bulldozed; rivers turned red. A 2011 report by the state Lokayukta estimated the loss to the public exchequer at roughly Rs 16,000 crore. The Supreme Court intervened, banning iron ore mining in Karnataka in 2011. The ban was lifted only in stages, with strict quotas. The Ballari case is now studied in law schools worldwide as a textbook example of how unchecked mining can capture a state.

**Story 2 — Pavagada Solar Park.** Spread over 13,000 acres of barren, drought-prone land in Tumakuru district of Karnataka, the **Pavagada (Shakti Sthala) solar park** generates 2,050 MW — making it one of the largest single-site solar parks in the world. Local farmers, instead of being displaced, lease their land to the government on a 28-year revenue-sharing basis. Many of them now earn more from leasing their drought-prone plots than they ever did from cultivating them. Pavagada is one model of how renewable energy can be made fair to local communities.

**Story 3 — Niyamgiri and the Dongria Kondh.** The Niyamgiri hills in Odisha hold an estimated 78 million tonnes of bauxite. In 2003, Vedanta Resources proposed a refinery at the foot of the hills and a bauxite mine on top. For the **Dongria Kondh** tribe, Niyamgiri is not a hill — it is **Niyam Raja**, their god. After a decade of resistance, the Supreme Court in 2013 ruled that the village councils — the gram sabhas — of the affected villages had to vote on the project. All 12 villages voted **no**. The mine has not opened to this day. Niyamgiri became a global symbol of indigenous resistance to mining.

### Try it at home

Pick **any one** of the following and spend a weekend on it:

- **Audit your home electricity for one month.** Note the wattage of every major appliance (look at the rating sticker on the back). Estimate hours of daily use. Multiply: watts × hours ÷ 1000 = kWh per day. Add it up. Compare with the figure on your electricity bill. Which appliance is the biggest energy guzzler? *(Hint: it is probably the air conditioner, geyser, or refrigerator.)*
- **Trace the minerals in 5 household items.** Pick five objects — a steel spoon, a copper wire, an aluminium foil, a cement wall, a salt jar. For each, write the mineral inside, where in India it is mined, and how it travelled to your home.
- **Spot 3 renewable energy systems in your area.** Walk through your neighbourhood and find three examples — a solar water heater, a rooftop PV panel, a biogas plant, a wind turbine on the horizon, a solar street light. Sketch them and write one sentence on each.
