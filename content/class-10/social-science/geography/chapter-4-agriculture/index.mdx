## What This Chapter Is Really About

If you stand in any Indian city for a day, you will see glass towers, metro lines, food-delivery riders, software offices. It is easy to forget that **roughly half of all working Indians still earn their living from the soil**. Agriculture is the oldest job in the country, the most argued-about, and arguably the most political. Every chapatti, every plate of rice, every glass of milk, every cotton shirt, every cup of chai begins as a decision a farmer made about which crop to plant and when.

This chapter looks at Indian farming with three honest questions in mind. **First, what kinds of farming exist in India and why are they so different from one region to another?** A farmer in Nagaland clears a hillside with fire; a farmer in Punjab leases a tractor; a farmer in Kerala manages a rubber estate that ships latex to Europe. They are all "Indian agriculture" — but their worlds are almost unrelated.

**Second, what did the Green Revolution of the 1960s and 70s actually do?** It is one of those moments that everyone praises in textbooks and many people quarrel about in real life. We will look at both sides.

**Third, what is happening to the Indian farmer now, in the age of globalisation, WTO rules and shrinking government support?** Why are farmers protesting? Why are some taking their own lives? Why does the country need a new conversation about food, fairness and the soil itself?

The story is not all gloom. India today produces more food than it can eat. We export rice, sugar, spices, cotton, and tea. But the gap between the country's food and the farmer's earnings is one of the strangest puzzles of modern India — and this chapter is where you begin to make sense of it.

## Types of Farming in India

Indian agriculture is not one thing. Geographers usually sort it into four broad families: **primitive subsistence farming, intensive subsistence farming, commercial farming, and plantation agriculture**. Each one belongs to a particular landscape, a particular history, and a particular kind of farmer.

### Primitive Subsistence Farming

In the hills of the north-east and small pockets of central India, you can still find a very old style of farming. A patch of forest is cleared, the cut vegetation is burnt, and seed is dropped into the warm ash-rich soil. The family grows just enough to eat. When the soil gets tired after two or three seasons, the family moves on and cuts a fresh patch of forest. The old plot is left to nature, which will slowly rebuild its fertility.

This is called **"slash and burn" or shifting cultivation**, and it has a different name in almost every region. In Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Nagaland, it is **jhumming**. In Manipur it is **pamlou**, in Bastar it is **dipa**. Madhya Pradesh calls it **bewar** or **dahiya**; Andhra Pradesh calls it **podu** or **penda**; Odisha calls it **pama dabi**, **koman** or **bringa**. The Western Ghats call it **kumari**; Rajasthan calls it **valre** or **waltre**; the Himalayan belt calls it **khil**; Jharkhand calls it **kuruwa**. The world calls it many things too — **milpa** in Mexico, **conuco** in Venezuela, **roca** in Brazil, **ladang** in Indonesia, **ray** in Vietnam.

The tools are simple — hoes, **daos** (a kind of machete), and digging sticks. The labour is the whole family. The yields are low because no fertiliser, no improved seed, no irrigation pipe ever touches this field. As long as forests were plentiful and people were few, the system worked beautifully — it gave the soil decades to recover before being cleared again. But population pressure has squeezed the cycle down to two or three years in many places, and now soils never really recover. Jhum cultivation is, slowly, becoming a story of the past.

### Intensive Subsistence Farming

Walk through the rice fields of Bihar, the wheat-and-mustard plots of western Uttar Pradesh, the densely packed deltas of Bengal — and you are in the world of **intensive subsistence farming**. The plots are small, sometimes shockingly so (less than a hectare per family is common), but the soil is squeezed for every grain it can give. Farmers use **high doses of fertilisers, irrigated water from canals or tubewells, improved seeds**, and a great deal of family labour.

Why is it so intensive? Because the **right of inheritance** divides a father's land among his sons in every generation. After three or four generations, what was once a respectable family farm becomes a patchwork of postage-stamp plots. With nowhere else to go and no alternative jobs to take, the farmer has only one option — squeeze more output from the same land. India's most populous farming regions — the Ganga plains, coastal deltas, the wet rice belts — all run on this logic.

### Commercial Farming

In the third type, the field is no longer about feeding the family. The whole crop is grown to be **sold in the market**. The inputs are modern and serious — **HYV (high-yielding variety) seeds, chemical fertilisers, pesticides, insecticides, machine ploughing, tubewell irrigation**. The aim is high productivity per acre.

Whether a crop is "commercial" or "subsistence" depends partly on the region. **Rice is a commercial crop in Punjab and Haryana** — most of it leaves the farm and goes into the government's procurement chain or the open market. But **rice in Odisha** is largely a subsistence crop — most of it stays in the village and the family pot. The same plant, two completely different economic stories.

### Plantation Agriculture

The fourth type is also commercial, but with a very particular look. **A single crop is grown on a very large area** — tea estates in Assam and Darjeeling, coffee estates in the Nilgiris and Karnataka, rubber estates in Kerala, sugarcane in western UP. Plantations sit at the **interface of agriculture and industry**: the leaf or cane that comes off the field is processed in factories nearby and turned into a packaged product for the world market.

Plantations need three things in heavy doses — **capital, migrant labour, and excellent transport**. Without good roads and rail to move the produce to the port, a tea garden is just a hillside of bushes. India's important plantation crops include **tea (Assam, North Bengal, Kerala, Tamil Nadu), coffee (Karnataka mainly), rubber (Kerala), sugarcane, and bananas**.

## Cropping Seasons: Kharif, Rabi, Zaid

Indian farming runs on three calendars, not one. Each follows the monsoon.

The **kharif season** is the monsoon crop. Seeds go into the ground with the first rains around **June**, and harvest comes in **September–October**. The kharif basket is the wet-season basket — **paddy (rice), maize, jowar, bajra, tur (arhar), moong, urad, cotton, jute, groundnut, soyabean**. Festivals like **Onam in Kerala** are tied to this harvest — the new rice arrives in time for the feast.

The **rabi season** is the cool-weather crop. Seeds are sown after the monsoon ends, between **October and December**, and the harvest comes in **April to June**, just before the next monsoon arrives. Rabi crops live on winter rains and irrigation. The basket includes **wheat, barley, peas, gram, mustard**. The northern states — **Punjab, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Uttar Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir** — are the kingdoms of rabi, helped by the gentle winter rain brought by **western temperate cyclones** sweeping in from the Mediterranean. **Baisakhi**, the spring festival of Punjab, is the song of the rabi wheat harvest.

Between the rabi harvest and the next kharif sowing, there is a short, hot **zaid season** from **April to June**. This is when farmers with irrigation grow quick crops — **watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber, vegetables, and fodder**. **Sugarcane sits a little outside this neat calendar** because it takes almost a year to mature.

In states like **Assam, West Bengal, and Odisha**, the warm wet climate allows farmers to grow **three crops of paddy in a year** — Aus, Aman, and Boro. India's farming calendar is far more crowded than the textbook box suggests.

## Major Crops: A Tour Across the Country

### Rice — the Staple of Half a Billion

**Rice is the staple food of most Indians**, and India is the second-largest producer in the world after China. It needs **temperatures above 25°C, high humidity, and at least 100 cm of annual rainfall**. The big rice-growing belts are **Assam, West Bengal, the coastal regions of Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, the Konkan coast of Maharashtra, eastern UP and Bihar**. Thanks to canals and tubewells, rice now also grows in much drier regions — **Punjab, Haryana, western UP, and parts of Rajasthan** — though many ecologists worry about how long this can continue. South Indian festivals like **Pongal** in Tamil Nadu literally cook the year's first rice in earthen pots as a thanksgiving.

### Wheat — the King of the North

Wheat is India's second-most-important cereal and the staple of the north. It is a **rabi crop** that wants a cool growing season and bright sunshine when the grain is ripening. Annual rainfall of **50–75 cm**, spread evenly, suits it well. There are two great wheat zones — the **Ganga-Satluj plains in the north-west** and the **black soil region of the Deccan**. The leading producers are **Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar, and Rajasthan**. The fields of Punjab in March, golden and rippling, are one of the great sights of Indian agriculture.

### Millets — Coarse Grains, Mighty Nutrition

We call them "coarse grains" — but **jowar, bajra and ragi** are nutritional powerhouses. **Ragi** is exceptionally rich in iron, calcium, micro-nutrients and roughage. **Jowar** is India's third-most-important food crop by area, mostly rain-fed and grown in **Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh**. **Bajra** thrives on sandy and shallow black soils — its leaders are **Rajasthan, UP, Maharashtra, Gujarat and Haryana**. **Ragi** belongs to the dry regions and grows well on red, black, sandy and loamy soils, with **Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Sikkim, Jharkhand and Arunachal Pradesh** as the main producers.

For decades, urban India ignored millets. The government's recent push to revive them — **bajra rotis, ragi dosas, jowar bhakri** showing up in middle-class kitchens — is partly a story about climate (millets need very little water) and partly a story about nutrition.

### Maize and Pulses

**Maize** doubles up as food and fodder, likes temperatures of 21–27°C, and grows on old alluvial soil. Big producers are **Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana**. In Bihar, it is even grown as a rabi crop with the help of irrigation.

**Pulses** are India's vegetarian protein, and **India is the world's largest producer and largest consumer of pulses**. The major ones are **tur (arhar), urad, moong, masur, peas and gram**. Pulses are tough little crops — they need little moisture, survive in dry conditions, and (except for arhar) **fix nitrogen from the air**, leaving the soil healthier than they found it. Farmers grow them in rotation with cereals for exactly this reason. The big producers are **Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Maharashtra, UP and Karnataka**.

### Sugarcane — The Cane that Makes Festivals

Sugarcane is a tropical and sub-tropical crop. It loves **hot, humid weather (21–27°C) and 75–100 cm of rain**, with irrigation when rain falls short. It is the main source of **sugar, gur (jaggery), khandsari and molasses**. India is the world's second-largest sugarcane producer after Brazil. The major states are **Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Bihar, Punjab and Haryana**. Pongal in Tamil Nadu, Lohri in Punjab — much of the joy of these January festivals comes from this single tall green plant.

### Oilseeds

About **12% of India's cropped area** is under oilseeds. The list is long — **groundnut, mustard, coconut, sesamum (til), soyabean, castor seeds, cotton seeds, linseed, sunflower**. Most are edible cooking oils, but they also feed industries that make soap, cosmetics, and ointments. **Groundnut alone accounts for nearly half of major oilseed output**; **Gujarat** is the largest producer, with **Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu** close behind. Linseed and mustard are rabi crops; sesamum is kharif in the north and rabi in the south; castor seed grows both seasons. In 2020, India was the second-largest groundnut producer in the world after China.

### Tea — The Daily Cup

**Tea cultivation is plantation farming.** The British introduced it; today most plantations are Indian-owned. The plant likes **tropical and sub-tropical climates, deep, fertile, well-drained soils rich in humus, and a warm-moist-frost-free year**. Frequent evenly distributed showers keep the leaves young and tender. The processing must happen **inside the tea garden itself** to keep the leaves fresh, which is why tea estates are little industrial worlds.

The big tea states are **Assam, the Darjeeling and Jalpaiguri hills of West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, and Kerala**. Add to that **Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Meghalaya, Andhra Pradesh and Tripura**. In 2020, India was the world's second-largest tea producer after China.

### Coffee — Quality with a Long History

Indian coffee enjoys a global reputation for quality. The **Arabica variety**, originally brought from Yemen, is grown mainly in the **Nilgiri hills of Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu**. The first plants supposedly came up on the **Baba Budan Hills** centuries ago. The crop is small but prized.

### Horticulture — Fruits, Vegetables, the New Money

In 2020 India was the world's **second-largest producer of fruits and vegetables** after China. We grow tropical and temperate fruits both — **mangoes from Maharashtra, Andhra, Telangana, UP and West Bengal; oranges from Nagpur and from Cherrapunjee in Meghalaya; bananas from Kerala, Mizoram, Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu; lichi and guava from UP and Bihar; pineapples from Meghalaya; grapes from Andhra, Telangana and Maharashtra; apples, pears, apricots and walnuts from Jammu & Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh; oranges from Sikkim**. Pea, cauliflower, onion, cabbage, tomato, brinjal and potato come out of Indian fields in enormous quantities.

This is also where many farmers are quietly **moving up the value chain** — a hectare of grapes earns far more than a hectare of paddy, if you have the cold chain and the buyer.

### Rubber, Cotton, Jute — The Industrial Crops

**Rubber** is an equatorial crop that India grows in the hot, humid south. It needs over 200 cm of rain and temperatures above 25°C. The big states are **Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and the Garo hills of Meghalaya**. Without rubber, no tyres, no medical gloves, no slippers — modern life leans on it more than we realise.

**Cotton** is one of the four major fibre crops (along with **jute, hemp, and natural silk**). India is believed to be the **original home of the cotton plant** and is the world's second-largest producer after China. Cotton wants **high temperatures, light rainfall or irrigation, 210 frost-free days, bright sunshine, and black cotton soil** — which is exactly what the Deccan plateau offers. The big producers are **Maharashtra, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Tamil Nadu, Punjab, Haryana, and UP**.

**Jute** is the "**golden fibre**". It grows on **well-drained fertile flood-plain soils renewed each year** and wants high temperatures during growth. The major producers are **West Bengal, Bihar, Assam, Odisha and Meghalaya**. Jute goes into gunny bags, mats, ropes, yarn, carpets and a thousand artefacts — and is enjoying a comeback as a plastic-free alternative.

## The Green Revolution and What It Cost

For most of independent India's first decade, the country was hungry. We imported wheat under the American **PL-480** scheme. We faced near-famine in 1965 and 1966. Something had to change.

In the late 1960s and 70s, the government rolled out a package the world would later call the **Green Revolution**. The package had three legs: **HYV seeds** (developed from Mexican wheat by Norman Borlaug), **heavy fertiliser and pesticide use**, and **assured irrigation**. To this were added **minimum support prices (MSP)**, government procurement through the **FCI (Food Corporation of India)**, and a network of agricultural research universities and extension officers.

The result was historic. By the 1980s, India was no longer a country that needed to beg for wheat. Punjab and Haryana, where the package landed first, became the country's grain basket. Yields per hectare doubled and tripled.

But the bill was also large, and we are still paying it. **Groundwater tables in Punjab and Haryana have crashed** — paddy is a thirsty crop and it was never meant to grow in such a dry zone. **Soil health has degraded** under decades of urea overuse. **Pesticide residues** have entered groundwater and human bodies. The package was **regionally lopsided** — it lifted Punjab while it largely bypassed Bihar, eastern UP, and most of the rain-fed Deccan. And the wheat-and-rice bias of MSP slowly **pushed out millets and pulses** from many fields, narrowing India's food basket. The Green Revolution fed the country; nobody disputes that. But the question now is how to keep feeding it without finishing the soil and the water.

## Institutional and Technological Reforms

After Independence, the government also tackled the **structure** of farming, not just the seeds. **Land reforms** were the focus of the First Five-Year Plan: **abolition of zamindari**, **collectivisation**, **consolidation of fragmented holdings**, **cooperative societies**, **cooperation among farmers**. The right of inheritance had already broken up land into tiny scattered plots — consolidation tried to stitch them back together so that machinery and irrigation could actually work.

The laws were passed; the implementation was, in many states, **lacking or lukewarm**. Zamindari was successfully abolished. Consolidation worked in some states (Punjab, Haryana) and barely happened in others (Bihar). The mismatch between paper reform and ground reality is a thread that runs through Indian agriculture even today.

The **Bhoodan-Gramdan movement**, led by **Vinoba Bhave** — Mahatma Gandhi's spiritual heir — was a remarkable parallel attempt. After Gandhiji's martyrdom, Vinoba Bhave undertook a **padyatra** across the country. At Pochampally in Telangana in 1951, landless villagers asked him for land. Bhave couldn't promise it himself, but **Shri Ram Chandra Reddy stood up and donated 80 acres** for distribution among 80 landless families. That single act became **Bhoodan** (the gift of land). Some zamindars donated whole villages — **Gramdan**. It came to be called the **Blood-less Revolution**.

In the 1980s and 90s, a more comprehensive package was tried: **crop insurance** against drought, flood, cyclone, fire and disease; **Grameen banks**; **cooperative societies and banks** offering low-interest loans; **Kisan Credit Cards (KCC)**; **Personal Accident Insurance Scheme (PAIS)**; **weather bulletins and farm-radio programmes**; and the **minimum support price (MSP)** for major crops to protect farmers from being squeezed by middlemen.

The two big revolutions — the **Green Revolution (food grains)** and the **White Revolution / Operation Flood (milk)** — also belong to this story.

## Food Security and the PDS

A country with 1.4 billion mouths cannot leave food entirely to the market. India runs one of the largest food-security systems in the world. The **Food Corporation of India (FCI)** buys wheat and rice from farmers at the **MSP**, stores it in buffer stocks, and releases it through the **Public Distribution System (PDS)** — the network of ration shops that sells subsidised food to poor families. The **National Food Security Act, 2013** entitles two-thirds of the population to subsidised grain.

There is a tension at the heart of this system. The MSP keeps the farmer's earnings predictable; the PDS keeps the poor consumer's plate full. But the prices the FCI pays the farmer are much higher than the prices it charges the ration card holder — the difference is the **food subsidy**, one of the biggest items in the Union Budget. Critics say the system is wasteful, leaky, and biased towards wheat and rice. Defenders say a country with India's poverty levels cannot do without it. Both sides have a point.

## Globalisation and the Indian Farmer

Since 1991, India has opened up. Tariffs have come down, the WTO has imposed rules, foreign agribusiness has entered the seed and pesticide industry. **Globalisation has changed Indian farming more in the last 30 years than the previous 300**.

Some changes are good. Indian rice, wheat, sugar, cotton, spices and tea now travel to world markets. Farmers near cities are growing high-value crops — grapes, exotic vegetables, flowers — for export. **FPOs (Farmer Producer Organisations)** are pooling small farmers' produce so they can sell at scale.

But the strain is real. International grain prices crashed through the 2000s, undermining MSP. Costly hybrid seeds, expensive pesticides, unstable rainfall and falling water tables have raised the cost of growing. Small farmers borrowed to buy inputs, then watched their crops fail or their prices crash. In states like **Maharashtra, Karnataka, Telangana, and Andhra Pradesh**, the result has been one of independent India's most shameful statistics: **farmer suicides**, often by drinking the very pesticide bought on credit.

The recent farmer protests, the long debates about MSP guarantees, the questions about contract farming, the slow shift back to millets and organic methods — all of this is the same conversation. **What is a fair deal for the person who grows our food?** This chapter cannot answer that. But it is the question that will shape rural India in your lifetime.

## Apply: Take This Outside the Textbook

### Stories from Real Indian Fields

**Daadi's Monsoon Song.** Sushila's grandmother grew up in a village near Patna. Every July, when the rains broke, the women of the village would walk into the paddy fields together, bending in long lines to plant seedlings, singing **ropai geet** — planting songs — to keep the rhythm. Daadi can still sing them. Sushila records one on her phone. Six minutes long, one melody, a hundred verses about rain and rice and gods. Daadi says when the planting was done, the women would walk home covered in mud, laughing. "Our backs hurt for three days," she says. "But the song was so beautiful you forgot."

**The Punjab Water Crisis.** Manjit's father has been a paddy farmer outside Ludhiana for thirty years. The Green Revolution made his family rich; today it is bleeding them dry — literally. Their tubewell, which once drew water at 40 feet, now reaches 250 feet. Last summer it ran dry mid-season and they paid 8 lakh rupees to drill deeper. Manjit, twenty-one, is studying agricultural sciences. He wants to switch his father's fields away from paddy to maize, vegetables and millets. His father agrees on paper, then panics when planting season arrives. "Paddy at least has MSP," he says. "Who will buy my bajra?"

**An FPO in Andhra.** In Anantapur district, 800 small groundnut farmers pooled themselves into a Farmer Producer Organisation in 2019. They bought a small oil expeller together, started selling **cold-pressed groundnut oil** under their own label, and now ship to retailers in Bengaluru and Hyderabad. Each farmer earns about 30% more than before. The chairperson, a woman named Lakshmi, used to be a daily-wage labourer. "Earlier the middleman set the price," she says. "Now we do."

### DIY: Three Ways to Make This Chapter Real

1. **Spend a morning at your nearest mandi or wholesale vegetable market.** Notice which crops are arriving, where the trucks are from (the state board on the truck will tell you), what the auctioneer is shouting, how the farmer's price is set. Write down five things you didn't expect.
2. **Pick five grains, dals or oils from your kitchen.** For each one, find out which state it most likely came from and which season (kharif/rabi/zaid) it grew in. Try to find this only from the packet and the internet — no asking parents.
3. **Track one week's meals against the cropping calendar.** Make a small chart: for each meal, list the main grain/pulse/vegetable/oil. Beside each, write the crop's season. By Sunday you will have a picture of how much of your week's eating comes from kharif, rabi and zaid fields — and how mixed the Indian plate really is.
