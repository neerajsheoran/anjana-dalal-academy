**Child:** Maa, why do we still have farmers when most other countries have factories doing everything?
**Mother:** Because India is not most other countries, beta. We have **1.4 billion mouths to feed every single day**, and you cannot import that much food forever. Also, "having factories" hasn't actually replaced farming anywhere. America has huge farms, just fewer farmers. We have small farms and many farmers — that's the difference. About **half of our working population** still earns from the soil. Until we have other jobs for them to move into, agriculture will stay enormous. It is not a backward thing. It is the engine that keeps the country running.

## Why Are There So Many Different Types of Farming in India?

**Child:** Then why don't they all just farm the same way? Why is one farmer using a tractor and another one burning a forest with a stick?

**Mother:** Because India is many countries inside one country. The geography, the rainfall, the soil, the history, the population pressure — all of it differs from state to state. So farming has shaped itself accordingly.

In the **north-east hills** — Nagaland, Mizoram, parts of Assam — population is thin and forests are still plenty. So families clear a small patch, burn the vegetation, plant for two or three seasons, then move on. The forest grows back. This is **jhumming**, the oldest type of farming there is. It only works when there is enough land per family. As population pressure grows, even jhumming breaks down.

In the **Gangetic plains** — Bihar, eastern UP, Bengal — the opposite is true. There are millions of farmers and very little land per family. So they push every inch hard. **Intensive subsistence farming**: small plot, heavy fertiliser, irrigation, family labour, two crops a year. The yields per acre are some of the highest in the country. But the farmer himself is poor because the plot is so small.

**Child:** What about Punjab? My friend's uncle has a tractor.

**Mother:** Punjab is **commercial farming**. There the farmer grows rice and wheat **to sell**, not to eat. HYV seeds, chemical fertiliser, tubewells, tractors, harvesters — the whole machinery package. Government buys the produce at MSP. It is closer to a business than a way of life.

And then in Kerala, in Karnataka's hills, in Assam — you see **plantation agriculture**. One single crop — tea, coffee, rubber — grown on a huge estate with migrant labour and a processing factory right there. The leaves of an Assam tea garden become tea bags in London. That is plantation farming.

So when you ask "why are they different" — they are different because the land is different and the families are different. India couldn't have just one type of farming even if it wanted to.

## What Made the Green Revolution Such a Big Deal?

**Child:** They keep saying the Green Revolution like it was a war.

**Mother:** It almost was, beta. In 1965 and 1966 India had two failed monsoons in a row. We were importing wheat from America under a programme called **PL-480**. It was humiliating — a country of farmers begging for wheat. We could see famine coming.

So the government did something dramatic. They brought in a **package** — new high-yielding wheat seeds developed by an American scientist called Norman Borlaug from Mexican varieties, lots of chemical fertiliser, tubewells for assured irrigation, and a guaranteed price (**MSP**) so the farmer wouldn't be cheated. They focused on Punjab, Haryana, and western UP — places that already had irrigation and could absorb the package.

It worked beyond their dreams. By the 1980s, India was not just self-sufficient — we had **surplus grain**. The Food Corporation of India was running out of warehouse space.

**Child:** So why does everyone now say it was bad?

**Mother:** Not bad. **Costly**. The bill is being paid now. In Punjab the **groundwater table has crashed** — paddy was never supposed to be grown in such a dry zone, but MSP made it profitable, so everyone did it. Tubewells used to draw water at 40 feet; now they go down to 250 feet. The **soil is tired** from years of urea overuse. **Pesticides** have entered the water and people's bodies — cancer rates in some Punjab villages are frighteningly high. And the revolution was **only for two crops** — rice and wheat. **Millets, pulses, oilseeds** got pushed to the margins. Now we're trying to bring millets back, fifty years later.

So when you ask was it a big deal — yes, it saved India from hunger. And yes, it also gave us new problems we are still solving. Both are true.

## What's the Truth Behind Farmer Suicides?

**Child:** Why do farmers kill themselves, Maa? I keep seeing it in the news.

**Mother:** Sit down, this one is hard.

A small farmer in Maharashtra or Karnataka or Telangana usually owns one or two acres. He cannot grow modern hybrid crops without buying **expensive seeds, fertiliser, and pesticide** every season. He doesn't have that money, so he **borrows** — from a moneylender, or from a bank, or from a microfinance company. The interest is high.

Then the monsoon fails. Or the price crashes because cotton from Brazil is cheaper that year. Or his crop gets a disease. He loses everything. Now he owes a lakh of rupees and has nothing to pay it with. The lender comes home. His wife is shamed. His children might be pulled out of school.

In that despair, some farmers drink the pesticide they bought on credit. The same chemical that was supposed to grow their crop ends their life. In some states this has been happening for **thirty years**.

**Child:** Why doesn't the government just give them money?

**Mother:** Sometimes they do — there are loan-waiver schemes, crop insurance, the **Kisan Credit Card**, the **PM-Kisan** scheme that gives 6,000 rupees a year. But these are sticking plasters. The deeper problems are structural: **landholdings are too small**, **prices are too unstable**, **MSP doesn't cover all crops**, **water is running out**, **climate is becoming unpredictable**, and **rural India has very few alternative jobs**.

You can't fix a farmer's despair by giving him another loan. You fix it by giving him a fair price, a stable market, working insurance, water that doesn't dry up, and a way out for his children if they don't want to farm. That is what the country is still working out.

## Why Do Some Crops Belong to Some States?

**Child:** Why is tea always Assam and coffee always Karnataka? Can't they swap?

**Mother:** They could try, but the plants wouldn't agree. Each crop has very specific needs.

**Tea** wants warm humid weather all year, frequent rain, deep well-drained soil rich in humus, and **no frost**. Assam and Darjeeling give it exactly that. The Nilgiri hills also work. But you can't grow tea in Delhi — too dry, too cold in winter.

**Coffee** also wants the hills but a slightly different microclimate — the Western Ghats and Nilgiris suit Arabica best. There's a famous story that the first coffee plants were brought to India by a Sufi saint called **Baba Budan** who hid the seeds in his beard on his way back from Yemen. Those hills in Karnataka are still called the Baba Budan Hills.

**Rice** needs heat and water — that's why Tamil Nadu, Kerala, the Gangetic plains, Bengal, and Assam are its homes. Wherever you have monsoon plus warmth, you have rice.

**Wheat** needs the **opposite** — cool growing weather, dry sunshine when ripening, moderate rain. Punjab, Haryana, western UP and the Deccan black soil give it that.

**Cotton** wants **black cotton soil**, hot days, 210 frost-free days, and bright sunshine. That's why the Deccan plateau — Maharashtra, Gujarat, Telangana, Karnataka — became India's cotton belt.

**Jute** wants soils that get **renewed every year by floods**. So it lives on the flood plains of West Bengal, Bihar, Assam.

So crops belong to states the way fish belong to oceans. Move them, and they sulk or die.

## How Did Globalisation Change Farming?

**Child:** What does globalisation have to do with a man growing wheat in Punjab?

**Mother:** Everything, beta. Until **1991**, Indian farming was a fairly closed system. Indian farmers grew Indian seeds, sold to Indian markets, at prices the Indian government managed. Then we opened up — the **liberalisation** of 1991, then the **WTO agreement** of 1995. Suddenly the world's prices started showing up in Indian villages.

Some of it has been good. **Spices, basmati rice, tea, coffee, cotton, sugar** now travel from Indian fields to global supermarkets. Farmers near cities are growing **grapes, exotic vegetables, flowers** for export — earning much more than paddy ever paid. Farmer Producer Organisations help small farmers pool their crop and bargain better.

But the strain is real. Foreign companies sell **hybrid seeds** that have to be bought every season — you can't save seed the old way. **Fertiliser and pesticide costs** keep rising because they're tied to global oil and chemical prices. **International grain prices crashed** in the 2000s, putting pressure on MSP. **Subsidised American and European farmers** dump their surplus into world markets, and our farmers cannot compete.

So globalisation has been a door that swings both ways. On one side, opportunity for those who can grab it. On the other side, pressure on those who can't.

## What Should I Take From This?

**Child:** This is a lot to take in, Maa.

**Mother:** I'll give you four things. Carry just these.

**One — agriculture is not an old subject. It is a current one.** Half the country still earns from the soil. Every meal you eat is somebody's working day. Treat that as sacred information.

**Two — the same crop is not the same crop everywhere.** Rice in Punjab is a business. Rice in Odisha is a family's dinner. The geography of India shapes the meaning of farming. Don't generalise.

**Three — the Green Revolution was a gift with a price tag.** It ended hunger. It also broke the groundwater and narrowed our food basket. Both are true. When you read about Indian agriculture, hold both truths together.

**Four — the farmer's price problem is not natural. It is political.** MSP, PDS, FCI procurement, crop insurance, loans, mandi systems — these are choices the country makes. They can be improved. When you grow up and vote, you'll be deciding what kind of deal we give the person who feeds you.

**Child:** Maa, when I eat my roti tonight, can I just think about who grew the wheat?

**Mother:** That would be a very good start. Most of us never do.
