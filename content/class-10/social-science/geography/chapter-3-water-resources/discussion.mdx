## How Can a Country with So Many Rivers Have Water Scarcity?

**Child:** Maa, but India has so many rivers — Ganga, Yamuna, Krishna, Kaveri. Why does the chapter say there's scarcity?

**Mother:** Beta, having rivers is not the same as having usable water at the right time and place. Think of our own kitchen. We get rice once a month from the ration shop — twenty kilos all at once. If we did not have a steel container to store it, by the second week it would be on the floor, in the rain, eaten by mice, gone. India's rainfall is like that ration. Almost three-quarters of it falls in just June, July, and August. If we cannot store it, it just flows out to the sea. The other nine months we sit looking at empty rivers.

**Child:** So the problem is storage?

**Mother:** Storage is one part. Distribution is another. Meghalaya gets 10,000 millimetres of rain, Rajasthan gets 150. Pipes cannot carry water across thousands of kilometres easily — and rivers do not flow across watersheds. And then there is the third problem — *waste*. Cities lose 30 to 50 per cent of treated water through leaking pipes. Farmers flood their fields when sprinklers and drip would do the job with half the water. Factories dump effluent into rivers, ruining what is still flowing. So even the water that *is* available, we are destroying as fast as nature gives it.

**Child:** Three problems wearing the same disguise.

**Mother:** Exactly. Scarcity in India is not nature's failure. It is timing, geography, and management — three human-shaped problems. And there is a fourth one, slower and scarier — **groundwater**. Your dadi used to draw water from a well 30 feet deep. Now most farmers in Punjab are pumping from 300 feet. In some places, 500. Once an aquifer is empty, it can take decades to refill. We are spending the savings account that thousands of years of monsoons deposited under the soil.

**Child:** Like spending grandfather's land to buy phone bills.

**Mother:** A sharper way to put it than the textbook does, beta. That is exactly what it is.

## Why Are Big Dams So Controversial?

**Child:** Mother, I'm confused. Nehru called dams "temples of modern India." Bhakra Nangal gave us the Green Revolution. So they are good — right? But then the chapter spends pages on what's wrong with them. Which is it?

**Mother:** Both. And the chapter is doing what good textbooks rarely do — telling you that a thing can be enormously useful and seriously harmful at the same time. Bhakra Nangal made Punjab green. Hirakud stopped Odisha's terrible Mahanadi floods. Nagarjuna Sagar irrigates Andhra. These are real, life-saving benefits — millions of people eat because of dam-fed canals.

**Child:** Then why do people protest?

**Mother:** Because the benefits and the costs do not fall on the same heads. The benefits flow to large farmers, cities, factories — often hundreds of kilometres downstream from the dam. The costs fall on the families whose villages got drowned. Across India since Independence, around 50 million people have been displaced by dams. About 40 per cent of them are Adivasi communities, who are only 8 per cent of the population. So Adivasis are bearing five times their share of the pain. Many never got the land or jobs that were promised. Their compensation papers got lost, their forests submerged, their ancestral shrines underwater.

**Child:** That's not fair.

**Mother:** It is not. And there are ecological costs too — dams trap the silt that used to fertilise downstream fields, they block fish migration, the rotting plants in the reservoir give off methane (a greenhouse gas), and the reservoirs themselves silt up, so even the storage capacity falls every year. Plus, when monsoon overshoots, dam managers are forced to release water suddenly — and that man-made flood downstream can be worse than what the river would have done naturally. Some of the 2018 Kerala flooding was made worse by sudden reservoir releases.

**Child:** So should we stop building dams?

**Mother:** That is not what the activists are saying either, beta. What they say is — *count the full cost*. Build smaller, more careful projects. When you must build big, count the displaced honestly, compensate them properly, leave a flow downstream for the river to live. And try alternatives first — rainwater harvesting, watershed management, drip irrigation. Many of those alternatives produce more water per rupee, with no displacement at all.

**Child:** That sounds like wisdom, not protest.

**Mother:** That is exactly the difference between the Narmada Bachao Andolan and a riot. The activists were not against development. They were asking for honest accounting.

## How Did Our Ancestors Conserve Water Without Tech?

**Child:** Maa, this Sringaverapura thing in the chapter shocked me. A flood-water harvesting system from 1st century BCE? With desilting tanks? That sounds modern.

**Mother:** It *is* modern. We just stopped doing it for a few centuries and forgot. The ancients knew exactly what they were doing because they had no choice — no diesel pumps, no concrete dams, no government tankers. If they did not catch water and store it, they died. So they invented brilliantly.

**Child:** Like what?

**Mother:** Think of Karikalan Chola's Kallanai — the Grand Anicut — on the Kaveri river. He built it in roughly the 2nd century CE. **It is still working two thousand years later.** Tamil Nadu farmers still get water through it. Compare that to a modern dam that needs major repair after 50 years and silts up in a hundred. The Cholas knew something about durable engineering. The whole Chola country had a *cascade* of irrigation tanks — water that overflowed one tank flowed downhill into the next, then the next, then the next. Nothing was wasted.

**Child:** And Rajasthan?

**Mother:** In Rajasthan they went the other way — *underground*. Every traditional Rajasthani house has a **kund** in the courtyard. The rain hits the roof, runs into sloped channels, drains into this underground tank lined with lime plaster, and stays cool, clean, and bacteria-free for a year. Some big havelis had kunds large enough to last through three drought years. They had **johads** — small earthen check-dams across seasonal streams — to make the runoff stop, sink, and recharge the local wells. In the 1990s, when Rajendra Singh started reviving johads in Alwar district, dry rivers like the Arvari began *flowing year-round again*. The water had been there. The structures to slow and catch it had been broken.

**Child:** And Meghalaya?

**Mother:** Meghalaya is the magic one. The Khasi and Jaintia tribes for 200 years have run a **bamboo-pipe drip irrigation** system. They tap a spring high on the hill, split bamboo trunks into pipes, and run a network down the slope. The pipes narrow as they go, and the water arrives at each areca nut or betel plant as drops, not floods. Gravity does all the work. No pumps, no electricity, no chemicals. When the Israelis "invented" drip irrigation in the 1960s, the Khasis politely watched and said — yes, we have been doing this for two centuries.

**Child:** So the ancestors were not primitive at all.

**Mother:** They were brilliant. They worked with the local landscape instead of bulldozing it. What we lost is not the knowledge — it is in books, in living villages, in the structures themselves. What we lost is the *social arrangement* that maintained them. The Tamil custom of **kudimaramat** required every village to clean its tank once a year. Once that custom broke, the tanks silted up. Now we are using MGNREGA labour to desilt them again. We are paying ourselves to do what villagers used to do for themselves.

**Child:** That's sad.

**Mother:** It is. But it is also fixable. Knowledge survived. We just have to choose to use it.

## Why Did Medha Patkar's Narmada Bachao Andolan Matter?

**Child:** Maa, why is Medha Patkar treated almost like a national hero in this chapter? She didn't stop the dam — Sardar Sarovar was built. The Supreme Court allowed it. The dam was inaugurated in 2017. So what did she actually achieve?

**Mother:** A question I hear a lot, beta. And the honest answer is — she may not have stopped the dam, but she changed forever the way India *talks about* dams, displacement, and development.

**Child:** How?

**Mother:** Before Patkar, when a government announced a dam, the project was presented purely as benefits — so many megawatts of power, so many hectares irrigated, so many tonnes of food. The costs were buried in small print or denied altogether. Displaced families were given a small token compensation and forgotten. Most of them, especially Adivasis, were not even *counted*. They had no land titles, lived in collective village holdings, and the government's resettlement lists simply skipped them.

**Child:** So they got nothing?

**Mother:** Often nothing. Patkar walked into the Narmada valley in 1985 and found families who didn't even know their homes were going to be drowned. She organised them — *dharnas*, *padayatras*, the *jal samarpan* protest where villagers stood in their own houses as the reservoir water rose around their necks. She refused to leave the valley. She went to jail many times. She fasted. And she pulled international attention — the World Bank, which had funded part of the project, withdrew in 1993 after a damning review.

**Child:** That's huge.

**Mother:** It was. And even though the Supreme Court in 2000 permitted construction to continue, the court placed conditions on resettlement — and every Environmental Impact Assessment in India since then must include a social cost analysis. No project today can pretend that the displaced do not exist. The phrase "development for whom?" became part of every educated Indian's vocabulary because of her.

**Child:** So she changed the conversation even if she didn't change the dam.

**Mother:** Yes. And sometimes that is the bigger victory. She won the Right Livelihood Award in 1991 — sometimes called the "alternative Nobel." Her real achievement is that every future dam in India will have to face an organised, informed civil society. The age of building dams in secret on the heads of unrepresented people is over. That is what she gave us — not a stopped dam, but a permanently widened democracy.

**Child:** That's a beautiful way to lose a battle.

**Mother:** A wise way, beta. She lost the dam but won the country's conscience.

## Can Rainwater Harvesting Really Solve Our Problems?

**Child:** Maa, this rooftop rainwater thing — is it actually serious or is it just a feel-good thing in textbooks? Can a few buckets on the roof really make a difference compared to a dam that holds billions of litres?

**Mother:** Do the maths with me, beta. Suppose our terrace is 100 square metres. Suppose our city gets 900 millimetres of rain a year, which is average for India. The water that lands on our roof in one year is: 100 square metres × 0.9 metres = **90 cubic metres = 90,000 litres**.

**Child:** Ninety thousand litres? From our roof alone?

**Mother:** From our roof alone. Now imagine every roof in Delhi, Bengaluru, Mumbai, Chennai, Pune, Hyderabad. There are crores of roofs. The total potential is enormous — more than what many dam reservoirs hold. Tamil Nadu has been doing this since 2003 — they made rooftop rainwater harvesting mandatory in every new building. Chennai's groundwater table actually rose. Wells that had been abandoned started yielding again. The whole state has retrofitted about 4.7 million structures.

**Child:** So it really does work at scale?

**Mother:** It does. And look at Gendathur village in Mysuru district, Karnataka. About 200 households, average annual rainfall around 1,000 mm, each home with a roof of 20 square metres. Each household harvests about 50,000 litres a year — enough to cover most of its drinking and cooking needs. The village wells, which used to dry up in April, now hold water year-round.

**Child:** So why don't we just do it on every roof?

**Mother:** Three reasons, beta. First, **rules**. Tamil Nadu *made it mandatory*. Other states have rules only for big buildings, or no rules at all. Second, **maintenance**. A harvesting system needs cleaning before every monsoon — clearing the gutters, checking the first-flush diverter, inspecting the tank. If people install it once and forget, the system fails. Third, **education**. Most urban Indians don't know their roof could harvest 50,000–100,000 litres a year. So they accept the tanker truck as normal.

**Child:** So the technology is ready, but the habit isn't.

**Mother:** Precisely. Rainwater harvesting will not replace every dam — irrigation for crores of hectares still needs canal water and groundwater. But it can solve a huge chunk of urban household demand, ease pressure on dams and aquifers, and recharge the groundwater under our cities so we are not chasing it deeper every year. It is one of the rare problems in modern India where the solution is cheap, ancient, decentralised, and democratic. Every family can do it without waiting for a government scheme.

**Child:** That's empowering.

**Mother:** That is the part textbooks should be shouting about, beta. Water is the one resource crisis that does not need crores of rupees from above to fix. Our roofs are the answer.

## What Should Our Family Start Doing Today?

**Child:** Maa, OK — I'm convinced. But what does that mean for *us*, now, this week? I don't run the city. We can't build a dam. So what's our share?

**Mother:** That is the most important question in the chapter, beta. There are three levels of action.

**First level — stop the leaks.** Walk around the house today and check every tap, every flush, every pipe joint. A single leaking tap dripping once a second wastes around 30 litres in a day, about 10,000 litres in a year. Now multiply that by every leaky tap in India. Replace washers, tighten joints, get the plumber if needed. This costs almost nothing and is the cheapest water you will ever "save."

**Child:** OK, I can do that. What else?

**Mother:** **Second level — change the habits.** Bucket and mug instead of shower for most days. A shower can use 50 litres in five minutes; a bucket bath uses 15. Use the washing machine and dishwasher on full loads, not half. Use the leftover water from washing dal and rice to water plants. If we have an RO purifier, catch the reject water (which is 60–80 per cent of what goes in!) in a bucket and use it for floor washing, plant watering, and the toilet tank. Switch to a dual-flush toilet when we next replace the cistern. Water plants in the evening, not the morning, so less evaporates.

**Child:** And the third level?

**Mother:** **Third level — install rainwater harvesting.** Talk to your father about adding a small rooftop system. The cost for a small home is just a few thousand rupees and pays itself back in tanker savings within a year. Even if we can't store it all, we can dig a recharge pit — let the water sink into the ground under our house and refresh the local groundwater. Every borewell in the neighbourhood will benefit.

**Child:** And what about food?

**Mother:** Smart question, beta. The water we drink is tiny compared to the water inside our food. Producing one kilo of polished rice can take 3,000 to 5,000 litres of water. One kilo of beef takes around 15,000 litres. Local seasonal vegetables and fruits use much less. So when we eat seasonal *bhindi* in summer, local mango from Konkan, *amaranth* greens from the market — we are also drinking less of the country's water than if we eat out-of-season tomatoes flown in from Spain. This is called our **water footprint**.

**Child:** I had no idea food carried water inside it.

**Mother:** Most people don't, beta. That is why this chapter matters. Every meal is a water decision. Every flush is a water decision. Every shopping bag is a water decision. None of us is going to save the country alone — but if every Indian household saved 50 litres a day, the city tankers, the falling water tables, and the next dam fight would all change.

**Child:** Then let's start tonight, Maa. I'll check the taps.

**Mother:** That is exactly the spirit, beta. Big problems start to shrink when households start to count.
