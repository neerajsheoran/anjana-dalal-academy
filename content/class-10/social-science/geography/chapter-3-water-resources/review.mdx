## In One Paragraph

India receives a huge amount of annual rainfall — around 4,000 cubic kilometres — but suffers chronic water scarcity because 75 per cent of it falls in just three monsoon months, is unevenly spread across geography, and is wasted, polluted, or over-pumped. The country holds about 4 per cent of the world's freshwater but supports 18 per cent of the world's people, of which agriculture uses 80 per cent of the available supply. After Independence, India built giant **multi-purpose river projects** — Bhakra Nangal on the Sutlej, Hirakud on the Mahanadi, Damodar Valley dams, Nagarjuna Sagar on the Krishna, Sardar Sarovar on the Narmada, and Tehri on the Bhagirathi — to store water, generate hydroelectricity, control floods, and irrigate fields. Nehru called them "temples of modern India." From the 1980s, the costs became visible: tribal displacement (50 million people, 40 per cent of them Adivasi), ecological damage, lopsided benefits, reservoir siltation, and tragic resettlement failures. The **Narmada Bachao Andolan**, led by **Medha Patkar** from 1985, became the symbol of this critique. Alongside the dam debate, India has rediscovered the genius of its own traditional water systems — Rajasthan's **kund** and **johad**, Meghalaya's **bamboo-pipe drip irrigation**, Karnataka's **kere** tanks, Tamil Nadu's **Chola eris**, and the 1st century BCE Sringaverapura flood-harvesting system. Modern **rooftop rainwater harvesting** — mandatory in Tamil Nadu since 2003 and adopted in villages like Gendathur — shows that ancient techniques scaled with modern engineering can solve much of India's water crisis without further dam-building, displacement, or aquifer mining.

## Key Takeaways

- **The Indian water paradox** — A country with massive rainfall (around 4,000 cubic km a year) faces severe scarcity because 75 per cent of the rain comes in a 3-month monsoon, spread unevenly across geography, and is wasted or polluted.
- **4 per cent and 18 per cent** — India has 4 per cent of the world's freshwater and 18 per cent of its population — among the most water-stressed populations per person on earth.
- **Agriculture uses 80 per cent** of India's freshwater. Flood irrigation wastes more than half through evaporation and runoff. Drip and sprinkler systems could cut this dramatically.
- **Groundwater over-extraction** — In Punjab, Haryana, western UP, and much of peninsular India, water tables have fallen from 30 feet (a generation ago) to 300+ feet today.
- **Two kinds of scarcity** — **Quantitative** (not enough water available) and **qualitative** (water polluted by sewage, industrial effluents, arsenic, fluoride, and nitrates).
- **Multi-purpose river projects** combine irrigation, hydroelectric power generation, flood control, water supply, navigation, and fisheries from one structure — Nehru's "temples of modern India."
- **Major dams** — **Bhakra Nangal** on the Sutlej (Punjab), **Hirakud** on the Mahanadi (Odisha — longest dam at time of completion), **Damodar Valley Corporation** (modelled on the US TVA), **Sardar Sarovar** on the Narmada (Gujarat), **Tehri** on the Bhagirathi (Uttarakhand — India's tallest at 260 m), **Nagarjuna Sagar** on the Krishna.
- **Costs of big dams** — Displacement (50 million people displaced since 1947, 40 per cent Adivasi), ecological disruption (trapped silt, blocked fish migration, methane emissions), inter-state disputes, sudden-release floods, reservoir siltation, and uneven distribution of benefits.
- **Narmada Bachao Andolan** — Led by **Medha Patkar** from 1985 against the Sardar Sarovar Dam; demanded fair count, hearing, and resettlement of 200,000+ displaced (mostly Adivasi); used *jal samarpan* protests; Patkar won the Right Livelihood Award (1991). The Supreme Court permitted construction to continue in 2000; dam inaugurated 2017.
- **Tehri Dam controversy** — India's tallest dam (260 m) on the seismically active Himalayas; opposed by Sundarlal Bahuguna's hunger strikes; raises questions about earthquake safety for downstream populations.
- **Rainwater harvesting** — The ancient and increasingly modern practice of catching rain where it falls; bypasses many problems of large dams.
- **Traditional structures** — **Kund/tanka** (Rajasthan underground tank), **johad** (small earthen check-dam, revived by **Rajendra Singh** of Tarun Bharat Sangh in Alwar), **bamboo-pipe drip irrigation** (Khasi and Jaintia tribes of Meghalaya, 200 years old), **kere** (Karnataka tanks), **eri** (Chola tanks of Tamil Nadu — the Kallanai/Grand Anicut built by Karikalan Chola is still functional after 2,000 years).
- **Ancient hydraulic engineering** — Kalinga inscriptions (3rd c BCE), **Sringaverapura flood-harvesting system** near Allahabad (1st c BCE) with desilting tanks, Chola tank cascades, Bhopal lake by Raja Bhoj (11th c), Gujarat and Rajasthan stepwells.
- **Tamil Nadu rooftop harvesting law (2003)** — First Indian state to make rooftop rainwater harvesting mandatory for all new buildings; transformed Chennai groundwater recovery.
- **Gendathur village (Karnataka)** — A model rural community where rooftop rainwater harvesting in around 200 households recharged wells and ended seasonal water shortages.
- **The household level** — Fix leaks, dual-flush toilets, bucket bathing, evening watering, reuse RO reject water, eat seasonal local food; a 100 m² roof in a 900 mm rainfall area can harvest 90,000 litres a year.

## Key Terms

- **Water resources** — The portion of the planet's water that is accessible, fresh, and usable by humans for drinking, agriculture, and industry. About 2.5 per cent of the earth's total water is freshwater; only a fraction of that is easily reached.
- **Water scarcity** — A situation in which the available, clean, affordable water is less than what is needed. May be **quantitative** (not enough water) or **qualitative** (water available but polluted).
- **Multi-purpose river project** — A dam-and-reservoir scheme designed to serve several purposes at once: irrigation, hydroelectric power, flood control, water supply, navigation, and fisheries.
- **Hydroelectricity** — Electricity generated by the force of falling or running water turning turbines; a renewable energy source that depends on river flow.
- **Rainwater harvesting** — The collection and storage of rainwater at the place where it falls — on rooftops, in fields, or in storage ponds — for later use or to recharge groundwater.
- **Groundwater** — Water held in soil and rock layers (aquifers) beneath the ground surface; accessed through wells and borewells; recharged naturally by rain seeping in.
- **Kund (or tanka)** — A traditional underground rainwater storage tank in Rajasthan, dug into the courtyard of a house and fed by rooftop and compound runoff; can hold a year's drinking water for a family.
- **Johad** — A small earthen check-dam built across a gully or seasonal stream to slow runoff and let it sink into the soil, recharging local groundwater; revived in Rajasthan by Rajendra Singh's Tarun Bharat Sangh.
- **Bamboo-pipe drip irrigation** — A 200-year-old micro-irrigation system of the Khasi and Jaintia tribes of Meghalaya, using split bamboo to carry spring water down hillsides and deliver it drop by drop to plants.
- **Sardar Sarovar Dam** — The controversial dam on the Narmada in Gujarat, 138.7 m high, submerging 245 villages and displacing over 200,000 people, mostly Adivasi; focus of the Narmada Bachao Andolan.
- **Narmada Bachao Andolan** — The "Save the Narmada Movement" started in 1985, led by Medha Patkar, opposing the Sardar Sarovar Dam over inadequate resettlement of displaced communities.
- **Displacement** — The forced movement of people away from their land and homes, often by dam reservoirs, mines, or industrial projects; in India, dams alone have displaced an estimated 50 million people since 1947.
