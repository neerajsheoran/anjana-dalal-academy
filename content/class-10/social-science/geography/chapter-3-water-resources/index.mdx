## A Strange Indian Paradox

If you fly over India in July, you will not see a country that is short of water. You will see flooded fields in Bihar, brown rivers spilling across Assam, monsoon clouds smothering the Western Ghats, and Cherrapunji — a town in Meghalaya that gets nearly **eleven thousand millimetres** of rain a year — practically drowning under cloud. India receives, on average, around **4,000 cubic kilometres** of precipitation each year. That is one of the largest natural endowments of fresh water on the planet.

And yet, by April, in countless cities and villages, queues form at municipal taps. In Chennai in 2019, the city's four reservoirs ran dry and the government had to bring water in by train. In rural Rajasthan, women still walk five kilometres each morning with brass pots. In Bengaluru, apartment towers depend on private tanker lorries. The same India that floods in July goes thirsty in May.

This chapter is about that paradox — a country **rich in rainfall but poor in usable water**, and what we have done, are doing, and must do about it. You will meet engineers who built giant dams, villagers in Meghalaya who pipe spring water through bamboo, kings of the third century BCE who built irrigation tanks still in use today, and an activist named Medha Patkar who lay down in the Narmada's rising waters rather than move her home. By the end you should be able to argue both sides of the dam debate, sketch how a rooftop catches a monsoon, and look at your own family's water bill differently.

## What Counts as a Water Resource?

When geographers say **water resources**, they mean the water humans can actually reach and use — freshwater for drinking, irrigation, and industry. The earth holds an enormous volume of water, but **97 per cent of it is salty seawater**. Of the remaining 3 per cent of freshwater, most is locked up in glaciers and polar ice or buried deep underground beyond easy reach. Only a sliver — about **2.5 per cent of the planet's total water** — is freshwater, and only a fraction of that is available in rivers, lakes, and shallow groundwater for human use.

So when we speak of "running out of water," we never mean the planet is drying up. The water cycle returns every drop. What we mean is that the **accessible**, **clean**, and **affordable** portion is shrinking — being pumped faster than it is being replenished, or being polluted into uselessness.

India has roughly **4 per cent of the world's freshwater** but **18 per cent of the world's population**. That ratio tells the story. There is just less to go around per person here than almost anywhere else.

## Why a Rain-Rich Country Faces Scarcity

If India gets so much rain, why the shortages? The answer is in three words: **time, space, and waste**.

**Time.** Almost three-quarters of India's rainfall arrives in just three months — June, July, and August — during the southwest monsoon. The other nine months are largely dry. A country that needs water all year gets most of its supply in a single quarter. Without storage, the rest of it runs off to the sea.

**Space.** The rain is shockingly uneven across the map. Meghalaya gets over 10,000 mm a year. Western Rajasthan gets less than 150 mm. The east coast floods while the west coast cracks. Even within a single state, one district may be in deficit while the next is in surplus. Pipes and canals cannot fix geography quickly.

**Waste.** And here is where humans take over from nature. Cities lose 30–50 per cent of treated water through leaking pipes before it reaches a single tap. Indian agriculture, which uses about **80 per cent** of all freshwater drawn, still relies heavily on flood irrigation that loses more than half the water to evaporation and runoff. Industries discharge untreated effluents into rivers, making the water unusable downstream. Households leave taps running, leak from cisterns, and over-water gardens. The Central Water Commission estimates that if Indian agriculture simply switched to **drip and sprinkler irrigation**, the country would have a 30 per cent surplus instead of a deficit.

There is also a deeper, slower kind of scarcity: **groundwater depletion**. Across Punjab, Haryana, western UP, and much of peninsular India, farmers and city dwellers have been pumping out groundwater faster than the rain can refill it. Wells that drew water at 30 feet in your grandmother's time now go down 300 feet — and in some places, even that fails. The Central Ground Water Board lists hundreds of "over-exploited" or "critical" blocks. Once an aquifer is empty, refilling it can take decades. This is **scarcity by overuse**, and it is happening invisibly under our feet.

## The Two Faces of Scarcity: Quantity and Quality

Scarcity is not only about how much water there is. It is also about whether the water is **safe to drink**. A river in spate carries thousands of litres past your village every second, but if it is full of pesticide runoff, industrial chromium, untreated sewage, and faecal bacteria, then a child cannot drink any of it. Geographers call this **water-quality scarcity**.

India suffers from both kinds. Bihar, West Bengal, and parts of Assam have enough surface water but suffer **arsenic contamination** in shallow wells. Punjab pumps too much groundwater, and what remains is contaminated by fertiliser nitrates. Delhi's Yamuna runs black with sewage for most of its city length. The Ganga at Kanpur receives so much tannery effluent that fish populations have collapsed.

So when you ask "does India have a water problem?" the honest answer is — yes, two problems. One of **quantity** (not enough where and when we need it), and one of **quality** (a lot of what flows past is poisoned by us before anyone can use it).

## Multi-Purpose River Projects: India's Big Bet

After Independence in 1947, India faced a vast country that needed food, electricity, and protection from floods. Jawaharlal Nehru called large dams the **"temples of modern India"** and the state set about building them on every major river system. These were not single-purpose dams — they were designed to do many things at once. Hence the name **multi-purpose river projects**.

A typical multi-purpose project does several jobs from one structure:

- Stores monsoon water in a reservoir for use throughout the year
- Generates **hydroelectric power** as water falls through turbines
- Releases water through canals for **irrigation**
- Controls floods downstream by holding back excess monsoon flow
- Supplies water to towns and industries
- Sometimes adds a navigation channel, a fishery, and a tourist site

The **Bhakra Nangal project** on the Sutlej is the most famous. Begun in 1948 and completed in 1963, Bhakra Dam is 226 metres tall — one of the highest gravity dams in the world. It transformed Punjab, Haryana, and Rajasthan, sending canal water to fields that had once depended on uncertain rain. The Green Revolution of the 1960s would have been impossible without it.

The **Hirakud project** on the Mahanadi in Odisha — the world's longest dam at the time of its completion in 1957 — was built mainly to control the murderous Mahanadi floods that had devastated coastal Odisha for centuries. It also irrigates lakhs of hectares and powers the steel and aluminium industries of Rourkela.

The **Damodar Valley Corporation**, modelled on the American Tennessee Valley Authority, harnessed the destructive Damodar river through a chain of dams (Tilaiya, Konar, Maithon, Panchet) and changed the economic geography of Bihar and Bengal.

The **Sardar Sarovar Dam** on the Narmada in Gujarat is the most controversial of all — we will return to it. The **Nagarjuna Sagar** on the Krishna, the **Tungabhadra** on its tributary, the **Mettur** on the Kaveri, the **Indira Sagar** on the Narmada, and the **Tehri** on the Bhagirathi all followed the same logic: store, generate, irrigate, control.

For three decades, the dam was India's symbol of progress. By the 1980s, the costs began to be added up — and the dam debate began.

## The Case Against Big Dams

Big dams are not only big in size. They are big in their consequences — and a generation of social scientists, ecologists, and the people who live in the submerged valleys began to argue that those consequences had been hidden in the official accounts.

**Displacement of people.** When a dam fills its reservoir, the water drowns everything upstream — fields, villages, forests, temples, ancestral graves. The people who lived there must move. Across India's dams since 1947, an estimated **50 million people**, many of them tribal communities (**Adivasis**) who had lived in the river valleys for generations, have been displaced. Most never received the land, jobs, or compensation they were promised. Studies by the Indian Institute of Public Administration found that **40 per cent of those displaced by large dams are Adivasis**, though Adivasis make up only 8 per cent of India's population. The pain has been distributed unequally.

**Ecological damage.** A dam cuts a river in two. The natural flow of sediment — the silt that built the Gangetic plains over millennia — is trapped behind the wall. Downstream, the river starves of nutrients and gradually deepens its bed, undercutting embankments and farmlands. Fish that migrate up the river to breed (like the *hilsa*) can no longer cross. Wetlands downstream dry up. Mangroves at the delta shrink as the freshwater pulse weakens. The reservoir itself, by trapping decomposing vegetation, **emits methane** — a powerful greenhouse gas. Studies have shown some tropical reservoirs emit as much greenhouse gas per unit of energy as a coal-fired plant.

**Inter-state and inter-regional disputes.** Rivers do not respect state borders. The same dam can benefit Karnataka while angering Tamil Nadu (the Kaveri waters dispute), or benefit Gujarat at the cost of Madhya Pradesh (the Narmada). Politics gets layered onto every spillway.

**Floods, not flood-control.** Critics point out that the cure has sometimes worsened the disease. When a dam reaches full capacity in an unexpectedly heavy monsoon, the operators are forced to release vast volumes of water suddenly. The flood downstream — controlled by no embankment — can be worse than what the river would have produced naturally. The 2018 Kerala floods and several Damodar valley floods have been linked partly to sudden reservoir releases.

**Silting and shrinking life.** Every reservoir slowly fills with silt washed in from upstream. As silt accumulates, storage capacity declines. The Tehri reservoir was designed for a 100-year life — Indian engineers now believe siltation may halve that.

**Lopsided benefits.** The water and power often go to large farmers and cities far away, while the displaced get bare compensation. The benefits flow uphill in social terms — and the costs stay with the poorest.

## The Narmada Bachao Andolan and Medha Patkar

No project crystallised the dam debate more than the **Sardar Sarovar Dam** on the Narmada in Gujarat. Planned to be 138.7 metres high and to irrigate 18 lakh hectares across Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, and Rajasthan, the dam would also submerge **245 villages**, displace over 200,000 people (most of them Adivasi), and drown a stretch of fertile valley that had been inhabited for thousands of years.

In 1985, a young social worker named **Medha Patkar**, then in her early thirties, walked into the Narmada valley. She found that the families about to be displaced did not even know it. Many had not been counted in the project's resettlement lists. Compensation packages assumed everyone owned title deeds — but Adivasi villages held land collectively, with no papers. She and others formed the **Narmada Bachao Andolan** (the "Save the Narmada Movement").

Their argument was not "no dams ever." It was: *not until the people who will lose everything are counted, heard, and properly resettled.* They demanded a full review of the project's costs and benefits — including the ones the government had hidden. They organised **dharnas**, padayatras, and a tactic that became iconic — *jal samarpan*, in which villagers stood in their own homes as the rising reservoir water reached their necks, refusing to move.

The Supreme Court of India in 2000 allowed construction to continue, with conditions on resettlement that have remained controversial. The dam was completed and inaugurated in 2017. Medha Patkar was awarded the **Right Livelihood Award** in 1991 — sometimes called the "alternative Nobel" — for her work.

Whatever side you take, the Narmada Bachao Andolan changed how India talks about development. After it, no major dam project could pretend that the displaced did not exist. Every Environmental Impact Assessment now must include a social cost assessment. The phrase "development for whom?" entered the Indian conversation.

## Tehri: A Dam in the Himalayas

The **Tehri Dam** on the Bhagirathi in Uttarakhand is India's tallest at 260 metres. Completed in 2006 after decades of construction, it submerged the old town of Tehri and around 100 villages, displacing some 100,000 people. Like Sardar Sarovar, Tehri was opposed by a long activist movement — the environmentalist **Sundarlal Bahuguna** sat on a hunger strike at its construction site multiple times.

Tehri has added powerful arguments that Sardar Sarovar lacked: **seismic risk**. The Himalayan region is one of the most earthquake-prone zones on earth. Critics asked what would happen if a major earthquake cracked a 260-metre dam holding back the Ganga's tributary. The reservoir's water would race down the valley, hit Rishikesh and Haridwar within hours, and reach the densely populated Gangetic plains soon after. The government's geologists insist the dam is built to withstand the strongest expected quake. Activists argue we cannot know until it is tested.

The Tehri question — how much risk is acceptable for how much electricity — remains live across the Himalayas, where dozens more hydro projects are planned or under construction.

## The Quiet Revolution: Rainwater Harvesting

While the country argued about big dams, a quiet revolution was happening in villages that the dam-building decades had ignored. People were looking again at the **traditional water systems** their grandparents had built — and finding that, scaled up, those old techniques could solve problems that crores of rupees of concrete had not.

**Rainwater harvesting** is the simple, ancient idea of catching rain where it falls and storing or sinking it for later use. In a country where 75 per cent of rain arrives in 100 days, the technique is almost magical. Caught water is free of fluoride and arsenic, paid for by no electricity bill, and held by no quarrelling state government.

India has been doing this for at least 2,500 years.

## Old Indian Genius: Hydraulic Structures Across History

Long before Bhakra, Indians built water structures that still command an engineer's respect.

In **3rd century BCE Kalinga** (present-day Odisha), King Kharavela's stone inscriptions describe an irrigation channel he had built — one of the earliest references to public hydraulic engineering on the subcontinent. The Mauryan Emperor Chandragupta is recorded by Greek visitors as having a department of water management whose officers measured rainfall and supervised the levelling of canals.

**Sringaverapura**, near Allahabad, has a 1st century BCE flood-water harvesting system: an elaborate set of brick channels that diverted the Ganga's flood waters into a series of three desilting tanks before storing the cleaned water in an underground reservoir. The system was rediscovered by archaeologists in the 1990s — Indians of two thousand years ago had already worked out **sediment management**.

The **Chola dynasty** of southern India (9th to 13th century CE) built thousands of irrigation tanks, called **eris** in Tamil. The Cholas' tanks were often arranged in **cascades** — water that spilled from one tank flowed into the next downhill, so that no drop went unused. Some of these tanks still irrigate Tamil Nadu farmland today. The most famous, the **Kallanai** or Grand Anicut built by Karikalan Chola on the Kaveri, is still functional 2,000 years later — among the oldest working hydraulic structures in the world.

The **Bhopal lake** in Madhya Pradesh, said to have been built by Raja Bhoj in the 11th century, was once Asia's largest artificial lake. The stepwells of **Gujarat and Rajasthan** — Rani-ki-Vav in Patan, Adalaj near Ahmedabad, Chand Baori in Abhaneri — are simultaneously engineering marvels, temples, and rain harvesters.

These were not curiosities. They were the *normal* infrastructure of a civilisation that knew its monsoon.

## Rajasthan's Underground Tanks: Kund and Tanka

In the deserts of western Rajasthan, where the rains may give 100 mm or 200 mm in a year — and sometimes nothing at all for three years — survival has always depended on catching every drop. The traditional answer is the **kund** (also called **tanka**): a circular underground tank, dug into the courtyard of a house and lined with chuna (lime) plaster, fed by sloping channels from the rooftops and the surrounding compound. After one monsoon, a well-built tanka can hold enough drinking water for a whole family for the entire year. Wealthy households had tanks large enough to last through multiple bad years.

In Phalodi and Bikaner, almost every traditional haveli has its kund. The water collected this way is cool, clean (the underground temperature prevents bacterial growth), and free of the salinity that ruins much of Rajasthan's groundwater. In drought years, the kund water was the only water — and a household that had not maintained its tanka would have to beg or buy from those who had.

A related structure is the **johad** — a small earthen check-dam built across a gully or seasonal stream to slow the runoff and let it sink into the soil, recharging local wells. In the 1980s and 90s, the social worker **Rajendra Singh** and his organisation **Tarun Bharat Sangh** in Alwar district revived the johad with such success that **dry rivers like the Arvari began flowing year-round again**. Singh became known as the "**Waterman of India**" and received the Magsaysay Award in 2001.

## Meghalaya's Bamboo-Pipe Marvel

Travel two thousand kilometres east, into the hills of Meghalaya — the wettest place on earth — and you will find a completely different problem. Rainfall is not the issue; it is too much rainfall, falling onto a slope, washing topsoil away, never settling into a usable supply. The **Khasi and Jaintia tribes** developed a 200-year-old system of **bamboo-pipe drip irrigation** that solves it elegantly.

A spring high on a hillside is tapped at its source. Bamboo trunks — light, hollow, cheap, naturally replaceable — are split, cleaned, and joined into a network of pipes that carry water down the slope to plantations of betel leaf, areca nut, and pepper hundreds of metres below. The pipes branch and narrow as they descend, regulated by smaller bamboo segments and sometimes wooden plugs to drip water exactly where it is needed. The water arrives at each plant in droplets, not floods — a kind of natural drip irrigation working entirely on gravity, with no pumps, no chemicals, and no electricity. About **18 to 20 litres** entering the main pipe high on the slope drips through a vast distribution network and reaches the plants at the bottom as just a few drops per second per plant. The whole system can be made and maintained by a family with hand tools.

When 20th-century irrigation engineers studied the bamboo systems, they realised the Khasis had been doing **micro-irrigation** centuries before the Israelis "invented" it.

## Karnataka's Tanks and Tamil Nadu's Tankas

The Western Ghats sit just above the dry plateau of southern Karnataka. As the monsoon clouds spill over, they drop their water on the western slopes and leave the eastern districts in rain shadow. To survive there, kings and villages built **kere** — irrigation tanks — by walling off seasonal streams and creating shallow lakes that filled in the monsoon and irrigated through the dry months. Bengaluru itself was once a city of dozens of interconnected lakes — Sankey, Ulsoor, Madivala, Hebbal, Bellandur — each part of a cascade. Most of those tanks have been concreted over in modern times, and Bengaluru's water troubles today are direct consequences.

In Tamil Nadu, where the **kudimaramat** custom required villagers to clean and maintain their irrigation tanks once a year, neglect of the maintenance has reduced thousands of working tanks to silted, weed-choked ponds. Several state governments are now reviving them under MGNREGA labour programmes — desilting, rebuilding bunds, reconnecting feeder channels. The lesson is repetitive: the old structures still work; what fails is the social arrangement that maintained them.

## Modern Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting

You do not need to live in a Rajasthani desert or a Meghalayan hill to harvest rain. Any rooftop in India can collect it. **Tamil Nadu** in 2003 became the first state to make **rooftop rainwater harvesting** **mandatory** for every new building, and the change was visible within five years — Chennai's groundwater levels rose, and city wells that had been abandoned began yielding again. The state has now spread the rule and some 4.7 million structures have been retrofitted with harvesting systems.

A basic rooftop system has four parts: a **catchment** (the roof), **conveyance** (the pipes carrying water down), a **first-flush diverter** (which throws away the first dirty water of the season that has washed dust off the roof), and **storage or recharge** — either a tank, or a pit dug deep enough to let the water sink down and recharge the groundwater under the building. The cost of installation for a small home is a few thousand rupees; the saving on tanker water and electricity to pump from the borewell pays it back in a year or two.

**Gendathur**, a village in Mysuru district, Karnataka, has become a poster case. About 200 households in a semi-arid area installed rooftop rainwater harvesting tanks. With an average annual rainfall of around 1,000 mm and a roof area of around 20 square metres per house, each household harvests around 50,000 litres a year — enough to cover the bulk of its drinking and cooking needs. The village wells, which used to dry up by April, now hold water year-round.

**Bengaluru** has tried similar legislation, with mixed success. Properties above a certain size are required to install harvesting systems, but enforcement has been patchy. Where it works — in the apartment complexes of Whitefield and Sarjapur — it has reduced tanker dependence by 30–40 per cent.

## What This Means for You

You are likely reading this in a building that lets every monsoon downpour roll off its roof and into a drain. A house with a 100 square metre roof in a 1,000 mm rainfall zone could harvest about **100,000 litres of rainwater a year** — enough to cover much of a small family's non-drinking needs. The maths is so favourable that the only mystery is why every rooftop in India is not already collecting.

Beyond rainwater harvesting, the everyday list is unglamorous but powerful: **fix leaking taps** (a single drip wastes 30+ litres a day), **switch to dual-flush toilets** (saves up to 60 per cent per flush), use a **bucket and mug** instead of a shower for most days, water plants in the **evening** to reduce evaporation, **reuse RO reject water** (which is 60–80 per cent of input) for floor washing or plants, and **eat seasonal local produce** — half the country's water footprint is the irrigation cost of what we eat, especially polished rice and out-of-season vegetables.

Indian cities will not solve their water problem by drilling deeper, building larger reservoirs further away, or arguing across state borders forever. They will solve it the way Tamil Nadu, Gendathur, and a hundred Khasi villages already have — by treating every roof as a catchment, every aquifer as a savings account, and every drop as something a civilisation thousands of years old already knew how to honour.

## Apply: Stories, DIY, and a Question to Keep Asking

### Story 1 — Chennai's "Day Zero" (2019)

In June 2019, Chennai officially announced that its four main reservoirs — Poondi, Cholavaram, Red Hills, and Chembarambakkam — had together fallen to **0.2 per cent of capacity**. India's sixth largest city, home to over ten million people, had no surface water left. Trains were chartered from Vellore to bring 2.5 million litres a day. Hotels rationed water. IT companies closed. Families bathed once in three days. The crisis was not a single failed monsoon — it was the slow death of Chennai's lakes, encroached and concreted over decades, combined with falling groundwater from over-pumping. After Day Zero, the city aggressively expanded rainwater harvesting and began desilting old eris in its hinterland. The 2020 monsoon refilled the reservoirs — but the warning hangs over every Indian metro.

### Story 2 — Cherrapunji Rebuilds Traditional Harvesting

Cherrapunji (Sohra) in Meghalaya gets nearly 11,000 mm of rain a year — and runs out of drinking water by March. The same geography that delivers the wettest air on earth also lets it all rush down the slope before anyone can catch it. After decades of looking to government pipelines that never quite worked, communities around Cherrapunji have begun reviving the bamboo-pipe systems and adding modern rooftop tanks. NGOs have helped build catchment ponds high on the hills to slow the runoff. Slowly, water is being kept on the slope long enough to use it — proof that even the world's wettest town must harvest.

### Story 3 — Hiware Bazar, Maharashtra

In the early 1990s, Hiware Bazar in Maharashtra's Ahmednagar district was a dying village — drought-stricken, alcoholic, and depopulated. Its sarpanch, **Popatrao Pawar**, refused to accept the verdict. Under his leadership the village imposed strict water rules — no water-intensive crops like sugarcane or banana, drip irrigation only, and a complete watershed treatment using contour bunds, percolation tanks, and check dams. Within a decade Hiware Bazar's groundwater table had risen, its agricultural income had multiplied many times, and 90 per cent of its families had crossed the poverty line. Pawar received the Padma Shri. The lesson — water shortage is rarely just a rainfall problem; it is a rules-and-discipline problem.

### DIY — Three things you can do this week

1. **Track your home water use for one week.** Read the meter or note tanker deliveries. Keep a daily log: drinking, cooking, bathing, washing, gardening, toilet. At the end of the week, calculate litres per person per day. The WHO basic minimum is 50 litres per person per day. The Indian average is 135 litres. You may be shocked at which activity is using the most.

2. **Calculate your home's rainwater harvesting potential.** Measure your rooftop area in square metres. Multiply by your city's average annual rainfall in millimetres. Divide by 1,000. That number is litres per year your roof could harvest. For example, a 100 m² roof in a 900 mm rainfall city = 90,000 litres a year. Compare to your annual water consumption.

3. **Visit a local water body and document its condition.** Pick a nearby lake, tank, well, or river. Photograph it. Note the colour and smell of the water, whether anyone uses it, what is dumped into it, what fish or birds you see. If it is an old structure — a stepwell, a johad, an eri — find out who used to maintain it.

### Show what you found

Combine the three exercises into a one-page report or poster: "My family's water — where it comes from, where it goes, what the rooftop could catch, and one nearby water body that has been forgotten." Share it with your class or with your housing society's water committee. Many neighbourhoods in India have started rooftop-harvesting drives because one student walked in with this kind of map.
