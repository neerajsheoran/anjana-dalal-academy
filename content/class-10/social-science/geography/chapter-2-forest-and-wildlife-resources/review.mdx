## In One Paragraph

India occupies only 2.4% of the world's land but holds about 8% of its known species — roughly 47,000 plants and 89,000 animals, including endemic wonders like the Asiatic lion of Gir, the lion-tailed macaque of the Western Ghats, and the one-horned rhino of Kaziranga. Scientists classify species using the IUCN Red Data Book into normal, vulnerable, endangered, rare, endemic, and extinct categories — and at least 10% of India's flowering plants and 20% of its mammals are now threatened. The drivers of depletion are habitat destruction, colonial-era logging for timber and railways, mining in Jharkhand and Odisha, dam projects like Narmada Sagar, overgrazing, poaching for skin and horn, pollution, invasive species like Lantana, and unequal consumption that blames the poor while the urban rich consume more. India's conservation response began with the Indian Wildlife Protection Act of 1972, followed by Project Tiger (1973), Project Crocodile, Project Elephant, biosphere reserves, and 104 national parks. Forests are legally classified as reserved (about half), protected (about a third), and unclassed. The deepest conservation, however, has come from communities: the Bishnois of Rajasthan who died for the khejri trees in 1730, the Chipko hug-the-tree movement of 1973 in Garhwal led by Gaura Devi, the village-protected Bhairodev Dakav Sonchuri in Alwar, and Joint Forest Management launched in Odisha in 1988 — proof that legal protection works best when paired with cultural protection rooted in the people who live closest to the forest.

## Key Takeaways

- **India = 2.4% of world land, ~8% of world species** — roughly 47,000 plants and 89,000 animals, with about 15,000 endemic flowering plants.
- **IUCN Red Data Book** classifies species as **normal, vulnerable, endangered, rare, endemic, and extinct** — a global health chart for biodiversity.
- **Endangered Indian species** include the Asiatic lion, blackbuck, Indian rhino, lion-tailed macaque, sangai, Indian wild ass, and great Indian bustard.
- **Extinct Indian species** include the Asiatic cheetah (declared extinct in 1952), the pink-headed duck, and the Indian aurochs — though the cheetah was reintroduced to Kuno NP in 2022.
- **Drivers of depletion**: habitat destruction, colonial timber extraction, mining, big dams, overgrazing, poaching, pollution, invasive species, and unequal consumption.
- **Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972** — the foundational law: banned hunting of listed species, created national parks and sanctuaries, set up state wildlife wings.
- **Project Tiger (1 April 1973)** — started with 9 reserves and 1,827 tigers; today over 50 reserves with **3,167 tigers** (2022 census), the world's largest wild population.
- **Sariska scandal (2005)** — Sariska Tiger Reserve was found to have zero tigers; villagers had raised alarm before officials admitted it.
- **Other projects**: Project Crocodile (1975), Project Elephant (1992), Project Hangul, Project Snow Leopard (2009), Rhino Vision 2020.
- **18 biosphere reserves** in India — including Nilgiri, Nanda Devi, Sundarbans, Gulf of Mannar, Manas.
- **Forest classification**: reserved (~50%), protected (~33%), unclassed — with high reserved-forest shares in MP, AP, Maharashtra, Odisha, Jharkhand.
- **National Forest Policy 1988** targets **33% cover in plains, 67% in hills**; India currently has only about 24.6% forest cover.
- **The Bishnois of Khejarli (1730)** — 363 men, women and children died defending khejri trees from the Maharaja of Jodhpur's woodcutters; their martyrdom is the oldest recorded environmental sacrifice in India.
- **Chipko Movement (1973–74)** in Garhwal — led by Gaura Devi, Sunderlal Bahuguna, Chandi Prasad Bhatt; villagers hugged trees to stop loggers; put rural women at the centre of Indian environmentalism.
- **Joint Forest Management (JFM)** — Odisha launched the model in 1988; village forest committees co-manage degraded forests in exchange for a share of produce; now covers 17+ million hectares across 27 states.
- **Sacred groves** — community-protected forest patches like Mawphlang (Meghalaya), Devarakadu (Karnataka), Orans (Rajasthan), Kavus (Kerala), Sarna (Jharkhand) — India's oldest unwritten conservation policy.

## Key Terms

- **Biodiversity** — The total variety of plant and animal life in a region, including the diversity within species, between species, and of ecosystems.
- **Flora** — The plant life of a region or period.
- **Fauna** — The animal life of a region or period.
- **Endemic species** — Species found only in one particular geographic area, often because of isolation; e.g., Asiatic lion (Gir), lion-tailed macaque (Western Ghats).
- **Endangered species** — Species in immediate danger of extinction unless conditions change; e.g., Indian rhino, blackbuck, sangai.
- **Vulnerable species** — Species whose populations are declining and that may become endangered if the threats continue; e.g., Gangetic dolphin, Asiatic elephant.
- **Extinct species** — Species no longer found anywhere on Earth (or in a region); e.g., the Asiatic cheetah in India (1952).
- **IUCN Red Data Book** — The international list maintained by the International Union for Conservation of Nature that records the conservation status of every assessed species.
- **Biosphere reserve** — A large protected area covering whole ecosystems and the human settlements within them, designed for both conservation and sustainable use; India has 18.
- **Reserved forest** — Forest legally declared the most protected — about half of India's forest area — where felling, grazing, and entry are tightly restricted.
- **Joint Forest Management (JFM)** — A 1988 policy beginning in Odisha that gives local communities a formal role and a share in managing and restoring degraded forests.
- **Sacred grove** — A community-protected forest patch preserved by religious tradition; one of India's oldest forms of conservation.
