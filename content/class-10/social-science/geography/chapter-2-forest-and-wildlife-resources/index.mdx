## A Country Built on Living Things

If you flew over India slowly enough — from the **snow leopards** padding through the Ladakhi cold, down through the deodar forests of Himachal, past the deciduous sal forests of central India, into the **Western Ghats** where a single hill can hold more frog species than all of Europe, and out to the **mangroves of the Sundarbans** where Royal Bengal Tigers swim between islands — you would notice something strange. India occupies only about **2.4% of the world's land area**, but it is home to roughly **8% of the planet's known plant and animal species**. We are, by any honest accounting, a **biodiversity superpower**.

This is not an accident. India sits at the meeting point of three biogeographic realms — the Palearctic from the north, the Ethiopian from the west, and the Indo-Malayan from the east. Add to that an extraordinary range of climates (from the Thar's blistering sand to Cherrapunji's drowning rain), of altitudes (from sea level to the world's highest mountains), and of cultures that have lived *alongside* wild creatures for millennia rather than wiping them out. Together, these things made India one of the great living museums of the world.

And in our lifetimes, much of that museum is being quietly emptied. This chapter is about what we have, what we are losing, and the surprising people who are still saving it.

## Flora and Fauna — What India Actually Holds

The word **flora** means the plant life of a region; **fauna** means the animal life. Together they make up the **biodiversity** of a place. India's numbers are sobering and proud:

- About **47,000** species of plants — making us the world's tenth richest country in plant diversity, fourth in Asia.
- About **89,000** species of animals — including roughly **1,200** kinds of birds, **2,500** kinds of fish, **400** kinds of reptiles, and more than 60,000 kinds of insects (we are still counting).
- Around **15,000** of our flowering plants and a vast number of our animals are **endemic** — meaning they are found nowhere else on Earth. The **Asiatic lion** survives only in **Gir National Park** in Gujarat. The **lion-tailed macaque** is found only in the dense rainforests of the **Western Ghats**. The **Indian one-horned rhinoceros** belongs to the swampy grasslands of **Kaziranga and Manas in Assam**. The **Nilgiri tahr** climbs only the high ridges of the Western Ghats.

But here is the harder truth: scientists estimate that **at least 10% of India's recorded flowering plants and 20% of its mammals** are now on the **threatened list**. Many of these may not survive to see your grandchildren.

## How Scientists Classify Species — The IUCN Categories

The **International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN)** is the global body that keeps a running list — called the **Red Data Book** — of how every known species is doing. The categories you must know are these:

- **Normal species** — populations are healthy enough to last. The **cattle**, the **sal tree**, the **rodents** of our fields.
- **Endangered species** — in danger of becoming extinct. Their habitat has shrunk so much that without immediate help, they will not last. Examples: the **blackbuck**, the **crocodile**, the **Indian wild ass**, the **Indian rhino**, the **lion-tailed macaque**, the **sangai (brow-antlered deer)** of Manipur.
- **Vulnerable species** — population is declining; if the pressures continue, they will slip into the endangered category. Examples: the **blue sheep**, the **Asiatic elephant**, the **Gangetic dolphin**.
- **Rare species** — small populations that could become vulnerable or endangered if the conditions around them worsen. Examples: the **Himalayan brown bear**, the **wild Asiatic buffalo**, the **desert fox**, the **hornbill**.
- **Endemic species** — found only in one particular area, usually because of geographic isolation. Examples: the **Andaman teal**, the **Nicobar pigeon**, the **Andaman wild pig**, the **mithun** of Arunachal Pradesh.
- **Extinct species** — gone forever from the area or from the planet. The **Asiatic cheetah**, declared extinct in India in 1952, is the most famous Indian example. The pink-headed duck has not been seen since the 1940s.

A useful way to read these categories is this: think of a hospital ward. *Normal* is the healthy person walking outside. *Vulnerable* is the patient with worrying symptoms. *Endangered* is the patient in the ICU. *Extinct* is the patient we lost. The Red Data Book is the daily chart for every species in this hospital, kept up to date by thousands of scientists.

## Why Are We Losing Species So Fast?

The depletion of India's flora and fauna is not a single accident — it is the cumulative result of decisions taken over centuries. The chapter divides the causes into a few large patterns:

**1. Habitat destruction.** When a forest is cleared, animals do not simply move next door. Most of them die. **Agricultural expansion** since Independence has converted vast tracts of forest into croplands. Between **1951 and 1980**, more than **26,200 square kilometres** of forest were converted to farmland in India alone. The chapter calls this **enrichment plantation** when monoculture trees (like teak or eucalyptus) replace the natural mixed forest — the trees are still there, but biodiversity collapses.

**2. Colonial-era exploitation.** When the British took over forests in the nineteenth century, they treated them as a source of **timber for shipbuilding, sleepers for railways, and revenue**. The Indian **sal, teak, and deodar** were cut on industrial scale. Trees that took 200 years to grow were felled in 200 minutes. The colonial **Forest Act of 1865** declared most Indian forests state property, pushing **tribal communities** out of lands they had lived on for generations.

**3. Mining.** Mineral extraction — particularly **iron ore, dolomite, and limestone** — has scarred enormous tracts of forest in **Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, and Odisha**. The **Buxa Tiger Reserve** in West Bengal is under threat from a dolomite mining project. Mining does not only cut the trees; it pollutes water bodies, blocks elephant corridors, and silences the forest for generations.

**4. Big dams and reservoirs.** Large hydroelectric projects flood huge areas of forest. The **Narmada Sagar project** in Madhya Pradesh submerged roughly **40,000 hectares of forest**. The **Tehri dam** drowned ancient deodar valleys.

**5. Grazing and fuelwood collection.** In areas where local communities depend on the forest for daily fuel and fodder, **overgrazing** by livestock and over-collection of firewood degrade what is left.

**6. Hunting and poaching.** **Tiger skins, rhino horn, elephant ivory, musk-deer pods, peacock feathers** — for centuries these were status symbols, and even today an underground trade kills hundreds of animals every year. India lost an estimated **40,000 tigers** in the colonial period; we have today around **3,000**.

**7. Pollution and invasive species.** Pesticides washed into rivers killed off freshwater life. **Lantana camara** and **prosopis juliflora**, introduced as ornamental or fuel plants, have invaded forest floors and choked native vegetation.

**8. Unequal access and consumption.** The chapter makes a sharp social point — **the rich consume far more wood, paper, and energy per person than the poor**, and the poor are often blamed for the destruction while the urban consumer escapes notice. Conservation that ignores this inequality will always fail.

The destruction of plant and animal life affects people too — and most of all, the **poor and the women** who depend most directly on the forest for fuel, fodder, food, and medicinal plants. When the forest dies, they walk further, eat less, and lose the knowledge their grandmothers carried.

## How the Indian Government Has Responded — The Conservation Strategy

The first serious legal response came in **1972**, when Parliament passed the **Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act**. This single law did several things:

- It created an **all-India list of protected species** whose hunting was banned and whose trade was heavily restricted.
- It set up **national parks and wildlife sanctuaries** as protected areas where habitat is preserved.
- It authorised state governments to establish wildlife wings.

Today India has about **104 national parks**, more than **560 wildlife sanctuaries**, and **18 biosphere reserves** — including the **Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve**, the **Nanda Devi Reserve**, the **Sundarbans**, and the **Gulf of Mannar**.

A **national park** is a strictly protected area where private rights of land, grazing, or firewood collection are not allowed. A **wildlife sanctuary** allows some such activities, with restrictions. A **biosphere reserve** is larger still, covering ecosystems and the human settlements within them, and is designed for both conservation and sustainable use.

### Project Tiger — 1973

The most famous conservation programme India has run is **Project Tiger**, launched on **1 April 1973** by the central government, when the wild tiger population had collapsed from around **55,000 at the start of the twentieth century to about 1,827** in 1973. Nine tiger reserves were set up — **Corbett (Uttarakhand), Sariska (Rajasthan), Ranthambore (Rajasthan), Manas (Assam), Sunderbans (West Bengal)**, Palamau, Bandipur, Melghat, and Kanha among the original — and have grown today to **more than 50 reserves** covering over 75,000 km².

Project Tiger has been a partial success and a partial scandal. By **2006**, tiger numbers had recovered to around **1,411**; by the **2022 census**, India counted **3,167 tigers** — the largest wild tiger population in the world. Yet in 2005, the **Sariska Tiger Reserve** was famously discovered to have *zero tigers left*, after poachers had quietly emptied it while the official records said otherwise. The bigger lesson: conservation is only as honest as the people watching the watchers.

### Other Conservation Projects

- **Project Crocodile (1975)** has restored populations of the **gharial, mugger, and saltwater crocodile** in the Mahanadi, Kaveri, and Sundarbans.
- **Project Elephant (1992)** protects elephant corridors across 16 states.
- **Project Hangul** protects the Kashmiri stag.
- **Project Snow Leopard (2009)** runs across the Himalayan states.
- The **Indian Rhino Vision 2020** lifted Kaziranga's one-horned rhino population from around 600 in the 1970s to **2,613 by 2022**.
- Conservation of **Olive Ridley turtles** at **Gahirmatha (Odisha)** protects one of the world's largest sea turtle nesting beaches.
- **Vulture conservation programmes** have begun to reverse the collapse caused by the veterinary drug **diclofenac**.

In **1991**, an amendment to the Wildlife Act added comprehensive protection for **plants** and a long list of **butterflies, moths, beetles, and one dragonfly** — a quiet recognition that biodiversity is not only about the tiger.

## Types of Forests and Their Legal Status

Not all Indian forests have the same legal status. The chapter divides government-classified forests into three kinds:

**Reserved forests** — about **half** of India's total forest area falls in this category. These are considered the most valuable for conservation and for forest produce. Felling timber, grazing, and entry are tightly controlled by the Forest Department.

**Protected forests** — about **one-third** of total forest area. Here the forest is protected from further destruction, but local communities are allowed certain rights of grazing and firewood collection.

**Unclassed forests** — the rest, including forests on private land and on community-owned land. Both governments and private owners manage these. In many North-Eastern states (Assam, Manipur, Nagaland), large portions are unclassed and managed by **tribal village councils**.

States like **Madhya Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, and Jharkhand** have a high proportion of reserved forests. States like the **North-East and Gujarat** have a high share of protected forests.

A crucial point the chapter emphasises: the **forest cover of India today is approximately 24.6%** (according to the latest Forest Survey of India), but the **National Forest Policy of 1988** set the target at **33% for the plains and 67% for the hills**. We are decades short of our own promise.

## When Communities Save Forests — Without the Government

Here is the part of the chapter that should stay with you longest. Some of the most remarkable conservation in India is being done not by laws, but by communities — usually poor, often tribal, who have lived alongside specific forests for generations.

### The Bishnois of Rajasthan and the Black Buck

The **Bishnoi community**, founded by **Guru Jambheshwar in 1485**, follow 29 rules (*bishnoi* literally means "twenty-nine") — among them, the prohibition on cutting green trees and on harming any wild creature. In the villages around **Khejarli**, the **blackbuck and chinkara** wander into Bishnoi courtyards without fear. The community sees these animals as part of the family. When poachers come, the Bishnois have been known to chase them down on motorcycles and bring them to justice.

The story of the Bishnois is also one of India's first recorded environmental martyrdoms — and we'll return to it in the Apply section below.

### The Sariska Tiger Watch

When **Sariska Tiger Reserve** lost its tigers to poachers in 2005, it was the local **Bhairon Singh** and his community of villagers who first sounded the alarm, well before the Forest Department admitted the loss. Sariska's villagers have since organised informal tiger-monitoring patrols, walking the forest in shifts and reporting illegal activity. When tigers were reintroduced to Sariska from Ranthambore in 2008, it was these village monitors who tracked the new animals.

### Bhairodev Dakav Sonchuri

In **Alwar district of Rajasthan**, the villages of five settlements have declared **1,200 hectares of forest** as the **Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri'** — their own community-protected sanctuary. They have written their own rules against hunting and outsider exploitation. The Government of Rajasthan does not officially recognise it as a protected area; the villagers do not particularly care. The forest is recovering anyway.

### The Chipko Movement — Garhwal, 1973

In **March 1973**, in the village of **Mandal in Garhwal**, contractors from a sports goods company arrived to cut ash trees for tennis rackets. Local villagers — many of them women — went into the forest and **hugged the trees** so the loggers could not strike them. The contractors withdrew. Word spread. Through 1973 and 1974, women across Garhwal, led by **Gaura Devi** and inspired by Gandhian leaders **Sunderlal Bahuguna** and **Chandi Prasad Bhatt**, used the same simple, unarmed tactic. The movement came to be called **Chipko** (from *chipakna*, to embrace).

Chipko did more than save trees. It transformed Indian environmental thinking. It put rural women — usually invisible in policy debates — at the centre of the environmental movement. And it produced the slogan that summarised the village's defence of the forest: "*What do the forests bear? Soil, water, and pure air.*"

### Traditional Sacred Groves

Across India, communities have for centuries maintained **sacred groves** — small forest patches dedicated to a deity, never cut, never disturbed. The **Mawphlang sacred grove in Meghalaya**, the **Devarakadu in Karnataka**, the **Sarna groves of Jharkhand**, the **Kavus of Kerala**, the **Orans of Rajasthan** — these are India's oldest, unrecorded protected areas. The **Mundas and the Santhals** of Chota Nagpur worship the **mahua** and **kadamba** trees; the **tribes of Odisha and Bihar** worship the **tamarind and mango**. Their religion is, in effect, conservation policy with deeper roots than any law.

## Joint Forest Management — When Government and Community Sit Together

In **1988**, **Odisha** became the first Indian state to formally invite local communities into forest management — through the **Joint Forest Management (JFM) Programme**. The idea was simple: villagers who live on the edge of a degraded forest are given a share in its restoration and a share in the produce. The government formally hands over management of selected forest patches to **village forest committees (VFCs)** of about **10–15 members**. In return, the community plants trees, prevents felling, fights forest fires — and is allowed to collect non-timber forest produce like fruits, honey, leaves, and medicinal plants. After the forest matures, they also receive a share of the timber.

By the early 2000s, JFM had spread to **27 states** and was managing more than **17 million hectares** of degraded forest. The model is imperfect — committees can be captured by the powerful, and the government does not always honour its share — but it represents a fundamental shift: the forest is not just a state asset but a **shared trust** between the state and the people who live with it.

## What Should a Class 10 Student Take Away?

The story of India's flora and fauna is not one story but a tug-of-war. On one side: the great forces of population, profit, and political short-sightedness that have torn down forests for two centuries and counting. On the other side: a determined web of laws, scientists, forest officers, monks, sarpanches, and Bishnoi grandmothers who refuse to let it all disappear.

Some honest conclusions:

- Top-down conservation alone cannot work — Sariska's empty cages proved that.
- Community-led conservation alone cannot work either — without national laws, even the bravest village cannot stop the bulldozer.
- The deepest conservation is **cultural**, not legal. The Bishnois do not need a Forest Act because their religion already wrote one in 1485.
- The poor and the women who live closest to the forest are not the destroyers — they are often the keepers.
- Every species lost is a piece of India that does not come back.

The Asiatic cheetah, declared extinct in 1952, was reintroduced in 2022 in **Kuno National Park, Madhya Pradesh** with cheetahs flown in from Namibia. It is a tiny, fragile attempt to undo seventy years of loss. Whether you, and your generation, will let more species go that way — or pull them back from the edge — is the question this chapter is really asking.

## Apply

Reading about forests is not the same as walking through one. Three stories below, one DIY exercise, one reflection question — to take this chapter off the page and into your week.

### Story 1 — The Day 363 Bishnois Died for the Khejri Trees (1730)

On a cool September morning in 1730, the Maharaja of Jodhpur's soldiers arrived at the village of **Khejarli in Marwar** with axes. The king was building a new palace and needed wood. The villagers were Bishnois. A woman named **Amrita Devi**, hearing the axes, ran to the trees and wrapped her arms around the first khejri. "If a tree is saved at the cost of my head," she said, "it is worth it." The soldiers killed her. Her three young daughters did the same and were killed in turn. Word spread to nearby Bishnoi villages. Men, women, and children poured in and embraced the trees. By the end of the day, **363 Bishnois lay dead** beside the khejris. The Maharaja, when told what had happened, called his men back and decreed that **no green tree would ever again be cut in any Bishnoi village**. Two hundred and forty-three years before Chipko, the world's first hug-the-tree movement had already happened — and paid the highest price.

### Story 2 — How a Garhwali Grandmother Stopped a Logging Truck (1974)

In March 1974 in the village of **Reni in Garhwal**, contractors arrived to fell 2,500 trees while the men were away in a town meeting. The plan had been deliberate. But **Gaura Devi**, an illiterate woman in her fifties and head of the village women's association, gathered 27 women and walked into the forest. They stood between the trees and the axes. "This forest is our mother's house," she told the loggers. "Cut us first." The contractors withdrew. The Reni protest became the moment Chipko broke into the world's news. Gaura Devi had no slogans, no organisation, no degrees. She had a forest and a refusal. She has a statue today in her village — but the better memorial is that the deodars she defended still stand.

### Story 3 — The Penguins of Marine Drive (March 2020)

When India locked down for the COVID-19 pandemic in March 2020, Mumbai's roaring traffic suddenly fell silent. Within days, residents began reporting things they had never seen. **Flamingos** flooded into the Thane creek in numbers not seen in decades. **Olive Ridley turtles** returned to nest at **Versova beach** for the first time in twenty years. In Navi Mumbai, **peacocks** were spotted dancing on apartment terraces. Wildlife photographers captured a **leopard** walking through the empty streets of Chandigarh, and **dolphins** swimming in the cleaned-up waters off the Marine Drive coastline. None of these animals had arrived. They had always been there, just outside our cities — pushed to the margins by our noise and traffic. The moment we paused, they came back. The lockdown taught India a strange ecological lesson: nature does not need us to do anything special to recover. It just needs us to step out of the way occasionally.

### DIY — Try One This Week

- **Walk a sanctuary.** Find the nearest wildlife sanctuary or national park (every state has at least one — Sanjay Gandhi NP for Mumbai, Asola Bhatti for Delhi, Bannerghatta for Bangalore, Mudumalai for Tamil Nadu). Spend half a day there with a notebook. Write down every animal, bird, and tree species you can identify.
- **Survey the trees in 500m around your home.** Count them. Identify them (use the Google Lens or iNaturalist app). Mark on a map any trees that have been cut in the past year. Ask the local sarpanch or RWA who authorised the cutting.
- **Five birds in a week.** Borrow your mother's binoculars (or use the camera zoom on a phone). Spot, photograph, and identify five different bird species in your neighbourhood by the end of the week. Even the city pigeon, common myna, house sparrow, koel, and red-vented bulbul count. You will be surprised how much life is moving above your head.

### A Question to Keep Asking

If the Bishnois saved 363 lives' worth of trees in 1730, and Gaura Devi saved 2,500 trees in 1974, and Sunita Narain spends her life saving forests through policy — **what is the equivalent action for someone of your generation, who lives mostly in a city and on a phone**? It does not have to be dramatic. It only has to be honest. Ask yourself this every few months. The answer will change. The asking should not.
