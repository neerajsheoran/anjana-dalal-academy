## Why Are Some Species Disappearing Fast?

**Child:** Maa, my friend said tigers are coming back. Is that really true after everyone said they were almost gone?

**Mother:** Both things are true, beta. In 1900, India had about 55,000 wild tigers. By 1973, when Project Tiger started, there were only 1,827 left — fewer than the number of seats in your school auditorium. From there, we have climbed back to 3,167 by the 2022 census. So yes, the trend is upward. But — and this is important — we are still at less than 6% of what we had a century ago. So the tiger is coming back; the tiger has not come back.

**Child:** So why did we lose so many in the first place? Just hunting?

**Mother:** Hunting was only one reason. The bigger story is that we destroyed *where the tiger lives*. A tiger needs a big territory — about 20 square kilometres for a tigress, more for a male. When we cut a forest in half to build a road or flood a valley to build a dam, the tiger does not just walk to the other half. Its territory is broken. It cannot find a mate. It cannot find prey. Slowly the family dies out.

**Child:** So saving a tiger means saving a whole forest?

**Mother:** That is exactly right. And this is why the tiger is called an **umbrella species** — when you protect enough forest for tigers, you automatically protect everything else that lives under that umbrella: the deer, the langur, the hornbill, the orchid, the river that runs through it. The tiger is a symbol but also a strategy. Look after the apex and you look after the base.

**Child:** What about the cheetah? The chapter said it went extinct.

**Mother:** The Asiatic cheetah was declared extinct in India in 1952. The last three were shot in 1947 in what is now Chhattisgarh by the Maharaja of Korea. In 2022, the government brought eight African cheetahs from Namibia to **Kuno National Park in Madhya Pradesh** to try and reintroduce them. It is a brave experiment, but it is also a confession — that once you lose a species, even decades of effort and crores of rupees can only attempt to half-undo what was done in a single hunting trip. That is why the original cliché still holds: **prevention is cheaper than resurrection**.

## What's the Difference Between a Wildlife Sanctuary and a National Park?

**Child:** Maa, I keep getting confused between sanctuary, national park, and biosphere reserve. What's the actual difference?

**Mother:** Good question, beta. Think of it like three levels of protection.

A **wildlife sanctuary** is the gentlest. It is meant for a specific animal or group of animals. Private rights are partly allowed — local people can still graze cattle in some areas, collect firewood, and even live there with restrictions. Tourism is allowed. Boundaries can be changed by state government order. India has more than 560 of these.

A **national park** is stricter. No private rights are allowed inside. No grazing, no felling, no human settlements. The boundaries can only be changed by an Act of Parliament — meaning, it is much harder for a future government to shrink it for a mining project. India has about 104.

A **biosphere reserve** is the largest of all. It covers an entire ecosystem — say, the Nilgiri hills — and has three zones: a **core zone** where no human activity is allowed, a **buffer zone** for limited research and tourism, and a **transition zone** where villages live and farm sustainably. India has 18.

**Child:** So national park is the strictest?

**Mother:** Yes — but strictness on paper does not mean strictness in practice. The Sariska story showed us that. Sariska is a national park, but tigers were still poached out of it by 2005. So the legal category matters less than whether the rangers are paid, whether the local villagers cooperate, and whether powerful interests like mining lobbies are kept at bay. The strongest park in the world is only as strong as the will to protect it.

**Child:** Can a national park lose its status?

**Mother:** Legally it is very difficult, but in practice, yes — through neglect, encroachment, illegal mining, or by quietly carving out parts. There are many cases of "denotification" — where parts of protected areas are reclassified to allow industry. That is why citizen vigilance matters more than government promises.

## Did Project Tiger Really Work?

**Child:** Maa, I want to know honestly — did Project Tiger work?

**Mother:** It is the most honest question to ask, beta. The answer is: yes, partly. Let me give you both sides.

On the success side: India today has the world's largest population of wild tigers. We went from 1,827 in 1973 to 3,167 in 2022. The country's forest cover has stabilised around 24% instead of falling. Many of our finest reserves — **Corbett, Kanha, Bandipur, Sundarbans, Periyar, Tadoba** — are showing healthy tiger populations. International conservation looks at India as a success story.

On the failure side: in 2005, Sariska Tiger Reserve was found to have zero tigers — they had all been poached, while official records said the population was 26. The same year, **Panna Tiger Reserve in Madhya Pradesh** also lost all its tigers. In both cases, villagers had been telling officials for years. The officials did not listen. Even today, despite the numbers, tiger habitat is shrinking — the famous **Buxa, Palamau, and Dampa** reserves have very few or no tigers. Tigers are concentrated in a handful of reserves; the rest are running on paper.

**Child:** So it's like a half-passed exam?

**Mother:** Yes — and the dangerous thing about half-passing is the temptation to declare victory too soon. We can save tiger numbers in a few showcase parks while the rest of our wildlife quietly disappears. Project Tiger worked best when it had strong leadership at the centre and local communities that were genuinely involved. It failed where forest officers were corrupt, where villagers were treated as enemies instead of partners, and where political pressure protected illegal mining and resorts.

**Child:** So how should I write about it in the exam?

**Mother:** Tell both stories. Say the programme increased tiger numbers from 1,827 to 3,167 — a real recovery and a global model. But also say that Sariska's collapse and Panna's emptying show that the model fails without genuine community involvement and honest monitoring. The honest answer is always more interesting than the patriotic one.

## Why Did the Bishnois Die for Trees?

**Child:** Maa, I cannot stop thinking about the Bishnois. 363 people died to save khejri trees. Why would anyone do that?

**Mother:** It is one of the most beautiful and difficult questions in this chapter, beta. The Bishnois were founded by **Guru Jambheshwar in 1485** in the deserts of Rajasthan. He gave them 29 rules — to be vegetarian, to not cut green trees, to protect all animals, to filter their water so that no small life is killed. For them, the khejri tree is not a *resource*. It is part of the family. It gives shade in 50-degree heat, fodder for goats, food in famines, soil that holds against desert winds. To the Bishnoi, killing a khejri was no different from killing a brother.

**Child:** But why *die* for a tree? Couldn't they just protest?

**Mother:** In 1730, beta, there was no court to protest in, no newspaper to write to, no Twitter to shame the king on. There was only the Maharaja and his soldiers. When **Amrita Devi** wrapped her arms around the first khejri, she was making a statement that the king's authority ended where her belief began. The soldiers cut her down. Her three daughters did the same. Word spread. By the end of the day, 363 villagers — men, women and children — lay dead beside the trees they had refused to abandon.

**Child:** And the king listened?

**Mother:** He did, beta. The Maharaja of Jodhpur, when he was told what had happened, came in person and apologised. He decreed that **no green tree would ever be cut and no animal harmed in any Bishnoi village** in his kingdom. That decree still stands. The Bishnoi village near Khejarli today is full of khejri trees and blackbucks that walk into people's courtyards. Their faith built India's first wildlife sanctuary — three centuries before our Parliament thought of one.

**Child:** Was Chipko inspired by them?

**Mother:** The Garhwali villagers did not say so explicitly, but the spirit is exactly the same. Both said: *the tree and our body are one*. Both placed the body between the axe and the tree. Both were led, in important ways, by women. The Bishnois acted out of religion; the Chipko women acted out of ecology and livelihood. But the gesture — the human shield around the trunk — is the same. India is the only country in the world that has produced this gesture twice, three hundred years apart. That is something worth knowing.

## Can Communities Save Forests Better Than Governments?

**Child:** Maa, I am beginning to feel like the government has done less than the villagers. Is that fair?

**Mother:** It is half fair, beta. Let me explain. Governments can do things villagers cannot. They can pass national laws — like the Wildlife Protection Act of 1972 — that ban tiger skin trade in every state at once. They can negotiate international treaties so that no Indian rhino horn is sold in China. They can build big sanctuaries that cross district boundaries. They can fund science, training, and helicopters that no village can afford.

But governments cannot do everything villagers can. They cannot be in every patch of forest every night. They cannot smell a poacher hours before he arrives. They cannot care, deeply and personally, for a particular blackbuck herd the way someone who has lived alongside it for forty years does. Conservation needs *both* — the big legal umbrella and the daily local guardianship.

**Child:** So what's the right model?

**Mother:** The closest thing we have is the **Joint Forest Management (JFM) Programme** that began in Odisha in 1988. The government says: "This degraded forest patch — we cannot manage it alone. You villagers protect it, plant trees, prevent fires, and in return you get a share of the fruits, honey, leaves, and eventually the timber." It is a partnership. By the 2000s it had spread to 27 states and covered 17 million hectares.

**Child:** Did it work?

**Mother:** It worked where the government honoured its share of the deal — where it shared real authority and real income with the village. Where it just used JFM as a label and the forest officer still treated villagers like criminals, it failed. The big lesson, beta, is that conservation is a *relationship*. It works when both sides bring respect. It fails when either side cheats.

**Child:** What about places like the Bhairodev Dakav Sonchuri — the one in Alwar where villagers just declared their own sanctuary?

**Mother:** That is the most interesting case of all. In **Alwar district**, five Rajasthani villages got tired of waiting for the government. They simply declared 1,200 hectares of forest as their own sanctuary, wrote their own rules against hunting, and started enforcing them. The Government of Rajasthan does not formally recognise it. The villagers don't care. The forest is recovering. The animals are returning. It is a kind of quiet rebellion: *we don't need your stamp to protect what is ours.* In a country as large and unequal as India, that may be the most realistic model of all — a thousand Bhairodev Sonchuris, declared by people who refuse to wait.

## What Can a Class 10 Student Actually Do?

**Child:** Maa, I am sitting in Mumbai. I am not a Bishnoi. I am not in Garhwal. What can I actually *do*?

**Mother:** This is the most honest question in the chapter, beta. And I want to be careful not to give you a fake answer. Let me give you three real ones.

**First — pay attention.** Most damage to forests is done in silence. A mining lease here, a denotification of a sanctuary boundary there, a quiet permission to a luxury resort inside a tiger corridor. If young people simply *read what is happening* and tell their friends and family, that itself is a small force. Subscribe to one good environmental newspaper or website. Read it weekly. Know which tigers were poached, which forests were cleared, which laws were diluted. Information is the first stage of protection.

**Second — change your own consumption a little.** The chapter makes a sharp point: rich Indians and city Indians consume more wood, paper, and electricity per person than poor villagers, but the poor get blamed for forest destruction. So in your own house, beta, ask: do we print everything we could read on a screen? Do we leave lights on in empty rooms? Do we buy furniture made from rare hardwoods that took 100 years to grow? These are not heroic actions, but they are honest ones. Real conservation begins with honest accounting of one's own footprint.

**Third — visit and notice.** Once a year, go to a national park, a sanctuary, a sacred grove. Not for an Instagram post, but to look at one tree slowly. Learn the names of five birds in your own neighbourhood. Watch a sunset from the same spot for one whole year and see how the trees change. You cannot protect what you cannot recognise. The Bishnois could die for the khejri because they *knew* the khejri. We mostly cannot tell a banyan from a peepal. That is where the loss begins.

**Child:** Anything bigger?

**Mother:** When you are older, beta, you can decide. You may become a forest officer, or a wildlife biologist, or a lawyer fighting environmental cases, or simply a citizen who votes for parties that take this seriously. But none of that depends on Class 10. What depends on Class 10 is whether you learn to *see* the forest at all — not as a holiday photo, but as a living, breathing, fragile thing that needs human friends. If by the end of this year you can name ten trees within walking distance of your house, you will already have done more than most adults around you.

**Child:** I think I'll start with the gulmohar outside my window.

**Mother:** That is the perfect place to begin, beta. Every great conservationist in India started by loving one tree.
