## What Makes Something a Resource?

**Child:** Maa, the textbook says coal is a "resource". But in my drawing book it's a black thing. How is it suddenly so important?

**Mother:** Sit down, beta. Coal *is* just a black rock. But think — what does Papa use to charge his phone? Electricity. Where does most of India's electricity come from?

**Child:** Power plants?

**Mother:** And the power plants burn coal. So that black rock is the reason your phone charges, the fan above us spins, the rice in the cooker is hot, and the train your cousin took last week ran on time. The rock has not changed. *Your relationship with it has changed.* That is what a "resource" really means — something becomes a resource the moment human beings need it, can use it, and choose to use it.

**Child:** So a thing is not a resource by itself?

**Mother:** No, beta. Think of the wind. The wind has been blowing over Gujarat for thousands of years. To my mother and my grandmother, it was just weather — it dried the clothes, it cooled the courtyard. Today the same wind turns those tall white turbines near Kutch and lights up cities. The wind didn't change. Our *technology* changed. Suddenly it became a resource. Even the petrol in Papa's car wasn't a resource a hundred and fifty years ago — it was a sticky puddle nobody wanted. Then someone invented the engine, and the whole world ran after that puddle.

**Child:** So tomorrow something we don't care about could become a resource?

**Mother:** Of course. Right now there is hydrogen in seawater. Enough hydrogen to power humanity forever. We just don't have the technology to harvest it cheaply yet. The day we do, the ocean will become the world's biggest fuel station. The geographer Erich Zimmermann wrote a beautiful line about this: "Resources are not, they become."

**Child:** That's a strange sentence.

**Mother:** It is the most important sentence in this chapter. Remember it.

## Why Are Some Resources Common Property and Some Private?

**Child:** Maa, when we visit Dadi's village, there is a pond. Anyone can wash clothes there, anyone can take water. But the field next to it belongs only to Chacha. Why is one shared and one not?

**Mother:** Because some resources are easier to share, and some are easier to fence. Land can be fenced — you draw a line, you put up a boundary stone, and now everyone knows where Chacha's field ends and the next one begins. A pond is harder. The water flows underneath. The fish move. If Chacha tried to own the pond and charge money, the whole village would object — because they all need that water. So traditionally, the pond is **community property**. Your geography book calls these "common resources" or **community-owned resources**.

**Child:** And the cremation ground? The grazing land?

**Mother:** Same thing, beta. The cremation ground belongs to everyone because everyone dies. The grazing land belongs to everyone because everyone has cattle. These are India's *commons* — shared lands and shared waters that no one owns and everyone uses.

**Child:** That sounds nice. So why is everyone worried about them?

**Mother:** Because the commons are disappearing. Builders eye them for apartments. The government takes them for highways. Wealthy farmers slowly extend their fields onto them. And when nobody owns something, no one fights to protect it. In our economic textbook this is called the "tragedy of the commons" — a pond everyone uses but no one cares for gets choked with weeds and garbage.

**Child:** What can be done?

**Mother:** The smart way is to give the commons to the *community* — to the gram panchayat, to a forest committee, to the village itself — and let the village protect what it owns. The **Joint Forest Management** programs in Odisha and West Bengal have done exactly this. When a village owns its forest, the village watches over it. The same principle works for ponds, for grazing lands, for water bodies. Ownership decides care.

**Child:** And the river? Who owns the river?

**Mother:** Now you ask the big question. The Ganga, the Yamuna, the Krishna — these are **national resources**. They belong to the entire country. So the entire country must protect them. The sea up to 12 nautical miles from the coast is national too. The deep sea beyond 200 nautical miles is international — it belongs to no country and to all countries. Antarctica belongs to no one. The Moon belongs to no one. The biggest, most important resources are the ones with no fence at all.

## What's So Special About Indian Soil?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter has eight kinds of soil. Why so many?

**Mother:** Because India is many countries pretending to be one, beta. Did you know that you can drive from Kashmir to Kanyakumari and pass through every kind of climate the planet has — Himalayan snow, Punjab plains, Thar desert, Deccan plateau, Western Ghats rainforest, Bay of Bengal coast? Each climate makes its own soil. Each soil grows its own crop. So eight kinds is not "too many" — it is what makes India able to feed 1.4 billion people from one country.

**Child:** Which one do we eat from the most?

**Mother:** **Alluvial soil**, beta. From Punjab to West Bengal, all the way across the Gangetic plain, that is one giant strip of alluvial soil — the silt that the Himalayan rivers carried down for millions of years. It covers about 40% of India and feeds about 60% of us. Every chapati you eat is alluvial wheat. Every spoon of rice in your tiffin is alluvial rice. The sugar in your tea is alluvial sugarcane.

**Child:** And the soil under our city?

**Mother:** Tell me where we live, beta — what does the ground look like?

**Child:** Black. Dark, sometimes cracks in summer.

**Mother:** That's **regur**. The "black soil." It comes from lava that erupted from the earth 65 million years ago, when the dinosaurs were still alive. The lava cooled into basalt, and over millions of years the basalt weathered into this dark, deep, sticky soil. It is *famous* — Maharashtra cotton grows on it, so does Gujarat cotton. The reason the mills of Mumbai and Ahmedabad became so big is that the cotton was right outside the door. The soil decides which industry rises in which city.

**Child:** What about the red soil I saw in Karnataka?

**Mother:** That is **red and yellow soil**, found across the peninsular plateau. The colour comes from iron oxide — the same chemistry that makes rust orange. Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Odisha, parts of Andhra Pradesh — they all sit on red soil. It is not as fertile as alluvial, but with irrigation and a little fertiliser, it grows ragi, groundnut, pulses, millets.

**Child:** And the soil in Kerala when we visited Periyar last year?

**Mother:** **Laterite**. The word comes from Latin *later*, meaning brick — because in old days the soil was actually cut into bricks for building. The hot, heavy rains of Kerala wash out all the silica from the soil, leaving behind iron and aluminium. The result is a porous, low-nutrient soil. But the funny thing — and this is the magic — when laterite is manured, it grows the most beautiful cash crops in India. Tea, coffee, rubber, cashew. All those hill plantations of Munnar, Wayanad, Coorg sit on laterite.

**Child:** And the desert?

**Mother:** **Arid soils**. Sandy, salty, low in humus. People assumed for centuries that nothing would grow there. Then engineers built the **Indira Gandhi Canal** and suddenly wheat and barley grew in Rajasthan. The desert turned green where the water reached. Geography rewards courage — but only when you also respect what the land was already doing.

## Why Is Soil Erosion Such a Big Deal?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter has a scary line — soil takes a thousand years to form one centimetre. Is that really true?

**Mother:** Yes, beta. Soil is not the same as dirt. Soil is rock that has been weathered by sun, rain, frost, and life — by bacteria, by worms, by insect droppings, by the slow decay of dead leaves and dead animals. A single centimetre of healthy soil contains *billions* of microorganisms. And to build that centimetre, nature takes between two hundred and a thousand years. Sometimes more.

**Child:** Then why are we losing it so fast?

**Mother:** Because we are doing four things wrong at the same time, beta. **First**, we are cutting forests. Trees hold soil with their roots — when the roots are gone, the next monsoon carries the soil straight to the sea. **Second**, we are over-grazing. Too many cattle and goats nibble grass too quickly; the soil compacts; the rain can't soak in; it runs off and takes the soil with it. **Third**, we plough up and down the slope instead of along the contour. Each furrow then becomes a little river that drains the field. **Fourth**, we mine — open-cast coal in Jharkhand, iron in Chhattisgarh, bauxite in Odisha — and entire hillsides go away in ten years.

**Child:** Can the soil come back?

**Mother:** Not in our lifetime. That is what makes it so dangerous. You can plant a new tree in twenty years. You can refill a pond in two seasons. But you cannot grow new soil. So the Chambal ravines in Madhya Pradesh, where rivers have cut deep gullies into the land — that land is essentially gone. Tens of thousands of hectares.

**Child:** So what do farmers do?

**Mother:** They do what farmers in hilly Himachal and Sikkim have done for centuries — **terrace farming**. They cut flat steps into the hillside so the rain has to step down slowly. They do **contour ploughing** — making each furrow along the natural curve of the land, not across it, so each row becomes a small dam. They do **strip cropping** — alternating dense grass strips with grain strips, so the grass slows down both wind and water. They plant **shelter belts** of trees along the edges of fields to break the wind.

**Child:** Does it work?

**Mother:** It works where it's done. In the Indira Gandhi Canal area, the shelter belts have stabilised soil that was blowing away every summer. In the Western Ghats, terrace farming has kept villages on the hillside for generations. But it has to be done village by village, farm by farm. There is no single dramatic fix. Soil conservation is a thousand small projects in a thousand villages, all done patiently.

**Child:** That doesn't sound like the kind of work that wins elections.

**Mother:** No, beta. That is exactly the problem.

## Is Sustainable Development Just a Fancy Word?

**Child:** Maa, every time I open the newspaper, somebody is talking about "sustainable development." But what does it actually mean?

**Mother:** It means a promise, beta. A promise we make to the people who come after us. The cleanest definition came from a Norwegian Prime Minister named Gro Harlem Brundtland in 1987. She said: "Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." Read it slowly. *Without compromising the ability of future generations.*

**Child:** So the children who come after me?

**Mother:** Yes. And their children. Sustainable development means that when *you* are a mother and your child is in Class 10, there must still be groundwater in Punjab, fish in the Bay of Bengal, monsoon over the Western Ghats, and a sky over Delhi that you can breathe. We must grow the economy *and* leave these things behind. Not one or the other. Both.

**Child:** Is that even possible?

**Mother:** It is hard. It means turning off the lights when you leave a room. It means not buying things you don't need. It means a government choosing solar power over a new coal mine, even when the coal mine is more profitable today. It means a farmer growing pulses instead of rice when the water table is dropping. It means a city not paving over its last lake. Every one of these decisions costs someone something *now* — and saves someone something *later*. That is the trade.

**Child:** Did India sign anything about this?

**Mother:** In 1992, in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, more than 170 countries — including India — met at the **Earth Summit**. They signed conventions on climate change and biodiversity, and they wrote a master plan called **Agenda 21**. Each city, each village was asked to write its own "Local Agenda 21" — a small plan to clean its water, plant its trees, recycle its waste. Then in 2015 came the Paris Agreement — every country committed to slowing climate change. India is one of the most affected countries, and one of the most committed signatories.

**Child:** But Gandhi-ji said it long before, no?

**Mother:** You remembered. He said: "There is enough for everybody's need but not for everybody's greed." That is the Indian way of saying sustainable development. The Chipko movement of the 1970s — women in Uttarakhand hugging trees to stop logging — was sustainable development. The watershed work at Ralegan Siddhi by Anna Hazare was sustainable development. Sundarlal Bahuguna's campaign against the Tehri dam was sustainable development. We don't need to import this idea, beta. We just need to remember our own.

**Child:** It sounds simple when you say it like that.

**Mother:** It is simple. It is just not easy.

## What Should I Take Away from This?

**Child:** Maa, exams are next week. If you had to tell me only three things from this chapter, what would they be?

**Mother:** Listen carefully, beta. **One** — a resource is not a thing, it is a relationship. The wind became a resource only when we invented the turbine. The petroleum became a resource only when we built the engine. So when you look at the world, ask: what is here that is *not yet* a resource because we don't have the tools or the will? That is the future.

**Child:** Two?

**Mother:** **Two** — India's soil is its real wealth, more than its minerals. Alluvial in the north feeds 60% of us. Regur in the Deccan grows our cotton. Laterite in the Western Ghats grows our tea and coffee. Red soil grows our pulses. These are not just words in a textbook — they are the reason the food is on your plate. Treat the section on soils as a love letter to India.

**Child:** And three?

**Mother:** **Three** — sustainable development is not a fancy phrase. It is a promise to the Class 10 student of 2056. Every time you waste water, every time you throw away food, every time you leave a light on, you are breaking that promise. Every time you save, you keep it. You are not powerless, beta. You are a tiny, daily, important part of the answer.

**Child:** That's a lot to remember.

**Mother:** No, beta. It's just one thing said three ways. *Resources are not gifts to us. They are loans from those who come after us.*

**Child:** Maa…

**Mother:** Yes?

**Child:** I am going to switch off the tubelight in the kitchen.

**Mother:** Good. Begin there.
