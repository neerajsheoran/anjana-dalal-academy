## What Even Is a Resource?

Look around the room you are sitting in. The cotton in your shirt, the wood in your desk, the iron in the ceiling fan, the glass in the window, the data running through your phone — every single one of these started as something that nobody owned. Cotton was once a wild plant. Iron was once an ugly streak of red ore inside a hill. Even the silica in glass was just sand. Each of these things became a **resource** the moment three conditions came together: a human being needed it, a human being could *reach* it with the technology of the day, and a society agreed it was *acceptable* to use it.

This is the textbook definition Indian geographers borrow from the scholar **Erich Zimmermann**: "Resources are not, they become." A thing is not born as a resource. It becomes one when **human need**, **available technology**, and **cultural acceptability** all line up.

Consider three quick examples. **Petroleum** existed under the deserts of West Asia for millions of years before anyone touched it. It wasn't a resource — it was a smelly puddle in the rocks. Then in the nineteenth century the internal combustion engine was invented, and suddenly oil became one of the most valuable resources on Earth. **Uranium** was just another silvery metal until physicists in the 1940s discovered how to split its atom; only then did it become a resource. And in India, **wind** has blown over Gujarat and Tamil Nadu for thousands of years — but it became a resource only when modern turbine technology made it economical to harvest. Until then, it was just weather.

So when this chapter talks about "resources," it does not mean a fixed list of things. It means anything in our environment that satisfies a human need — provided we have the tools to use it and the social will to do so. This is why the same coal seam that was a "resource" in 1950 is now becoming a *problem* in 2026: technology has changed, climate science has changed, and what society finds acceptable has changed.

## Why Classification Matters

Geographers classify resources for the same reason a doctor classifies medicines — because each type behaves differently, and confusing them can do serious damage. The NCERT groups resources four ways: **by origin**, **by exhaustibility**, **by ownership**, and **by status of development**.

**By origin**, resources are either **biotic** or **abiotic**.

- **Biotic** resources come from the living world — humans, livestock, fish, forests, crops, even bacteria in the soil. The Sundarbans tigers, Punjab's wheat fields, and the cattle of Rajasthan are all biotic resources.
- **Abiotic** resources come from the non-living world — rocks, minerals, water, air. The iron ore of Jharkhand, the limestone of Rajasthan, the Yamuna's flowing water are abiotic.

The line is sometimes blurry. Coal, for instance, is technically abiotic (it is rock), but it was *formed* from buried forests millions of years ago — so its origin is biological. Geographers still classify it as abiotic because we use it as a mineral.

**By exhaustibility**, resources are either **renewable** or **non-renewable**.

- **Renewable** resources can be replenished by natural processes within a human timescale — solar energy, wind, flowing water in rivers, the air, and (with care) the forests and the soil. The sun will not run out for billions of years; the Ganga keeps flowing as long as it rains in the Himalayas.
- **Non-renewable** resources took millions of years to form and cannot be replaced within a human lifetime. Coal, petroleum, natural gas, and most metallic minerals fall here. India's coal reserves, formed during the Carboniferous period over 300 million years ago, will not regenerate when we exhaust them.

A useful sub-classification: some renewable resources are **flow** resources (sunlight, wind — must be used as they arrive or they are gone) and others are **stock** resources (forests, soil — can be saved up but only if used at a rate that allows regeneration). The Aravalli forests in Rajasthan are renewable in principle, but if cattle graze them faster than trees can grow, they will be gone forever in practice. Renewability is not a guarantee; it is a *budget*.

**By ownership**, resources are individual, community, national, or international.

- **Individual** resources are privately owned — your family's house, a farmer's land, the shop your uncle runs in town.
- **Community** resources are owned and used by the whole village or neighbourhood — the village pond, the grazing ground, the community well, the cremation ground. These are India's *commons*, and they are vanishing fast.
- **National** resources belong to the entire country — minerals under the ground, water in major rivers, roads, railways, and territorial waters up to **12 nautical miles** from the coast.
- **International** resources lie beyond any one country's control. Ocean waters beyond 200 nautical miles, Antarctica, and the deep seabed are governed by international agreements.

**By status of development**, resources are potential, developed, stock, or reserve.

- **Potential** resources are surveyed but not yet used. Rajasthan and Gujarat have enormous solar and wind potential that is only now being tapped. Ladakh has uranium deposits geologists know about but India has not yet exploited.
- **Developed** resources are surveyed *and* in use, with quantity and quality assessed and technology available. India's Punjab–Haryana alluvial farmland is a developed resource at full intensity.
- **Stock** resources are known to exist and meet the need, but we lack the technology to use them. Hydrogen in seawater is the textbook example — there is enough hydrogen in the oceans to power humanity forever, but the technology to harvest it economically is not yet mature.
- **Reserve** resources are a subset of stock — known *and* technologically usable, but kept aside for future use. The water in our dams above the dead storage line, the forests we have placed under protection, are reserve resources.

Why does this four-way grid matter? Because India's planning depends on it. You cannot tax a community pond like private land. You cannot treat a coal mine like a wind farm. You cannot plan a future based on resources you don't yet have technology for. Classification is not a definition exercise — it is the language of policy.

## Why Resource Planning Matters in India

India has a strange geography. Some parts are *resource-rich*. Jharkhand has coal, iron ore, mica, copper. Chhattisgarh has bauxite. Rajasthan has uranium, solar potential, and gemstones. Some parts are *resource-poor*. The state of Bihar, despite Jharkhand being carved out of it, has rich soil but few minerals and limited industrial development. The Himalayan states have abundant water but limited cropland. The Lakshadweep islands have rich marine resources but tiny landmasses with poor mineral wealth.

This uneven distribution is why **resource planning** — the careful identification, development, and use of resources — became the backbone of Indian planning from the **First Five Year Plan (1951)** onwards. The economist **P. C. Mahalanobis** designed the Second Plan around heavy industries and resource development. Even today, the **NITI Aayog** continues this tradition with sector-wise plans.

The textbook describes resource planning as a **three-stage process**:

1. **Identification and inventory** — surveying, mapping, and quantitative measurement of resources across the country. The **Geological Survey of India**, the **Forest Survey of India**, the **Central Ground Water Board**, and the **National Remote Sensing Centre** all do this work. They produce the maps and the numbers without which planning is blind.
2. **Building a planning structure** — creating the institutions, the laws, the technology, the skilled workforce, and the financial backing to actually use those resources. This is why India built engineering colleges, set up public sector mines, drafted the Mines and Minerals Act, and invested in irrigation departments.
3. **Aligning resource use with overall development plans** — making sure that the way we extract resources matches our larger goals of equity, sustainability, and self-reliance. A copper mine should not just produce copper; it should employ local people, repair its environment, and support downstream industries.

India has had stunning successes — the **Green Revolution** of the late 1960s used soil, water, fertiliser, and high-yielding seeds together to make a hungry country a wheat-surplus exporter. But there have also been costly mistakes — over-pumping of groundwater in Punjab, mining-driven displacement of tribal communities in Jharkhand and Odisha, and oil import dependence that now eats more than 80% of our crude needs every year.

Resource planning works only when the **3 Cs** are present: **conscience** (the will to plan), **capacity** (the institutions to execute), and **continuity** (the long-term political stability that lets a plan run more than five years). Where any one of these is missing, even rich resources are wasted.

## The Story of Land

The most fundamental resource of all is **land**. Of India's total geographical area — about **328.7 million hectares** — we have land-use data for only about **93%**. That gap itself is a problem; you cannot plan what you have not measured.

The official classification breaks land use into nine categories. The three that matter most are **net sown area** (the land actually cultivated in a year), **forests**, and **land not available for cultivation** (built-up cities, deserts, snow-covered Himalayas, and barren rocks).

India's **net sown area** is about **46% of total reporting area** — meaning nearly half the country is under the plough. This is one of the highest figures in the world; the global average is closer to 12%. We farm intensively because we have to feed 1.4 billion people on a landmass smaller than Brazil's.

But India's **forest cover** is below the **33%** target set in the National Forest Policy of 1952. The actual figure is closer to 23–24% if we count both forests and tree cover, but only about 22% if we are strict about it. The shortfall is environmentally serious — forests anchor the soil, regulate rainfall, store carbon, and shelter wildlife.

The land-use pattern is also unequal across states. **Punjab and Haryana** have over 80% of their land under cultivation; **Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh** have less than 10%. **Madhya Pradesh** has the largest forest cover by area; **Lakshadweep** has almost no agricultural land. Geography decides much of this; policy can adjust some of it.

There are also problems within the farming land we have. **Land degradation** — through over-cultivation, over-grazing, water-logging, salinity from canal irrigation, and chemical contamination — has affected an estimated **130 million hectares** of Indian land. Reclaiming that land is a national priority; afforestation, contour farming, control of grazing, and reduced chemical use are the remedies.

## India's Soils — The Crown Jewel

Of all India's resources, **soil** is perhaps the most precious and the most quietly threatened. Soil is the thin upper crust of the Earth — only about **15 cm to 1 metre deep** in most places — and it takes between **200 and 1,000 years** to form just one centimetre of it through the natural weathering of rock, mixing with humus from decayed plants and animals, and the slow work of microorganisms. Once destroyed, it does not return in our lifetime.

India is a soil-blessed country. The Indian Council of Agricultural Research recognises **eight major types** based on origin, colour, texture, and chemistry. The big six that fill most NCERT diagrams are alluvial, black, red and yellow, laterite, arid, and forest/mountain soils.

### Alluvial Soil — The Soil That Feeds India

The **alluvial soil** of the Indo-Gangetic plain is India's agricultural workhorse. It covers about **40% of the country** — the Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal, and most of Assam — and supports nearly **60% of India's population**. It is fertile because it is *young*: it has been deposited over millennia by the Himalayan rivers — the Ganga, Yamuna, Brahmaputra, Indus and their many tributaries — bringing down silt rich in potash, phosphoric acid, and lime.

Alluvial soil is classified into two sub-types based on age. **Khadar** is the new alluvium — the silt deposited each year by river floods on the lowlands closest to the river. It is finer, more fertile, and renewed annually. **Bhangar** is the old alluvium — found on the higher ground above the flood plain, with kankar nodules of calcium carbonate, and slightly less fertile but still productive.

Alluvial soil grows wheat in Punjab and western UP, rice in West Bengal and eastern UP, sugarcane in UP and Bihar, cotton in Haryana, and jute in West Bengal. The rice bowl, the wheat basket, and the sugar belt of India all sit on this single soil.

### Black Soil (Regur) — The Cotton King

The **black soil**, called **regur** in many regions, covers the **Deccan trap** — Maharashtra, parts of Gujarat, western Madhya Pradesh, northern Karnataka, and Andhra Pradesh. It was formed from the cooling of basalt lava that erupted from the Earth's interior 65 million years ago — the same volcanic event that may have wiped out the dinosaurs.

Regur is *deep* (often 1 to 2 metres), *dark* (almost black when moist), and *clayey*. It contains lime, iron, magnesia, alumina, potash, and humus. What makes it special is its ability to **hold moisture**. In summer, the soil dries and develops deep cracks that aerate it from below; when the monsoon arrives, the cracks close as the soil swells. This self-ploughing behaviour means it conserves moisture for months — perfect for **cotton**, which is why the textile industries of Mumbai, Surat, and Ahmedabad grew up on regur.

Beyond cotton, regur grows **jowar (sorghum)**, **bajra (pearl millet)**, sugarcane, oilseeds, tobacco, and citrus fruits. Maharashtra's cotton, Gujarat's groundnut, and Madhya Pradesh's soybean all owe their existence to this soil.

### Red and Yellow Soils

The **red and yellow soils** cover much of the eastern and southern peninsula — Odisha, Chhattisgarh, the southern Gangetic plain, parts of Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, and the middle Ganga valley. They are formed by the weathering of crystalline igneous rocks (granite and gneiss) under low rainfall conditions. Their red colour comes from the diffusion of **iron oxide**; when found in hydrated form, the same soil looks yellow.

These soils are sandy or loamy, low in nitrogen, phosphate, and humus, but with proper irrigation and fertiliser they support **groundnut, ragi, potato, maize, pulses, and millets**. Karnataka's ragi-growing belt and Tamil Nadu's groundnut tracts sit on red soil.

### Laterite Soil — The Hill Soil

**Laterite** comes from the Latin word *later*, meaning brick — these soils were once cut into bricks for building. Laterite forms in regions of **high temperature and heavy rainfall**, where intense weathering and leaching wash away the silica and leave behind iron and aluminium oxides. The result is a coarse, porous, low-humus soil.

Laterite is found across the western coast (especially Karnataka, Kerala, and Goa), the Eastern Ghats of Odisha and Andhra Pradesh, the Western Ghats hills, and small patches of Assam and Madhya Pradesh. Although low in nutrients, when properly **manured and irrigated**, laterite supports a surprising range of cash crops: **cashew** in Kerala and Karnataka, **tea and coffee** in the Western Ghats and Eastern Ghats hill stations, **rubber** in Kerala, and **areca nut** in coastal Karnataka.

The hill stations of Munnar, Wayanad, Coorg, and Coonoor — all those misty plantation landscapes — sit on laterite.

### Arid Soils

In the deserts of Rajasthan, the Rann of Kutch in Gujarat, and the dry parts of Haryana, the **arid soils** are sandy, light-coloured (red to brown), salty, and lacking in humus and moisture. Below the upper layer, a hard *kankar* crust of calcium content restricts the infiltration of water.

Yet arid soils are not barren. When given irrigation — as in the **Indira Gandhi Canal Project** of western Rajasthan — they can grow **wheat, barley, millets, gram, and oilseeds**. The desert blooms; ancient camel country becomes a wheat field. The same canal has also brought salinity problems, however, reminding us that no resource policy is free of trade-offs.

### Forest and Mountain Soils

The **forest soils** of the Himalayas, the Western Ghats hills, the Eastern Ghats, and the Andaman and Nicobar hills are formed in regions of heavy rainfall under forest cover. Their character changes with altitude: loamy and silty on the valley sides, coarse and gravelly on the upper slopes. The soils of the Himalayan valleys grow apples in Himachal Pradesh and Kashmir, oranges and pears in Uttarakhand, large cardamom in Sikkim, and tea in Darjeeling and Assam.

## Soil Erosion — The Quiet Catastrophe

If soil takes a thousand years to form one centimetre, what would you call it if a single monsoon shower can wash a centimetre away? You would call it a crisis. **Soil erosion** is exactly that — the loss of soil cover faster than it can regenerate. It is responsible for vast tracts of degraded land in India.

Erosion has natural causes — wind, water, glaciers — but in modern India the human causes are bigger.

- **Deforestation** strips the protective vegetation cover. The tree roots that hold the soil together are gone; the next rain takes the soil with it.
- **Over-grazing** by cattle and goats removes grass and compacts the soil so water cannot soak in.
- **Faulty farming methods** — ploughing up and down a slope instead of along the contour — turn furrows into tiny canals that channel water and soil downhill.
- **Mining**, especially open-cast mining for coal and iron ore in Jharkhand, Odisha, and Chhattisgarh, removes whole hillsides in a single decade.
- **Industrial effluents** and chemical fertilisers leach into the soil and degrade its structure.

The two visible forms are **gully erosion**, where running water cuts deep ravines into the land (the Chambal ravines of Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan are India's largest), and **sheet erosion**, where rain washes away a thin film of soil across a wide area — quieter, slower, but cumulatively more damaging. **Wind erosion** dominates in arid regions and the Thar, where dust storms strip away the topsoil.

Then there are the human-made forms — **salinity** in canal-irrigated lands of Punjab, Haryana, and western Rajasthan, where rising water tables bring up dissolved salts that crystallise on the surface; and **water-logging** in the same regions, where over-irrigation makes the land too wet to crop.

## Soil Conservation — Holding the Land Together

There is no single fix for soil erosion. The remedies depend on the geography and the cause. The NCERT highlights a handful that every Class 10 student should know.

- **Contour ploughing** — ploughing along the natural contour lines of a slope, so each furrow becomes a small barrier holding water and soil. Used in the Western Ghats and the hilly tracts of central India.
- **Terrace farming** — cutting flat steps into a steep hillside so each terrace catches the rain. The hill agriculture of Sikkim, Meghalaya, Uttarakhand, and Himachal Pradesh depends on it.
- **Strip cropping** — alternating strips of densely planted crops (like grass or pulses) with strips of grain crops, so the dense strips slow the wind and water and trap the soil.
- **Shelter belts** — rows of trees planted along the edges of fields and across deserts to break the wind. The shelter belts planted along the **Indira Gandhi Canal** have helped stabilise the soil in Rajasthan.
- **Afforestation** and the **Joint Forest Management** model in which forest departments work with local communities to replant degraded forests.
- **Ban on grazing** in degraded areas to allow grass to regrow.

These solutions all share a feature: they are local, slow, and need the cooperation of the people who live on the land. The lesson is that soil conservation is not a single government project — it is a thousand small projects in a thousand villages.

## Sustainable Development — A Promise to the Next Class 10 Student

In **1987**, a commission led by the Norwegian Prime Minister **Gro Harlem Brundtland** released a report called *Our Common Future*. It gave us the most quoted definition in environmental policy: **sustainable development** is "development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."

It is, in plain words, a promise. The Class 10 student writing this exam in 2026 promises the Class 10 student of 2056 that there will still be groundwater under Punjab, fish in the Bay of Bengal, monsoon over the Western Ghats, and breathable air over Delhi. We have to grow our economy *and* leave these things behind.

This idea reached international action at the **United Nations Conference on Environment and Development** — the **Earth Summit** held in **Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, in June 1992**. Over **170 countries** signed declarations on climate change and biodiversity. The Summit produced **Agenda 21**, a programme to combat environmental damage, poverty, and disease through global cooperation, and a smaller "**Local Agenda 21**" that asks every local government to build its own sustainability plan with citizens.

Long before Brundtland, India had a leader who said much the same thing in plainer language. **Mahatma Gandhi** observed: "There is enough for everybody's need but not for everybody's greed." The **Gandhian conservation ethic** — frugality, local self-reliance, distrust of heavy industry, respect for the land — is woven through Indian environmental thinking. The Chipko Movement of the 1970s, Sundarlal Bahuguna's anti-Tehri campaigning, Anna Hazare's watershed work at Ralegan Siddhi all draw on it. Indians do not need to import the idea of sustainability. We need to remember our own.

What sustainable development demands is hard. It demands that we use less coal even when more coal is profitable. It demands that we recharge our groundwater even when the next harvest is on the line. It demands that the Class 10 textbook of 2056 still has forests to write about. The conventions signed at Rio — on climate change and biodiversity — and the follow-up agreements like the **Paris Agreement of 2015** are the world's clumsy attempts to write down such promises. India is one of the most affected and most committed signatories. The work is far from finished.

## Pulling It Together

A resource is not a thing — it is a relationship between us, our technology, and our culture. India has rich and uneven endowments: fertile alluvial plains, cotton-growing regur, hill laterite, mineral-bearing peninsular plateaus. We have classified them and built planning around them, but we have also pushed many of them past safe limits. Soil — the most life-giving resource of all — is the most quietly endangered. Sustainable development is not a slogan; it is the only honest answer to the children who will follow us. The chapter you have just read is, in the end, a request that this generation does its part.

## Apply What You've Learned

### Three India Stories

**1. The Day Punjab's Tubewells Started Crying.** In the 1960s, the Green Revolution turned Punjab into India's wheat basket. By 2020, Punjab's farmers had drilled over **1.4 million tubewells** and were pumping groundwater at a rate that even four monsoons could not replace. Some districts now see the water table dropping by half a metre every year. The Bhakra dam, which once filled to brimming each monsoon, has periodic dry spells. The state that fed India is now begging it to grow less rice and more pulses. A resource we thought was endless was simply not budgeted.

**2. Sikkim, the Organic State.** In 2003, the small Himalayan state of **Sikkim** decided to become India's first fully **organic state**. It banned chemical pesticides and fertilisers, retrained farmers, certified every farm, and persuaded markets to pay a premium. By 2016, Sikkim achieved its goal — all **75,000 hectares** of farmland were officially organic. Production briefly fell, then recovered with healthier soil. Tourism boomed because foreign visitors wanted to see the model. Soil quality improved, costs went down, and exports grew. Small state, big experiment, big lesson.

**3. The Marathwada Drought.** Maharashtra's Marathwada region — once a sea of black regur growing cotton — has faced repeated droughts from 2012 onwards. The reasons are layered: over-cropping of water-thirsty sugarcane on land meant for jowar, falling groundwater from too many bore wells, deforested catchments, and warming summers. The **Jalyukt Shivar Abhiyan** (Water-Filled Field Mission) launched in 2014 used contour bunds, percolation tanks, and farm ponds to recharge water. Some villages turned green again. Others have not recovered. The fight is ongoing — and it is the fight of the regur soil itself to keep doing what it has done for 65 million years.

### Do It Yourself

Pick **one** of these three projects this week.

- **Soil survey of a farm or yard within 2 km of home.** Take a small spade. Dig three small holes, each about 30 cm deep, in three different spots. Note the **colour** (red, black, yellow, brown?), **texture** (sandy, loamy, clayey?), **moisture**, and what is growing there. Match it against the six major soil types in this chapter. Take photos. Draw a small map. Decide for yourself what soil your locality has.
- **A household water budget for one week.** Note every litre your family uses — for bathing, washing, cooking, cleaning, plants, pets. Compare it with the national per-capita average of about **135 litres per person per day**. Where are you using more? Where less? Could you cut by 20% without anyone noticing?
- **Map the resources within a 1 km walk from home.** Note every well, pond, garden, tree, field, shop, building, road. Mark whether each is **biotic or abiotic**, **renewable or non-renewable**, and whether it is **individual, community, or national** property. You will discover that even an urban street is full of resources.
