## Why a Country Needs Factories

Open the box your phone came in. Read the label on your shirt. Look under the wheel cap of any car on your street. Somewhere, in small print, you will find the word **"manufactured"** followed by a place — Pune, Sriperumbudur, Tirupur, Noida, Jamshedpur, Coimbatore. That single line tells you something powerful — somewhere a factory turned raw cotton, raw iron, raw silicon, raw plastic into the thing you now use.

That act of turning a low-value raw material into a high-value finished product is what we mean by **manufacturing**. A farmer grows cotton — that is primary activity. A mill spins it into yarn, weaves it into cloth, dyes it, cuts it, stitches it into a shirt — that is **secondary activity**, or manufacturing. The cotton may have been worth ten rupees in the field. The shirt sells for five hundred. The four hundred and ninety rupees of extra value was added by manufacturing — by machines, designers, workers, supply chains and brands.

Economists call this **value addition**. It is the single most important reason why countries care so deeply about their manufacturing sector. A country that only sells raw materials — cotton, iron ore, bauxite, leather — earns very little. A country that processes those raw materials into clothes, cars, aluminium foil and shoes earns many times more. The richest countries of the world — Germany, Japan, South Korea, the United States, and increasingly China — became rich largely because their manufacturing sectors are huge, sophisticated, and export-driven.

India's economic story since Independence has been one of trying to build manufacturing strength. The phrase **"Make in India"**, launched as a government campaign in 2014, is just the latest in a long line — Nehru's Five Year Plans focused on heavy industry, the 1991 reforms opened up consumer industries, and today we are pushing semiconductor fabs, electric vehicles, and solar panels.

This chapter asks four questions. **What kinds of industries does India have?** **Why are they located where they are?** **What problems do they create — especially pollution?** **And how do we balance the need to grow with the need to protect what is left of our air, water, and soil?**

## Why Manufacturing Matters for Development

The Indian economy has three sectors. **Primary** — agriculture, mining, fishing. **Secondary** — manufacturing. **Tertiary** — services like banking, retail, IT, healthcare. A healthy economy needs all three. But manufacturing has a special role.

**It creates large-scale employment.** A factory employing five thousand workers in a small town transforms that town. Houses are rented, shops open, schools fill up, buses run. Agriculture in India still employs nearly half our workforce but produces only a fifth of our GDP. Many of those farm hands are underemployed — they have only seasonal work and earn very little. Manufacturing is the only sector that can absorb tens of millions of such workers into steady wage employment.

**It modernises agriculture.** When you walk through a village in Punjab today, you will see tractors, threshers, drip irrigation pipes, fertiliser sacks and pump sets. None of these are agricultural products — they are industrial products being used by agriculture. The chemical plant that makes urea, the tractor factory in Pune, the PVC pipe maker in Gujarat, the pump-set assembler in Coimbatore — these are all secondary-sector industries supplying the primary sector. Without them, Indian farming would still be where it was in 1950.

**It brings in foreign exchange.** When India exports cotton yarn, machine tools, generic medicines or auto components, it earns dollars. Those dollars pay for the crude oil, gold, electronics and edible oil we import. A country that only exports raw materials cannot easily balance its imports — it has to keep borrowing. A country that exports manufactured goods is buying its way into stability.

**It reduces regional disparity.** This was the deepest idea behind India's planning era. If you build a steel plant in tribal Chhattisgarh or a fertiliser factory in eastern UP, you bring roads, electricity, schools, hospitals and jobs to a region that would otherwise stay backward. Bhilai, Bokaro, Rourkela, Durgapur, Visakhapatnam — these are all cities that grew around public-sector industries placed precisely where economic development was lagging.

The reverse is also true. A country whose factories shut down loses jobs, tax revenue, technical skill, and pride. That is why every Indian government — across political parties — talks about reviving manufacturing. We have made big strides in services. The challenge is to do the same in factories.

## How Industries Are Classified

There are thousands of industries in India, from tiny one-room workshops to giant integrated plants. To understand them we need to group them. Geographers use **four main ways** of classifying industries.

**By source of raw material.** This is the most basic division.

**Agro-based industries** use products of agriculture as raw material. **Cotton textiles** use raw cotton. **Jute mills** use raw jute. **Sugar mills** use sugarcane. **Vegetable oil mills** use mustard, groundnut, soyabean. **Tea factories** use green tea leaves. These industries are usually located near the farms that grow the crop, because the raw material is bulky and the transport cost would be huge otherwise.

**Mineral-based industries** use ores and minerals. **Iron and steel** uses iron ore, coking coal and limestone. **Aluminium smelting** uses bauxite. **Cement** uses limestone. **Petrochemicals** use crude oil. These industries are usually located near the mines, the ports, or — for power-hungry industries like aluminium — near cheap electricity.

**By their main role.** Some industries make goods that other factories use. Others make things you and I buy in the market.

**Basic or key industries** supply their products as raw materials to other industries. **Iron and steel** is the classic example — its output is used by car factories, shipyards, railways, bridge-builders, household-utensil makers, defence units. **Copper smelting** and **aluminium smelting** are also key industries. Without them, no other industry can run.

**Consumer industries** make things for direct use — **sugar, toothpaste, paper, fans, sewing machines, soap, shampoo, instant noodles**. Their product reaches you without further industrial processing.

**By capital investment.** This matters for government policy.

**Small-scale industries** are defined by the upper limit on investment in fixed assets. The limit has changed many times over the decades. They get tax benefits, easier loans, and reserved categories of products. **Large-scale industries** invest crores in plant and machinery — they include all the big mills, steel plants and car factories.

**By ownership.** Who owns the company decides how it behaves.

**Public sector** — owned and operated by the government. **BHEL** (Bharat Heavy Electricals), **SAIL** (Steel Authority of India), **HAL** (Hindustan Aeronautics), **ONGC**. These were set up to build national strategic capability.

**Private sector** — owned and operated by individuals or companies. **TISCO** (Tata Steel), **Bajaj Auto**, **Dabur**, **Reliance**, **Hindalco**. They run on profit and shareholder logic.

**Joint sector** — jointly owned by government and private parties. **Oil India Limited** (OIL) is a classic example.

**Cooperative sector** — owned by the producers or workers themselves, who pool their resources and share profits. The **sugar industry in Maharashtra** is the world's biggest example — farmers who grow sugarcane jointly own the mills that crush it. The **coir industry in Kerala** is another.

**By the bulk and weight of raw material.**

**Heavy industries** use bulky raw materials and produce bulky finished goods — iron and steel, cement, shipbuilding. **Light industries** use light inputs and make light products — electrical goods, watches, sewing needles.

These categories overlap. Tata Steel is a heavy, basic, private-sector, large-scale, mineral-based industry. A khadi spinning unit is a light, consumer, cooperative-sector, small-scale, agro-based industry. The classification is not a rigid box — it is a tool to help us think.

## Agro-Based Industries — Cotton, Jute, Sugar

The single largest agro-based industry in India is **textiles**. It is also the second largest employer after agriculture. From the cotton farmer to the boll-plucker to the ginner to the spinner to the weaver to the dyer to the tailor — one industry supports tens of millions of livelihoods.

**Cotton textiles** have been India's signature industry for more than two thousand years. The first modern textile mill came up in **Mumbai in 1854**, and Mumbai still has the country's largest concentration of cotton mills — a legacy of being in the cotton belt of Maharashtra and Gujarat, with port access for exports, moist climate for spinning thread (dry thread snaps), and a long history of mercantile capital from the China trade. **Ahmedabad** is the second great centre. Together these two cities employed lakhs of mill workers for over a century.

Cotton textile production has three stages — **spinning** raw cotton into yarn, **weaving** yarn into cloth, and **finishing** (dyeing, printing, packing). India produces world-class yarn — our spinning sector is highly mechanised and competes globally. But our weaving is much more decentralised. About two-thirds of fabric in India is still woven on **powerlooms** in small clusters (Bhiwandi near Mumbai, Surat in Gujarat, Erode in Tamil Nadu), with handloom (in Banaras, Kanchipuram, Sualkuchi) and mills taking the rest. This is why Indian cloth often does not match international quality — the world-class yarn from our spinning sector is woven on second-rate looms in clusters that cannot use it fully.

The handloom sector — **khadi** included — provides employment to many weavers in their own homes. This is why Mahatma Gandhi made spinning yarn and weaving khadi central to the freedom movement. Every metre of khadi cloth meant a few rupees in a poor weaver's hand, in his own village, instead of going to a Manchester mill or a Mumbai factory.

**Jute textiles.** India is the world's largest producer of raw jute and jute goods. Bangladesh slightly edges us as an exporter, but our internal market is the biggest. **Almost all the jute mills are along the Hugli river in West Bengal**, in a narrow belt around Kolkata — at Rishra (where the first mill came up in 1855), at Titagarh, at Bhadreshwar, at Howrah. Why so concentrated? Because jute is grown in the Ganga delta around them, because the Hugli provides plenty of water to soak and process raw jute, because cheap labour comes from Bihar and Odisha next door, because Kolkata port handles the exports, and because the railway and waterway network is dense. After **Partition in 1947**, three-fourths of the jute-growing area went to East Pakistan (now Bangladesh) while the mills stayed in India — a problem we are still working through. Today jute is used for sacking, packing material, carpet backing, geotextiles, and even fashion bags. It is biodegradable, which makes it valuable in a plastic-saturated world.

**Sugar industry.** India is the world's second largest producer of sugar — and the largest producer of **gur and khandsari**, the traditional unrefined forms. The mills are located in **Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, Punjab, Haryana and Madhya Pradesh**. UP and Bihar together hold about sixty per cent of mills. But there is a trend — new mills are coming up in the south and west, especially Maharashtra. Why? Because the sugarcane grown in Maharashtra has a **higher sucrose content**, the cooler climate gives a longer crushing season, and the cooperative structure of mills works better there. Sugarcane is bulky and loses sucrose during long transport, so the mill must be close to the field. That is why sugar is so well suited to the **cooperative sector** — the same farmers who grow cane jointly own the mill that crushes it.

## Mineral-Based Industries — Iron and Steel

If cotton is the heart of India's agro-based industry, **iron and steel is the heart of its mineral-based industry**. Production and consumption of steel is so closely tied to a country's development that economists treat per capita steel consumption as a rough proxy for industrial development. India produces over 120 million tonnes of crude steel a year — among the top three producers in the world. But our per capita consumption is still much lower than China's or Japan's.

Iron and steel is a **basic industry** — every other industry depends on it. Cars need steel sheets. Buildings need steel rebars. Railways need rails. Ships need plates. Defence needs special grades. Bridges, dams, transmission towers, kitchen sinks, surgical instruments — all begin with a steel mill.

Iron and steel is also a **heavy industry**. The raw materials — iron ore, **coking coal**, and **limestone** — are needed in roughly the ratio of **4 : 2 : 1** by weight. To make one tonne of steel, you need four tonnes of ore, two tonnes of coal, and one tonne of limestone. Some manganese is added to harden it. All this is bulky. So the plant must be near at least one of these inputs — ideally near all of them.

This is why most of India's integrated steel plants sit in or around the **Chhotanagpur plateau region** — a single belt covering parts of Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh and West Bengal. This region has iron ore (Singhbhum, Keonjhar, Bailadila), coking coal (Jharia, Bokaro coalfields), limestone, water, and a dense rail network. It also has cheap labour, growing markets, and historical experience.

The big plants are:

**TISCO at Jamshedpur** — set up by Jamsetji Tata in 1907, started production in 1912. The very first integrated steel plant in India, in private hands, built before independence. It still runs and is part of Tata Steel.

**IISCO at Burnpur** in West Bengal — the second oldest, now also a SAIL plant.

**Bhilai Steel Plant** in Chhattisgarh — set up in 1959 in collaboration with the Soviet Union.

**Rourkela Steel Plant** in Odisha — 1959, with German collaboration.

**Durgapur Steel Plant** in West Bengal — 1962, with British collaboration.

**Bokaro Steel Plant** in Jharkhand — 1964, with Soviet help.

**Vizag Steel Plant at Visakhapatnam** in Andhra Pradesh — the only coastal steel plant, with the strategic advantage of importing high-grade coking coal cheaply and exporting finished steel directly.

**Salem Steel Plant** in Tamil Nadu — a stainless steel specialist.

**Bhadravati (VISL)** in Karnataka.

Look at any map of India and you will see a clear cluster — Chhotanagpur is the iron-and-steel heart. The visakhapatnam plant is the only major exception, placed on the coast for strategic reasons.

Despite our size, Indian steel still faces problems. **Coking coal** has to be partly imported — Indian coal is high in ash. Energy costs are higher than in China. Productivity in some plants is below global benchmarks. But the future is promising — domestic demand keeps rising as India urbanises.

## Aluminium, Chemicals, Cement

**Aluminium smelting** is the second most important metallurgical industry. Aluminium is light, doesn't rust, conducts heat well, and gets very strong when mixed with other metals. It is used in aircraft, cooking utensils, wires, packaging foil, window frames, and increasingly in cars (to save weight). It substitutes for steel, copper, zinc and lead in many uses.

Aluminium is made from **bauxite** — a bulky, dark-reddish rock found in plenty in Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Maharashtra and Gujarat. Smelting bauxite into alumina and then into aluminium needs huge amounts of **electricity** — making one tonne of aluminium uses as much power as a middle-class family uses in years. So aluminium plants want to be near both bauxite mines and cheap power.

The major plants are:

**NALCO** (National Aluminium Company) in **Odisha** — public sector, near the rich Panchpatmali bauxite reserves.

**BALCO** (Bharat Aluminium Company) in **Korba, Chhattisgarh** — close to coal-based thermal power.

**HINDALCO** (Hindustan Aluminium) in **Renukoot, Uttar Pradesh** — Aditya Birla group, sourced from Jharkhand-Odisha mines.

**VEDANTA** in Odisha and Chhattisgarh.

Aluminium plants are also scattered in West Bengal, Kerala, Tamil Nadu — wherever the power-and-ore combination works out.

**Chemical industry** is fast-growing in India and very diversified. It splits into two halves. **Inorganic chemicals** — sulphuric acid, nitric acid, soda ash, caustic soda — are used to make fertilisers, glass, soap, paper, paints, dyes. Plants are spread widely across the country because demand exists everywhere.

**Organic chemicals** — petrochemicals, synthetic fibres, synthetic rubber, plastics, drugs, dyes — are made near oil refineries and petrochemical complexes, because they all start from crude oil derivatives. **Maharashtra, Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh** are the big chemical-industry states. Plants cluster around Vadodara, Mumbai, Visakhapatnam, Chennai, Hazira, Dahej, and Paradip.

A key feature — the chemical industry is its own largest consumer. Basic chemicals are processed to make other chemicals that are used for industrial use, agriculture, or directly by consumers. It is a chain.

**Fertiliser industry.** India is one of the biggest fertiliser producers in the world. The industry makes **nitrogenous fertilisers** (mostly **urea**), **phosphatic fertilisers** (mostly **DAP** — diammonium phosphate), and **complex fertilisers** that combine nitrogen (N), phosphate (P) and potash (K) in measured ratios. Potash is **entirely imported** — India has no commercial potash reserves.

Major fertiliser plants are at **Sindri (Jharkhand)** — set up in 1951 as one of the first public-sector plants, **Nangal (Punjab)**, **Trombay (Maharashtra)**, **Gorakhpur (Uttar Pradesh)**, **Namrup (Assam)**, **Rourkela (Odisha)**, **Kanpur**, **Cochin**, and many others. After the Green Revolution the industry exploded — **Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab and Kerala** together produce about half of India's fertiliser today.

**Cement industry.** Cement is essential for every house, factory, road, bridge, airport and dam. The raw materials are bulky and heavy — **limestone, silica, gypsum**. Coal and electricity are also needed. And the finished cement is itself bulky.

So cement plants locate near limestone quarries. The big concentrations are in the **Gujarat-Karnataka-Andhra Pradesh-Tamil Nadu belt**, in Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan, and increasingly in the north-east. Gujarat plants enjoy the special advantage of port access — they export cement to the Gulf countries.

The first cement plant in India was set up in **Chennai in 1904**. After independence the industry expanded rapidly. Today India is the second-largest cement producer in the world (after China). Our consumption per person is still low, which means there is huge room to grow as urbanisation continues.

## Automobiles, Electronics, IT

**Automobile industry** has been the success story of post-1991 India. The reforms of that year opened up the sector to foreign collaboration and competition. Maruti, which had been a small public-sector venture making one car in collaboration with Suzuki, suddenly faced Hyundai, Toyota, Ford, GM, Honda, Volkswagen — and grew bigger by competing with them. Today India makes trucks, buses, cars, motorcycles, scooters, three-wheelers, tractors, and multi-utility vehicles at scale. We are the world's largest market for two-wheelers and a top-five market for cars.

The industry has clustered in distinct belts. **Delhi-Gurugram-Manesar-Noida** in the National Capital Region — Maruti Suzuki, Honda, Hero, Yamaha, JCB. **Pune-Mumbai-Aurangabad** — Tata Motors, Mahindra, Bajaj, Skoda. **Chennai-Sriperumbudur-Hosur** — Hyundai, Ford (now exited), Renault-Nissan, Royal Enfield, TVS. **Bengaluru** — Toyota Kirloskar. **Kolkata** — Hindustan Motors (now mostly defunct). **Lucknow** — Tata Motors. **Indore-Pithampur** — Force Motors. **Hyderabad**. **Jamshedpur** — Tata. Each cluster has a port nearby (or train link to one) for exports.

The supplier ecosystem matters as much as the assembly plant. One car has roughly 20,000 parts. Each main plant is surrounded by hundreds of vendor units making seats, brakes, lights, dashboards, harnesses. This is the **ancillary industry** — small and medium units that depend on a big assembler. Tirupati, Rudrapur, Bhiwadi, Manesar, Chakan — all have built ancillary clusters.

**Electronics and information technology.** This is the most dramatic transformation of all. In 1990 India barely made transistor radios. By 2025, Bengaluru is the IT capital of India and arguably the third-largest software cluster in the world after Silicon Valley and Beijing.

The electronics industry covers transistors, televisions, telephones, mobile cellular handsets, telephone exchanges, radars, computers, set-top boxes, and the equipment that runs the telecommunications industry. **Bengaluru** is the centre, followed by **Mumbai, Delhi, Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai, Kolkata, Lucknow and Coimbatore**. Major industrial concentration is at **Bengaluru, Noida, Mumbai, Chennai, Hyderabad and Pune**.

What makes IT different from older manufacturing is that it does not need bulky raw materials. It needs **bright, trained engineers**, **fast networks**, **English-language schooling**, **predictable infrastructure**, **honest regulation**, and **a cluster of similar firms** to share talent and ideas. Cities that supply these win. Cities that don't, lose. That is why a small set of Indian cities have captured almost the entire IT industry — and why other places, equally rich in coal and iron, have nothing comparable.

The biggest impact of this sector has been on employment. IT and IT-enabled services (BPOs, call centres, business analytics) employ over 4 million people directly and many more indirectly. The continuing growth in both hardware and software is the key to the success of India's IT industry.

## Why Industries Locate Where They Do

You have already seen the pattern. Now let us be explicit about the rules.

A factory will be set up where it can produce most cheaply and sell most easily. Several **location factors** shape the decision.

**Raw material.** If the raw material is bulky or perishable, the factory must be near it. Sugarcane mills must be near cane fields because cane loses sucrose every hour after cutting. Steel mills must be near iron ore and coal. Cement plants must be near limestone. This is called being a **weight-losing industry** — the finished product is much lighter than the raw input, so moving raw material is more expensive than moving the finished good.

**Power.** Aluminium smelters need huge electricity — they go where power is cheap. Heavy electrical industry needs reliable supply. Often a thermal or hydro plant is built right next to the industry.

**Labour.** Cotton textile mills came to Mumbai and Ahmedabad partly because workers were available cheaply from the countryside. The IT industry goes to cities with engineering colleges. Garment clusters like Tirupur thrive because of skilled tailors.

**Market.** Heavy machine tools, printing presses, consumer goods — these need to be near their customers. A bottling plant for Coca-Cola is in every region because soft drinks are heavy (it is mostly water) and shipping them is expensive.

**Transport.** Ports, railway junctions, highway crossroads attract industry. Chennai got its automobile cluster partly because of its port. Visakhapatnam steel was set up because the coast lets it import coal and export steel cheaply.

**Capital and entrepreneurship.** Mumbai had Parsi merchant capital. Ahmedabad had Gujarati merchant capital. Kolkata had Marwari capital. These cities became industrial centres partly because there were rich families willing to risk money on factories.

**Government policy.** The whole public-sector strategy of locating Bhilai, Rourkela, Bokaro, Durgapur in poor regions was a deliberate policy decision. Industrial estates, tax holidays, special economic zones (SEZs) — all are tools used by the state to push industry where the market alone would not place it.

These factors interact. A small bias becomes self-reinforcing. Once a cluster forms — automobiles in Chennai, IT in Bengaluru, diamond polishing in Surat — new firms come because the old ones are already there. Workers are trained. Suppliers exist. Customers know the place. This is called the **cluster effect** or **agglomeration economy**.

## Industrial Pollution — The Cost We Pay

Industry creates wealth. Industry also creates serious pollution. The two are not separate problems — they are different sides of the same coin. We need to face this honestly.

Industries are responsible for **four types of pollution**.

**Air pollution.** Smoke from chimneys carries sulphur dioxide, carbon monoxide, nitrogen oxides, and tiny particles of dust, ash and soot. The biggest culprits are **chemical and paper factories, brick kilns, oil refineries, smelting plants, and thermal power stations that burn coal or oil and ignore pollution norms**. Sometimes a sudden gas leak can be catastrophic. The **Bhopal Gas tragedy** of 1984, when methyl isocyanate leaked from the Union Carbide pesticide plant and killed thousands, is the worst industrial disaster in human history. Many survivors are still living with lung damage four decades later. Air pollution harms human health, animals, plants, buildings (acid rain corrodes the Taj Mahal) and the atmosphere itself — contributing to climate change.

**Water pollution.** Organic and inorganic wastes from factories pour into rivers. The main culprits are **paper and pulp mills, chemical plants, textile and dyeing units, petroleum refineries, tanneries, and electroplating workshops**. They discharge dyes, detergents, acids, salts, heavy metals like **lead and mercury**, pesticides, fertilisers, synthetic chemicals, plastics, and rubber waste. Solid wastes like **fly ash** (from thermal plants), **phospho-gypsum** (from fertiliser plants), and **iron and steel slag** also enter water bodies. **Thermal pollution** is a special case — when factories or thermal plants drain hot water back into rivers without cooling it first, fish die because they cannot survive the temperature jump. **Nuclear waste** from atomic power plants causes cancer, birth defects and miscarriages if it leaks.

**Soil pollution.** When factories dump glass, harmful chemicals, packaging, salts and garbage on the ground, the soil becomes useless. Rain percolates this poison into the groundwater. **Soil and water pollution are closely linked** — once the soil is contaminated, the groundwater under it is too.

**Noise pollution.** Industrial machinery, factory equipment, generators, saws, pneumatic drills, electric tools — all produce noise far above safe levels. Unwanted sound causes irritation, anger, hearing damage, increased heart rate, increased blood pressure, and chronic stress.

Every litre of waste water discharged by industry pollutes about **eight times its volume** in fresh water downstream. That is the multiplier we are dealing with.

## How Pollution Can Be Controlled

Controlling industrial pollution is technically possible. It is also expensive — which is why so many factories cheat. But the answers are well known.

**For water pollution**, treat the effluent before releasing it. The technical process is in three stages. **Primary treatment** uses mechanical means — screening out solids, grinding, settling. **Secondary treatment** uses biological processes — bacteria break down organic waste. **Tertiary treatment** uses chemical and physical methods to recycle the water for reuse. Modern industrial parks have **common effluent treatment plants (CETPs)** that all factories must use. Water can be **reused** within the factory in multiple stages — first to wash, then to cool, then to clean — so that the same litre does several jobs. **Rainwater harvesting** can meet some water needs without drawing from rivers or groundwater. Drawing too much groundwater also needs **legal regulation** — many industrial areas have rapidly falling water tables.

**For air pollution**, smokestacks can be fitted with **electrostatic precipitators, fabric filters, scrubbers and inertial separators** to trap dust and particles. Switching from coal to **oil or natural gas** as fuel cuts smoke sharply. Equipment redesign can reduce emissions per unit of output.

**For noise pollution**, generators can have **silencers**. Machinery can be **redesigned** for quieter operation. Workplaces can use **sound-absorbing materials**. Workers must wear **earplugs and earphones** in noisy zones.

The deeper answer is **sustainable development** — integrating economic growth with environmental protection. Not choosing between them, but doing both.

A good example is the **NTPC (National Thermal Power Corporation)**, India's biggest electricity producer. NTPC runs many coal-fired power plants — historically one of the dirtiest industries on the planet. Yet NTPC has earned **ISO 14001 certification** for its Environment Management System and follows several proactive steps. It uses the latest technology and upgrades old equipment to higher efficiency. It minimises waste by **maximising fly ash utilisation** — selling ash to cement plants instead of dumping it. It plants **green belts** around its plants for ecological balance and afforestation. It uses **ash pond management** and **ash water recycling**. It runs **ecological monitoring** and on-line database management for all its power stations.

NTPC shows what is possible when an industry treats pollution control as a core function, not as a regulatory burden. The cost is not negligible — but the cost of poisoning rivers and damaging children's lungs is incalculably higher.

## What This Chapter Asks of You

You live in an industrial India. Your soap, your toothpaste, your phone, your school bag, your shoes — all of them come from factories. Some of those factories are clean, careful, and well-managed. Many are not. The black river you crossed on the way home, the smog in winter, the asthma cases in your school — these are industrial India too.

Three things are worth taking forward.

**One — every product carries a hidden history.** When you pick up a shirt, ask: where was the cotton grown, where was the yarn spun, where was the cloth woven, where was it stitched, who was paid how much at each stage, what was the pollution cost? Most products travel through many regions before reaching you. The brand on the label is only the last name in a long chain.

**Two — location patterns are not accidents.** Jamshedpur is where it is because of iron ore in Singhbhum, coal in Jharia, water from the Subarnarekha, and an entrepreneur named Jamsetji Tata who saw the combination first. Tirupur is where it is because southern Tamil Nadu had cheap labour, port access through Tuticorin, and Coimbatore as an engineering hub. The map of industries is also a map of geography, history, capital, and policy combined.

**Three — pollution is everyone's problem.** A pesticide factory leaking into a river kills fish a hundred kilometres downstream. A cement plant filling the air with dust gives children asthma in a town that gets no jobs from the plant. Either we figure out how to grow industries cleanly, or industrial growth ends up costing us as much as it gives us.

The story of manufacturing in India is far from over. The big names of 1900 — Tata, Birla, Bajaj, Mahindra — are still major players. New names are joining — Reliance, Adani, OYO, Zomato, Ola Electric. New industries are rising — electric vehicles, solar panels, semiconductors, biotechnology. Some of them will get the balance right. Some will not. As citizens, students, consumers and voters, the choices you make over the next few decades will shape which kind of industrial India wins.

## Apply — Three Stories

**Tata's company town.** When Jamsetji Tata picked a forested patch of land in Singhbhum in 1907 to build his steel works, he was building more than a factory. He had told his son the plant should sit in a **planned city** — with wide streets, parks, schools, hospitals, decent worker housing. The result was **Jamshedpur** — India's first true industrial city, designed before it was built. Even today, Jamshedpur stands out. The roads are wider than in most state capitals. Children of mill workers have studied in Tata schools and gone on to engineering colleges. The model proved one thing — a private factory can also build a humane city, if the owner chooses to. Most Indian industrial towns since then — Bhiwadi, Manesar, Tirupur — have not chosen to. The workers there live in unplanned slums, breathe industrial smoke, and travel hours by bus. Jamshedpur shows that another model was always possible.

**Tirupur's t-shirt boom.** A small town near Coimbatore in Tamil Nadu, Tirupur, became the **knitwear capital of India** in the 1990s. Today it makes about a third of India's cotton knitwear exports — t-shirts, polos, underwear, sportswear sold under brands like Nike, Adidas, GAP and H&M. Thousands of small units cluster together — some spin, some dye, some stitch, some embroider, some pack. Migrant workers from across south India fill the workforce. But the boom came at a cost. The **Noyyal river** that runs through Tirupur turned dark with effluent from dyeing units. Drinking water in surrounding villages became unsafe. After years of protest by farmers downstream, the Madras High Court in 2011 ordered all dyeing units to install **zero liquid discharge** systems — they must recover and reuse every drop of water. Many shut down. Many invested in expensive technology and survived. Tirupur today is cleaner than it was. But the story is a warning — without environmental rules enforced honestly, an industrial boom can wreck the very rivers that made it possible.

**Bhiwadi's slow choking.** Bhiwadi in Rajasthan, just south of Delhi, became an industrial hub in the early 2000s — chemical plants, paint factories, automobile-component units, packaging makers. Land was cheap, the highway was close, Delhi was a quick truck ride away. By the 2010s, Bhiwadi was regularly being ranked among the world's most polluted cities. Children there showed unusually high rates of asthma and skin problems. Investigations found that **industries were dumping untreated effluents into the groundwater**, that **diesel generators ran when grid power failed** (which was often), and that **enforcement was almost non-existent** because too many local jobs depended on the factories staying open. Bhiwadi is the dark mirror of Jamshedpur. The same instinct that built Tirupur built Bhiwadi. The difference is the seriousness with which pollution rules were enforced. Bhiwadi shows that without effective regulation, industrial growth and human health go in opposite directions.

**DIY.**

(a) **Visit a small factory or workshop in your area** — a flour mill, a printing press, a furniture workshop, a steel-rolling unit, a soap maker. Spend an hour. Talk to the owner and to one worker. Find out — what is the raw material, where does it come from, where does the finished product go, how many people work there, where does the waste go.

(b) **Audit 10 products in your house** — your toothpaste, soap, shampoo, biscuit, shirt, shoes, notebook, plate, fan, bulb. Look at each label and write down the state where it was manufactured. Make a small table — product, brand, state of manufacture, type of industry (agro / mineral / consumer). What patterns do you see? Is your toothbrush from one state and your shampoo from another? Why?

(c) **Sketch a map of industries within 50 km of your town.** Use Google Maps to identify factories — cement plants, brick kilns, sugar mills, dairies, textile units, automobile assemblers, anything visible. Mark them by type with different colours. Add the rivers and the railway line. What does the geography of your local industrial landscape tell you?
