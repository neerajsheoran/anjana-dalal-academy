## How Our Eyes Work

**Child:** Maa, how does our eye actually see things? Is it like a camera?

**Mother:** That is a lovely comparison, beta. The eye works very much like a camera. The cornea at the front bends the light first, and then the eye lens behind it fine-tunes the focus so that a sharp, clear image forms on the retina at the back -- just like a photo on the sensor of your phone camera.

**Child:** But Maa, a camera lens is fixed. How does the eye focus on things that are near and far?

**Mother:** Great observation! That is where the ciliary muscles come in. When you look at something far away -- say, the clock tower from our balcony -- these muscles relax and the lens becomes thin and flat, increasing its focal length. When you look at something close, like the comic book in your hands, the muscles tighten, the lens becomes thick and round, and the focal length decreases. This wonderful ability is called the power of accommodation.

**Child:** So there is a limit to how close we can see clearly?

**Mother:** Yes. The closest point at which a healthy eye can see clearly is about 25 centimetres -- roughly the distance at which you would hold a book. That is the near point. And the farthest is infinity -- the stars in the night sky, for instance.

**Child:** And the retina has rods and cones?

**Mother:** Exactly. Rod cells help you see in dim light, like when there is a power cut and only a candle is lit. Cone cells detect colour and work best in bright light. That is why at night everything looks greyish -- your cones do not have enough light to work properly.

## Vision Problems and How to Fix Them

**Child:** Maa, my friend Aditi just got spectacles. The doctor said she has myopia. What does that mean?

**Mother:** Myopia, or short-sightedness, means Aditi can see things close to her -- like her textbook -- perfectly, but objects far away, like the blackboard from the last bench, appear blurred. In her eyes, the image of distant things forms in front of the retina instead of on it.

**Child:** Why does that happen?

**Mother:** Either her eyeball is slightly longer than normal, or her eye lens curves too much. The fix is simple -- a concave lens is placed in front of the eye. It spreads the light rays a little before they enter the eye, pushing the image back onto the retina.

**Child:** What about Dadi? She holds the newspaper far away to read it.

**Mother:** Dadi might have hypermetropia -- long-sightedness. She can see distant things but nearby objects look fuzzy because the image forms behind her retina. A convex lens helps by bending the light inward so the image falls right on the retina.

**Child:** And Nana has bifocal glasses -- one part for distance and another for reading?

**Mother:** That is presbyopia, beta. With age, the ciliary muscles weaken and the lens loses its flexibility. The eye cannot accommodate as well as it used to. Bifocal lenses solve this -- the upper half has a concave lens for distance and the lower half has a convex lens for reading. So Nana can watch TV and read the Ramayan with the same pair of glasses!

**Child:** What about cataract? Is it the same?

**Mother:** Cataract is different. It is the clouding of the eye lens itself, usually with ageing. No spectacle can fix it -- surgery is needed to replace the cloudy lens with an artificial one. India conducts millions of cataract surgeries every year, especially through camps in rural areas, giving lakhs of people their sight back.

## Prisms and the Magic of Seven Colours

**Child:** Maa, when I held that glass paperweight near the window, I saw a rainbow on the wall! How?

**Mother:** That was dispersion in action! When white light passes through a glass prism -- or something shaped like one, such as your paperweight -- it splits into seven colours: violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, and red. We remember them as VIBGYOR.

**Child:** But why does the light split?

**Mother:** Because each colour travels at a slightly different speed inside the glass. Violet, being the fastest to slow down, bends the most. Red, which slows down the least, bends the least. Since each colour bends by a different amount, they separate out into a beautiful band of colours.

**Child:** So white light is actually a mixture?

**Mother:** Precisely! Isaac Newton proved this with a brilliant experiment. He passed white light through one prism to get the spectrum, then placed a second prism upside-down in the path. The seven colours recombined back into white light. This proved that the prism does not create colours -- it only reveals what is already hidden inside white light.

## Why the Sky is Blue and Sunsets are Red

**Child:** Maa, why is the sky blue during the day but turns orange and red in the evening?

**Mother:** This is one of the most beautiful questions in science! The atmosphere is filled with tiny molecules of nitrogen and oxygen. These molecules are very good at scattering short-wavelength light -- blue and violet. This scattered blue light reaches your eyes from every direction, making the sky look blue. This is called Rayleigh scattering.

**Child:** Then why not violet? Violet has an even shorter wavelength.

**Mother:** Sharp thinking! Violet does scatter more, but our eyes are less sensitive to violet and more sensitive to blue. Also, the upper atmosphere absorbs some violet light. So the overall effect is a beautiful blue.

**Child:** And at sunset?

**Mother:** When the Sun is near the horizon -- imagine watching the sunset from Juhu Beach -- sunlight has to travel through a much thicker layer of atmosphere. Along this long path, almost all the blue light gets scattered away before reaching your eyes. Only the red and orange light, which have longer wavelengths and scatter less, survive the journey. That is why the Sun looks like a glowing red ball at dusk.

**Child:** What about astronauts? What colour is their sky?

**Mother:** In space, there is no atmosphere at all. No molecules means no scattering. So the sky looks completely black, even in daytime! Astronauts can see stars during the day because there is no scattered blue light to overpower them.

**Child:** And the Tyndall effect -- is that related?

**Mother:** Yes! When a beam of light passes through fog, smoke, or a glass of diluted milk, you can see the beam's path because the tiny suspended particles scatter the light sideways. That is the Tyndall effect. You have seen it when sunlight streams through a dusty window -- those beautiful rays you can trace are scattered light made visible by dust particles.

## Exam Preparation Tips

**Child:** Maa, what should I focus on for the exam from this chapter?

**Mother:** The most important topics are defects of vision -- know the cause, the defect, and the correction for myopia, hypermetropia, and presbyopia. Draw clear ray diagrams showing where the image forms and how the corrective lens fixes it. This is worth 3 to 5 marks almost every year.

**Child:** What about the prism and scattering topics?

**Mother:** Dispersion of light by a prism and Newton's recombination experiment are regularly tested -- 2 to 3 marks. For scattering, know why the sky is blue, why the Sun is reddish at sunrise and sunset, and what the Tyndall effect is. Atmospheric refraction explaining the twinkling of stars and why planets do not twinkle is another favourite.

**Child:** Should I practise numerical problems too?

**Mother:** Yes. If the question gives you a shifted near point or far point, you should be able to use the lens formula to find the power of the corrective lens. For example, if someone's far point is at 60 cm, you need a concave lens that creates a virtual image of a distant object at 60 cm. These calculations come up periodically in boards and are excellent practice for competitive exams too.
