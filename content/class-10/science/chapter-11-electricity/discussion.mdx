## Current, Voltage, and Ohm's Law

**Child:** Maa, why do we say current flows from positive to negative when electrons actually move from negative to positive?

**Mother:** That is a great question, beta. Long before anyone knew about electrons, scientists assumed that something positive was flowing from the positive terminal to the negative terminal. By the time J.J. Thomson discovered the electron in 1897, that convention was already deeply rooted in physics. Changing it would have created enormous confusion, so we kept the old convention. It makes no difference to any calculation -- the maths works out the same either way.

**Child:** And Ohm's Law -- V equals I times R -- is it always valid?

**Mother:** It holds as long as the temperature stays constant and the conductor is an ohmic material. Metals like copper obey it beautifully -- if you plot voltage against current, you get a perfectly straight line through the origin. But components like LEDs and diodes do not follow Ohm's Law -- their resistance changes with the applied voltage. For your board exam, concentrate on ohmic conductors.

**Child:** Maa, why does the ammeter go in series and the voltmeter in parallel?

**Mother:** Think of it this way. The ammeter needs to count every bit of charge that flows through a component, so it must sit right in the path of the current -- in series. A voltmeter, on the other hand, checks the pressure difference across a component, so it must connect on both sides of it -- in parallel. An ammeter has very low resistance so it does not block the current, and a voltmeter has very high resistance so it does not divert current away from the component being measured.

## Resistance and Resistivity

**Child:** Resistance depends on length, area, material, and temperature. The formula is R equals rho times l over A. What exactly is rho?

**Mother:** Rho is the resistivity of the material -- it tells you how strongly that particular substance opposes current flow. Copper has an extremely low resistivity, which is why we use copper wires in our home wiring. Rubber has a huge resistivity, which is why electricians wear rubber gloves for protection. Nichrome, an alloy of nickel and chromium, has a moderately high resistivity and is used in the coils of room heaters and electric irons.

**Child:** Does resistivity change with temperature?

**Mother:** Yes. For metals, resistivity goes up when temperature rises -- the atoms vibrate more and get in the way of moving electrons. For semiconductors like silicon, it is the opposite -- resistivity falls as temperature increases, because heat frees up more charge carriers. This property makes silicon the foundation of every computer chip and smartphone processor.

**Child:** So when Maa's iron gets very hot, its resistance increases?

**Mother:** Exactly right. The nichrome coil in the iron has higher resistance at operating temperature than when it is cold. The design accounts for this, so the iron still draws the right amount of current for heating your school uniform!

## Series and Parallel Circuits

**Child:** Maa, why does Diwali happen when one bulb in the old string of lights goes out and all the rest go dark too?

**Mother:** That is a series circuit at work, beta! In a series string, all the bulbs share the same single path for current. If one bulb blows, the path breaks and current stops everywhere. That is why modern decorative lights use parallel wiring -- each bulb has its own path, so one blown bulb does not affect the others.

**Child:** In parallel, the total resistance is always less than the smallest single resistor?

**Mother:** Always. If you connect two 8-ohm resistors in parallel, the combined resistance is only 4 ohms. More paths for current means easier overall flow -- like opening a second billing counter at the grocery shop reduces the queue for everyone.

**Child:** How should I tackle complicated circuit problems?

**Mother:** Break the circuit into sections. First, identify which resistors are in parallel and combine them into a single equivalent resistance. Then add series resistors together. Redraw the simplified circuit at each step. Keep doing this until the entire network reduces to a single resistance. Then use V equals IR to find the total current, and work backwards to find individual currents and voltages.

## Heating Effect and Electric Power

**Child:** Maa, the geyser heats water using electricity. That is because of H equals I squared R t?

**Mother:** Precisely. When current flows through the high-resistance coil inside the geyser, electrical energy converts to heat. The heat produced depends on three things: the square of the current, the resistance of the coil, and the duration. This is Joule's law of heating. The same principle is at work in the toaster that browns your bread, the iron that presses your clothes, and the fuse that protects the wiring.

**Child:** Speaking of fuses, how do they protect us?

**Mother:** A fuse is a short piece of wire made from a metal with a low melting point. It is placed in series with the circuit. If something goes wrong and too much current flows -- say, during a short circuit -- the fuse wire heats up quickly, melts, and snaps the circuit open. This stops the excessive current before it can damage the wiring or cause a fire.

**Child:** And the electricity bill -- that is measured in kilowatt-hours?

**Mother:** Yes. One kilowatt-hour, or one "unit," is the energy consumed by a 1000-watt appliance running for one hour. Our electricity bill charges us per unit. The rate per unit varies from state to state in India -- and there are different slabs depending on how much you consume.

**Child:** If our 60-watt ceiling fan runs for 15 hours a day for 30 days, how many units is that?

**Mother:** Let us calculate. 60 watts multiplied by 15 hours multiplied by 30 days equals 27,000 watt-hours, which is 27 kWh or 27 units. If the tariff is 6 rupees per unit, that is 162 rupees per month just for one fan. Now imagine adding the fridge, the TV, the lights, and the geyser -- you can see why electricity bills add up!

## Exam Preparation Tips

**Child:** Maa, this chapter has a lot of formulas and numericals. What should I prioritise?

**Mother:** Numericals on Ohm's Law, series-parallel resistor combinations, and power-energy calculations carry the highest marks -- typically 5 to 8 marks from this chapter. Make sure you know all the formulas, can draw neat circuit diagrams with correct symbols, and understand when to use which formula.

**Child:** What mistakes should I watch out for?

**Mother:** Four common traps. First, mixing up the series formula with the parallel formula. Second, forgetting unit conversions -- milliamperes to amperes, kilowatts to watts, hours to seconds. Third, drawing messy or incorrect circuit diagrams. Fourth, confusing resistance (property of a specific conductor) with resistivity (property of the material). If you practise 15 to 20 varied numerical problems, you will be well prepared.

**Child:** Maa, does this chapter connect to anything in real life beyond exams?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Every time you charge your phone, switch on a light, or plug in the mixer grinder, you are using circuit principles from this chapter. India's push towards solar energy and electric vehicles relies on engineers who deeply understand series-parallel battery configurations, power management, and efficient energy usage. The physics you are studying now is at the heart of India's energy future.
