## Current, Voltage, and Ohm's Law

**Meera:** Arjun, in the prelim paper there was a 5-marker on Ohm's law. I had the formula but messed up the unit conversion.

**Arjun:** Unit conversion is the silent killer in electricity. NCERT spells it out — milliampere to ampere, kilowatt-hour to joules. Half the class loses marks here.

**Meera:** Also I keep confusing myself on conventional current. Current flows positive to negative, but electrons move the other way?

**Arjun:** Right. The convention was set before electrons were even discovered, so we just kept it. Maths works out the same either way. Examiners don't trick you on this — they just want the standard convention in your circuit diagrams.

**Meera:** And Ohm's law — V equals IR — works only for metallic conductors at constant temperature?

**Arjun:** Yes. NCERT calls these ohmic conductors. The V-I graph is a straight line through the origin. For LEDs and diodes the line bends. Board exam sticks to ohmic — copper, nichrome, the usual.

**Meera:** Ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel. I always remember the rule but forget why.

**Arjun:** Ammeter has very low resistance, so it doesn't choke the current — must sit in the current's path. Voltmeter has very high resistance so it doesn't divert current — must sit across the component. That "why" line is often the 1-marker.

## Resistance and Resistivity

**Meera:** R equals rho times l over A. So resistance depends on length, area, and material. Resistivity rho is the material property itself.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Copper rho is tiny — that's why our house wiring uses copper. Nichrome rho is high — that's why it's in the heater coil and the iron. Rubber rho is huge — that's the insulation around the wire.

**Meera:** So when our induction stove glows red, the resistivity actually goes up because the metal is hot?

**Arjun:** For metals, yes — resistivity rises with temperature. Atoms vibrate more, electrons collide more, harder to flow. For semiconductors like silicon it's the opposite — heat frees more charge carriers, resistivity drops. That's why every smartphone chip is silicon.

**Meera:** Does that ever come up in the board exam?

**Arjun:** Usually as a 1 or 2-marker. "Why is copper used for wiring and nichrome for heating elements?" Two clean lines — low resistivity for copper conductors, high resistivity and high melting point for nichrome heating coils. Done.

**Meera:** I keep mixing up resistance and resistivity in the definition.

**Arjun:** Resistance is for a specific conductor — depends on its length and area. Resistivity is just the material — independent of shape. NCERT's exact wording is worth memorising verbatim.

## Series and Parallel Circuits

**Meera:** Arjun, last Diwali our old fairy lights died — one bulb burned out and the entire string went dark. Classic series circuit?

**Arjun:** Textbook example. Single path, one break, everything stops. Modern lights are wired in parallel — each bulb has its own path. That's why one failure doesn't kill the whole string anymore.

**Meera:** In parallel, the total resistance is always smaller than the smallest single resistor. That's the weird bit my classmates keep getting wrong.

**Arjun:** Because adding paths is like opening more billing counters at DMart — total flow gets easier, total resistance drops. Two 8-ohm resistors in parallel give 4 ohms. NCERT example 12.7 is exactly this.

**Meera:** And in complicated circuits — three resistors, some series some parallel — how do you not panic?

**Arjun:** Step by step. Identify the parallel groups first, replace each with its equivalent. Then the series ones simply add up. Redraw the circuit at every stage. Eventually it reduces to one resistor and one battery, then V equals IR gives the total current.

**Meera:** That step-by-step redrawing is exactly what I keep skipping in practice.

**Arjun:** Don't. Examiners give partial marks for clean intermediate diagrams. Skip the redraw, lose two marks even if your final answer is right.

## Heating Effect and Electric Power

**Meera:** Our induction stove, the iron, the geyser — all of them use the heating effect of current. H equals I squared R t, right?

**Arjun:** Yes. Joule's law. The heat depends on the square of the current — so doubling the current quadruples the heat. That's why fuses respond so fast to overcurrent.

**Meera:** Fuses — thin wire, low melting point, in series with the circuit. Surge of current, fuse melts, circuit breaks.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Modern homes use MCBs which do the same job with a tripping switch instead of a melting wire. But the principle is the same — protect the wiring from overcurrent before it starts a fire.

**Meera:** And the electricity bill — one kilowatt-hour is one unit. That's the commercial unit, not the SI unit.

**Arjun:** Right. SI unit of energy is the joule. 1 kWh equals 3.6 into 10 to the 6 joules. Board questions love that conversion.

**Meera:** Our Adani bill last month was nearly 4500 rupees. Mostly the AC running 6 hours every night.

**Arjun:** Run the numbers. A 1.5-tonne AC is roughly 1500 W. Six hours a night for thirty nights — 1500 into 6 into 30, that's 2,70,000 watt-hours, or 270 units. At a residential tariff of around 10 rupees per unit in Mumbai slabs, that's 2700 rupees just for the AC.

**Meera:** That's almost certainly a 5-marker pattern — "calculate the monthly cost of running a 1500 W appliance for 6 hours daily for 30 days."

**Arjun:** Exactly. And on a BLDC fan instead of an old induction motor fan, you'd halve that load. Real-life numericals like this are showing up more often in board papers.

## Exam Preparation Tips

**Meera:** Arjun, this chapter has the most numericals in the entire physics section. What should I prioritise?

**Arjun:** Three numerical types. First, Ohm's law direct application — V equals IR with one unknown. Second, series-parallel combinations to find equivalent resistance, then current and voltage drops. Third, power-energy calculations — P equals VI and energy in kilowatt-hours. Together these usually carry 6 to 8 marks.

**Meera:** And the common traps?

**Arjun:** Four traps. Mixing the series formula with the parallel formula. Forgetting unit conversions — milliampere to ampere, watt to kilowatt, seconds to hours. Drawing messy circuit diagrams with wrong symbols. Confusing resistance with resistivity in definitions.

**Meera:** How many numericals before I feel solid?

**Arjun:** Fifteen to twenty varied ones, minimum. All the NCERT in-text and back exercises, then last five years of board papers. By the end you should be able to look at a circuit and immediately see the simplification path.

**Meera:** And theory — is there anything from this chapter that's pure theory?

**Arjun:** The factors affecting resistance, why alloys are used in heating elements, why tungsten in bulb filaments, why home appliances are wired in parallel. Four to five 2-markers can come from these. Easy marks if your NCERT phrasing is exact.
