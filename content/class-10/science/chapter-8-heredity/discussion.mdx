## Mendel's Experiments

**Meera:** Arjun, Mendel's pea plant experiments were in the 1860s but nobody paid attention for decades. Why is he such a big deal in NCERT now?

**Arjun:** Because he was the first biologist to treat heredity mathematically. He did not just observe — he counted. He picked pea plants because they had clear contrasting traits — tall vs dwarf, round vs wrinkled seeds, violet vs white flowers. He controlled which plants pollinated which, then counted the offspring generation by generation.

**Meera:** And when he saw a 3:1 ratio in F2, he figured out there must be invisible "factors" governing inheritance.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Those factors are what we now call genes. Mendel did all this in a monastery garden in what was then Czechoslovakia. Indian parallel — at IARI Pusa, scientists today use similar controlled breeding to develop new wheat varieties like Pusa Tejas. Same principle, modern crops.

**Meera:** Okay — monohybrid cross. TT crossed with tt. All F1 are Tt and appear tall because T is dominant.

**Arjun:** Yes. Every F1 plant gets one T from the tall parent and one t from the dwarf parent. All Tt. All look tall. But they secretly carry the recessive t inside.

**Meera:** Self-pollinate the F1 and F2 shows 3 tall : 1 dwarf.

**Arjun:** Genotypic ratio 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt. Phenotypic ratio 3 tall : 1 dwarf, because TT and Tt both look tall. The dwarf reappears in the one-quarter that are tt. NCERT loves this — it shows recessive traits do not vanish, they just stay hidden.

## Dihybrid Cross

**Meera:** Arjun, the dihybrid cross — 9:3:3:1 ratio. That looks scary in the textbook.

**Arjun:** It is just two independent 3:1 ratios multiplied. Mendel crossed plants with round yellow seeds (RRYY) with wrinkled green seeds (rryy). F1 — all round yellow (RrYy). F2 — 9 round yellow, 3 round green, 3 wrinkled yellow, 1 wrinkled green.

**Meera:** The new combinations — round green and wrinkled yellow — they did not exist in the original parents. So the two traits were inherited separately.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Seed shape and seed colour sort independently into gametes. Law of Independent Assortment. It works because the genes sit on different chromosomes, and chromosomes get distributed independently when gametes form.

**Meera:** Drawing the Punnett square with 16 boxes — that has come as a 5-marker in PYQ, right?

**Arjun:** Multiple times. The four gamete types from each parent are RY, Ry, rY, ry. Make a 4×4 grid. Fill each box carefully — RY × RY gives RRYY, RY × Ry gives RRYy, and so on. Then count phenotypes. Rushing this loses marks — write the gamete types at the top and side clearly, then go cell by cell.

**Meera:** And practice it until I can do all 16 boxes without thinking?

**Arjun:** Yes. The dihybrid Punnett is one of those questions where neatness and method beat raw speed. Examiners follow your logic — if your grid is clean and your phenotype count adds up to 16, you get full marks.

## Sex Determination

**Meera:** Arjun, in humans, females are XX and males are XY. The father's sperm decides the baby's sex.

**Arjun:** Scientifically — yes. Mother's eggs always carry X because she is XX. Father's sperms are two kinds — half carry X, half carry Y. X-bearing sperm fertilises egg, child is XX, girl. Y-bearing sperm fertilises egg, child is XY, boy. 50-50 each time. Like a fair coin.

**Meera:** So blaming the mother for the sex of the child is scientifically baseless.

**Arjun:** Completely. The mother contributes only X. She has no role in deciding the sex. This is exactly why the PCPNDT Act makes prenatal sex determination illegal in India. The biology is straightforward — the misuse of ultrasound to discriminate is what the law targets.

**Meera:** Any other sex determination systems in nature?

**Arjun:** Yes — birds use a ZW system where males are ZZ and females are ZW. Some turtles and crocodiles — incubation temperature decides sex. But for the board exam, focus only on human XX-XY. Examiners will not ask about turtles, but if you slip a one-line mention as value-added knowledge, it shows depth.

**Meera:** And the sex determination chart — that is a guaranteed diagram question.

**Arjun:** Yes. Father at top (XY), mother at top (XX), then the four boxes — XX, XX, XY, XY. Two girls, two boys. 50% probability each. Label everything. Easy 3 marks.

## Genes, Proteins, and Traits

**Meera:** Arjun, how does a gene actually produce a trait? Like, how does the T gene make a plant tall?

**Arjun:** Gene codes for a protein, usually an enzyme. Say there is a gene for an enzyme involved in making a growth hormone. If the gene is functional (T version), the enzyme is efficient, plenty of growth hormone is made, plant grows tall. If the gene is mutated (t version), the enzyme is less efficient, less growth hormone, plant stays short.

**Meera:** And in a heterozygous plant (Tt), one good copy of the gene is enough?

**Arjun:** Exactly. One functional T produces sufficient enzyme. That is why Tt plants look as tall as TT plants. Dominant trait needs only one good copy. Recessive trait appears only when both copies are the less efficient version (tt) — not enough functional enzyme to grow tall.

**Meera:** So genes code for proteins, proteins control processes, processes determine traits. It is a chain.

**Arjun:** Perfect summary. Gene → Protein → Process → Trait. Any change (mutation) in the gene changes the protein, which changes the process, which changes the trait. This is the molecular basis of heredity. NEET-level questions love this exact pathway.

**Meera:** And genes sit on chromosomes. Body cells are diploid, gametes are haploid.

**Arjun:** Yes. Two copies of each gene in body cells, one in each gamete. At fertilisation, sperm and egg fuse — diploid restored in the zygote. This is the physical basis of Mendel's laws. He had no idea about chromosomes when he did his work — it was figured out much later.

## Exam Preparation Tips

**Meera:** Arjun, what tops the revision list?

**Arjun:** Four must-do items. One — monohybrid cross Punnett square. Show parents, F1, F2. Write genotypic ratio (1:2:1) and phenotypic ratio (3:1). Comes nearly every year, 3-5 marks. Two — sex determination chart with father XY, mother XX, four outcomes, 50-50 probability. Always add the line about why blaming the mother is baseless — examiners reward that. Three — dihybrid cross with 9:3:3:1 explanation. Four — gene-protein-trait pathway.

**Meera:** Any tricks I should use?

**Arjun:** Yaar, mnemonics work if they stick in your head. "Three-to-one, the dominant won" for monohybrid. "Mother gives X, Father decides the rest" for sex determination. For the dihybrid Punnett, practice the 4×4 grid four or five times before the exam — it should become muscle memory.

**Meera:** And labelling diagrams neatly?

**Arjun:** Non-negotiable. NCERT-style labels, pencil, ruler for lines. Examiners reward neatness. A beautifully labelled Punnett square or sex determination chart is among the easiest 5 marks in the whole biology section.

**Meera:** If MBBS at AIIMS is the plan — does this chapter come back later?

**Arjun:** Massively. The gene-protein pathway expands into a full unit on molecular biology in Class 12. Mendel's laws become the foundation for population genetics. Sex determination becomes more nuanced. Build this base rock-solid now and Class 11-12 biology becomes much smoother.
