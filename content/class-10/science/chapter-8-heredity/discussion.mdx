## Mendel's Experiments

**Child:** Maa, Mendel did his experiments with pea plants way back in the 1860s, but nobody paid attention for decades. Why is he such a big deal now?

**Mother:** Because Mendel did something no biologist before him had done — he treated biology like mathematics. He did not just observe; he counted. He chose pea plants carefully because they had easy-to-spot contrasting traits — tall versus dwarf, round versus wrinkled seeds, violet versus white flowers. He controlled which plants pollinated which, and then he counted the offspring in each generation. When he found that traits appeared in specific ratios — like 3:1 in the F2 generation — he realised there must be invisible "factors" governing inheritance. Those factors are what we now call genes.

**Child:** So in a monohybrid cross — say TT crossed with tt — all the F1 plants are Tt and they all appear tall because T is dominant over t?

**Mother:** Exactly, beta. Every F1 plant inherits one T from the tall parent and one t from the dwarf parent, making them all Tt. Since T is dominant, they all look tall. But they carry the recessive t quietly inside.

**Child:** And when we self-pollinate the F1 plants, the F2 generation shows both tall and dwarf in a 3:1 ratio?

**Mother:** Yes. The genotypic ratio is 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt. Since both TT and Tt appear tall, the phenotypic ratio is 3 tall : 1 dwarf. The one-quarter that are tt have received the recessive gene from both parents, so the dwarf trait reappears. This is the beauty of Mendel's work — he showed that traits do not blend or disappear; they are passed on as discrete units.

## Dihybrid Cross

**Child:** Maa, when Mendel tracked two traits at the same time, the F2 ratio became 9:3:3:1. That seems complicated.

**Mother:** It looks complex, but it is actually just two independent 3:1 ratios multiplied together. Mendel crossed plants with round yellow seeds (RRYY) with wrinkled green seeds (rryy). The F1 were all round yellow (RrYy). In F2, he got 9 round yellow, 3 round green, 3 wrinkled yellow, and 1 wrinkled green.

**Child:** The new combinations — round green and wrinkled yellow — those did not exist in the original parents. So the two traits were inherited separately?

**Mother:** Precisely. Seed shape does not depend on seed colour and vice versa. Each trait is sorted into gametes independently. This is Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment. It works because the genes for these traits sit on different chromosomes, and chromosomes are distributed independently when gametes form.

**Child:** Drawing the Punnett square for a dihybrid cross with all 16 boxes — is that commonly asked in exams?

**Mother:** Very commonly. It can be a 5-mark question. Practice making the 4-by-4 grid. The four gamete types from each parent are RY, Ry, rY, and ry. Fill in each box carefully and then count the phenotypes. Take your time — rushing leads to silly errors.

## Sex Determination

**Child:** Maa, in humans, females are XX and males are XY. So the father's sperm determines whether the baby will be a boy or a girl?

**Mother:** That is scientifically correct. The mother's eggs always carry an X chromosome because she is XX. The father's sperms are of two kinds — half carry X and half carry Y. If an X-bearing sperm fertilises the egg, the child will be XX — a girl. If a Y-bearing sperm does, the child will be XY — a boy. It is a 50-50 chance each time, like flipping a fair coin.

**Child:** So blaming a mother for the sex of the child is completely wrong?

**Mother:** Absolutely. It is scientifically baseless and deeply unfair. The mother contributes only X chromosomes — she has no role in determining whether the child is a boy or a girl. This is why the PCPNDT Act in India makes it illegal to use ultrasound or any test to determine the sex of an unborn baby — to prevent the harmful practice of sex-selective practices.

**Child:** Are there other ways sex is determined in nature?

**Mother:** Yes. In birds, the system is reversed — males are ZZ and females are ZW. In some turtles and crocodiles, the temperature at which eggs incubate determines whether the hatchlings are male or female. But for your CBSE exam, focus on the human XX-XY system.

## Genes, Proteins, and Traits

**Child:** Maa, how does a gene actually produce a trait? Like, how does a gene make a plant tall?

**Mother:** A gene contains the code for making a specific protein — usually an enzyme. Let us take plant height as an example. Suppose there is a gene that codes for an enzyme involved in producing a growth hormone. If the gene is functional (the T version), it makes an efficient enzyme, plenty of growth hormone is produced, and the plant grows tall. If the gene is altered (the t version), the enzyme it produces is less efficient, less growth hormone is made, and the plant stays short.

**Child:** And in a heterozygous plant (Tt), even one working copy of the gene is enough?

**Mother:** Exactly. One functional T gene produces sufficient enzyme to maintain normal height. That is why Tt plants look just as tall as TT plants. The dominant trait needs only one good copy of the gene. The recessive trait appears only when both copies (tt) are the less efficient version — because there is simply not enough functional enzyme.

**Child:** So genes code for proteins, proteins control processes, and processes determine traits. It is like a chain.

**Mother:** Perfect summary, beta. Gene → Protein → Process → Trait. Any change (mutation) in the gene changes the protein, which changes the process, which changes the trait.

## Exam Preparation Tips

**Child:** Maa, what are the must-study topics from this chapter?

**Mother:** First, the monohybrid cross with a Punnett square — know how to show the cross, the genotypic ratio (1:2:1), and the phenotypic ratio (3:1). This appears almost every year, worth 3 to 5 marks. Second, sex determination in humans — draw the chart showing XX and XY, explain how the father's sperm decides the sex. Third, be ready to explain the dihybrid cross and the 9:3:3:1 ratio. And fourth, understand how traits are expressed through the gene-protein pathway.

**Child:** Any tricks for remembering all this?

**Mother:** For the monohybrid cross, remember "Three-to-one, the dominant won." For sex determination, remember "Mother gives X, Father decides the rest." For the dihybrid cross, practise the Punnett square several times until filling in the 16 boxes becomes automatic. And always label everything clearly in your diagrams — examiners reward neatness and completeness.
