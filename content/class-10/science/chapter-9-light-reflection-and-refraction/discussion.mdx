## Reflection and Spherical Mirrors

**Meera:** Arjun, in the prelim paper there was a 5-marker on mirror formula. I had the formula but messed up the sign convention.

**Arjun:** Sign convention is the silent killer in this chapter. NCERT spells it out in one box — incident ray comes from the left, all distances measured from the pole, downward is negative. Half the class loses marks here.

**Meera:** Concave and convex — they behave totally differently. What is the cleanest way to explain why?

**Arjun:** Picture the inside of a steel kadhai — concave. Curves inward. Parallel rays hit and converge at the focus. Now the outside of the kadhai — convex. Curves outward. Reflected rays spread apart, never actually meet — they only appear to come from a point behind the mirror.

**Meera:** For concave mirrors, image type depends on where the object is. Six cases — that confuses me every time.

**Arjun:** Think of it as a smooth progression. Object at infinity — image is tiny at F. Move the object closer, image moves outward and gets bigger. Object at C — image at C, same size as object. Object between C and F — image shoots beyond C, magnified and inverted. Object at F — image at infinity. Object between F and pole — image flips to virtual, upright, enlarged, behind the mirror. That last case is exactly your mummy's shaving mirror or makeup mirror.

**Meera:** And a convex mirror is always the same — virtual, upright, smaller.

**Arjun:** Always. Which is why autorickshaw rearview mirrors and truck side mirrors are convex — the image is never confusing, always upright, and shows a wider field of view. That "wider field of view" point is examiner-favourite — write it explicitly.

## Mirror Formula and Sign Convention

**Meera:** Arjun, the mirror formula is 1/v + 1/u = 1/f. But I keep tripping on the signs.

**Arjun:** Method that works every time. Draw a quick sketch first — mirror, principal axis, object. Direction of incoming light is positive. For a concave mirror, object, focus, and centre of curvature are all on the same side as the incoming light — so u is negative, f is negative, v is negative when image is real.

**Meera:** And for a convex mirror?

**Arjun:** Focus is behind the mirror, opposite to incoming light — so f is positive. Image is also behind the mirror — so v is positive.

**Meera:** So assign signs before substituting?

**Arjun:** Always. Rule number one. Write down: u = ?, f = ?, apply signs, substitute, solve. Then check the sign of v. Negative v — real image, same side as object. Positive v — virtual, behind the mirror. Then magnification m = -v/u. Positive m means upright, negative means inverted.

**Meera:** What is the most common mistake?

**Arjun:** Forgetting the negative sign on u or f for concave mirrors. Second-most-common — mixing up the mirror formula (1/v + 1/u = 1/f) with the lens formula (1/v - 1/u = 1/f). One sign difference, full marks gone. Write both formulae side by side on a flashcard, revise before every practice session until they are automatic.

## Refraction and Snell's Law

**Meera:** Arjun, why does a straw look bent when I dip it in a glass of water? That is refraction, right?

**Arjun:** Yes. Light from the submerged part travels from water into air at the surface, speeds up, bends away from the normal. Your eyes trace the light backward in straight lines, so the submerged part appears at a shifted position. The straw is straight — it is the light path that bends.

**Meera:** And Snell's Law gives the exact amount of bending.

**Arjun:** n = sin i / sin r. Refractive index of water is about 1.33, glass about 1.5, diamond about 2.42. Diamond's high refractive index is why it sparkles — light bends so dramatically inside it that you get those brilliant flashes of colour. Useful detail to slip into a value-added answer.

**Meera:** And the swimming pool looking shallower than it really is — also refraction.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Light from the bottom bends away from the normal as it exits the water into air. Your brain interprets the bent rays as coming from a higher point, so the pool looks shallower than it really is. Safety angle — a pool that looks one metre deep might be one and a half metres. Always check the depth markings. NCERT mentions this example specifically.

## Lenses

**Meera:** Arjun, lenses work on refraction. Convex converges, concave diverges.

**Arjun:** Right. Convex is thicker in the middle, light entering from the edges bends inward and converges at the focus on the other side. Concave is thinner in the middle, light bends outward and diverges — appears to come from a virtual focus on the same side as the incoming light.

**Meera:** The image table for a convex lens has six cases too — similar to a concave mirror?

**Arjun:** Same six-case pattern. Object at infinity — image at F2, tiny. Object beyond 2F1 — image between F2 and 2F2, diminished. Object at 2F1 — image at 2F2, same size. Object between F1 and 2F1 — image beyond 2F2, magnified. Object at F1 — image at infinity. Object between F1 and O — image virtual, upright, magnified, on the same side as the object. That last case is exactly how a magnifying glass works.

**Meera:** And a concave lens always gives a virtual, upright, diminished image?

**Arjun:** Always. No matter where the object is. That predictability is exactly why concave lenses are used to correct myopia — near-sightedness.

## Power of a Lens

**Meera:** Arjun, my mama went to Aravind Eye Hospital last month, came back with a prescription of -3.0 D. What does that mean exactly?

**Arjun:** D is dioptre, the unit of lens power. Power P = 1 divided by focal length in metres. So -3.0 D means f = 1 / -3 ≈ -0.33 m, or about -33 cm. The negative sign tells us it is a concave lens — used for myopia. If the prescription were +2.0 D, that would be a convex lens with f = +50 cm, used for hypermetropia.

**Meera:** So higher dioptre number means a stronger lens.

**Arjun:** Yes — higher absolute value of power means the lens bends light more sharply. +5 D is much more powerful than +1 D. Opticians across India write prescriptions in dioptres because it is more practical than quoting focal lengths in centimetres. Mentioning Aravind Eye Hospital or any ophthalmologist visit in your answer is a nice real-life touch — examiners notice.

**Meera:** I might go MBBS and consider ophthalmology actually.

**Arjun:** This whole chapter is the foundation. The human eye chapter (Class 10 Ch 10) builds straight on top of it. Then in Class 11-12 you get into wave optics, total internal reflection, optical instruments. NEET physics loves lens problems with sign conventions.

## Ray Diagrams and Exam Strategy

**Meera:** Arjun, ray diagrams carry a lot of marks. What standard rules?

**Arjun:** For mirrors, three standard rays. One — ray parallel to the principal axis reflects through the focus. Two — ray passing through the focus reflects parallel to the axis. Three — ray passing through the centre of curvature retraces its path. For lenses — ray parallel to the axis refracts through the focus; ray through the optical centre passes straight; ray through the focus refracts parallel to the axis. Use any two of three to locate the image.

**Meera:** Pencil or pen?

**Arjun:** Pencil and ruler. Straight lines drawn with a ruler look clean and earn more marks. Label everything — pole or optical centre, focus, centre of curvature, object, image. Use arrows for direction. State explicitly whether the image is real or virtual, erect or inverted, magnified or diminished.

**Meera:** What are the biggest traps in numerical problems?

**Arjun:** Three. One — wrong sign convention. Always sketch first and assign signs before substituting. Two — confusing mirror formula (plus) with lens formula (minus). Three — magnification is -v/u for mirrors but v/u for lenses. Keep those three distinctions clean and most of the chapter is yours.

**Meera:** Got it. Sketch first, sign convention, formula on a flashcard, magnification with the right sign.

**Arjun:** Yes. And revise the NCERT examples — those exact problems get rephrased in board exams. Pure muscle memory work. The chapter is generous to anyone who practises 30-40 problems carefully.
