## Reflection and Spherical Mirrors

**Child:** Maa, I know a plane mirror just gives a normal reflection. But what makes concave and convex mirrors behave so differently?

**Mother:** It all comes down to the shape of the reflecting surface. Picture the inside of a shiny steel bowl — that is like a concave mirror. When parallel rays of light hit different parts of this curved surface, each ray bounces off obeying the same law of reflection, but because the surface curves inward, all the reflected rays meet at a single point — the focus. That is why we say a concave mirror converges light. Now think of the outside of that bowl — a convex mirror. The surface curves outward, so reflected rays spread apart. They never actually meet; they only appear to come from a point behind the mirror.

**Child:** For a concave mirror, the image changes depending on where I place the object. That is confusing — there are six different cases.

**Mother:** Think of it as a smooth progression. When the object is very far away, the image is a tiny dot at the focus. As you bring the object closer, the image moves outward and gets larger. When the object reaches the centre of curvature C, the image is at C too and is the same size. Bring the object even closer — between C and F — and the image shoots beyond C and becomes magnified. At the focus F, the image goes to infinity. And once the object is between F and the pole, something special happens — the image flips to being virtual, upright, and enlarged, appearing behind the mirror. That is exactly how your shaving mirror or makeup mirror works — you stand close, between F and P.

**Child:** And for a convex mirror, it is always the same type of image?

**Mother:** Yes — always virtual, upright, and smaller. That is precisely why auto-rickshaws and trucks use convex mirrors as side mirrors. The image is never confusing — always upright — and the mirror shows a wider view of the road behind, which is crucial for safe driving.

## Mirror Formula and Sign Convention

**Child:** Maa, the mirror formula is 1 over v plus 1 over u equals 1 over f. But the sign convention trips me up every time.

**Mother:** Let me give you a method that always works. First, draw a quick sketch — place the mirror, mark the principal axis, and show where the object is. The direction the light comes from is your positive direction. For a concave mirror, the object, focus, and centre of curvature are all on the same side as the incoming light, so u, f, and v (when the image is real) are all negative. For a convex mirror, the focus is behind the mirror — on the opposite side from the incoming light — so f is positive, and v is also positive because the image forms behind the mirror.

**Child:** So I should always assign signs before putting numbers into the formula?

**Mother:** Always. That is the number one rule. Write down: u = ?, f = ?, apply signs, substitute, solve. After you get v, check its sign — negative v means the image is real (same side as object), positive v means virtual (behind the mirror). Then calculate magnification m = -v/u. Positive m means upright, negative means inverted. Check every answer against what you expect from the image formation table.

**Child:** What is the most common mistake students make?

**Mother:** Forgetting to apply the negative sign to u or f for concave mirrors. The second biggest mistake is mixing up the mirror formula (plus sign) with the lens formula (minus sign). Write both formulae on a separate sheet and review them before every practice session until they are automatic.

## Refraction and Snell's Law

**Child:** Maa, why does a stick look bent when I dip it in water? Is that refraction?

**Mother:** Yes, beta. When light travels from water into air, it speeds up and bends away from the normal. Your eyes trace the light rays backward in straight lines, so the submerged part of the stick appears to be at a different position than it actually is. The stick is perfectly straight — it is the light path that bends at the surface.

**Child:** And Snell's Law gives us the exact amount of bending?

**Mother:** Correct. The refractive index n equals sine of the angle of incidence divided by sine of the angle of refraction. For water, n is about 1.33 — meaning light travels 1.33 times faster in vacuum than in water. For glass, it is about 1.5. For diamond, it is about 2.42, which is why diamond bends light so dramatically and produces those brilliant flashes of colour.

**Child:** The swimming pool looking shallower than it really is — that is also refraction?

**Mother:** Exactly. Light from the bottom of the pool bends away from the normal as it exits the water into air. Your brain interprets the bent rays as if they came from a point higher up, making the pool appear shallower. This is actually a safety concern — a pool that looks one metre deep might really be one and a half metres deep. Always check the actual depth markings.

## Lenses

**Child:** Maa, lenses work on refraction, right? A convex lens converges light and a concave lens diverges it?

**Mother:** That is right. A convex lens is thicker in the middle, so light entering from the edges bends inward towards the centre, converging at the focus on the other side. A concave lens is thinner in the middle, so light bends outward, diverging as if it came from a virtual focus on the same side as the incoming light.

**Child:** The image table for a convex lens has six cases — just like the concave mirror. Is there a pattern?

**Mother:** Very similar pattern. When the object is far away, the image is tiny and near the focus. As the object moves closer to 2F, the image moves to 2F on the other side and is the same size. Between F and 2F, the image becomes enlarged and moves farther away. At F, the image goes to infinity. And when the object is between F and the optical centre, the image becomes virtual, upright, and magnified — on the same side as the object. This is how a magnifying glass works — you hold it close to the text, placing the text between F and O.

**Child:** And a concave lens always gives a virtual, upright, diminished image?

**Mother:** Always. No matter where the object is. That predictability makes concave lenses useful in specific applications, like correcting myopia (near-sightedness).

## Power of a Lens

**Child:** Maa, when Chacha went to the eye doctor, his prescription said -3.0 D. What does that mean?

**Mother:** The D stands for dioptre, which is the unit of lens power. Power P equals 1 divided by the focal length in metres. So -3.0 D means the focal length is 1 divided by -3, which is approximately -0.33 metres or -33 centimetres. The negative sign tells us it is a concave lens — used for correcting myopia. If the prescription were +2.0 D, that would be a convex lens with focal length +50 cm, used for hypermetropia.

**Child:** So a higher dioptre number means a stronger lens?

**Mother:** Yes — a higher absolute value of power means the lens bends light more. A +5 D lens is much more powerful than a +1 D lens. Opticians across India use dioptres as the standard unit because it is more practical than quoting focal lengths in centimetres for everyday prescriptions.

## Ray Diagrams and Exam Strategy

**Child:** Maa, drawing ray diagrams is worth a lot of marks. What rules should I follow?

**Mother:** For mirrors, remember three standard rays. First, a ray parallel to the principal axis reflects through the focus. Second, a ray passing through the focus reflects parallel to the axis. Third, a ray passing through the centre of curvature bounces straight back on itself. For lenses, the three rays are: parallel to the axis refracts through the focus; through the optical centre passes straight; through the focus refracts parallel to the axis. Use any two of these three to locate the image.

**Child:** Should I use a ruler?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Straight lines drawn with a ruler look clean and earn more marks. Label the pole or optical centre, the focus, the centre of curvature (for mirrors), the object, and the image. Use arrows to show direction. State clearly whether the image is real or virtual, erect or inverted, and magnified or diminished. Complete characterisation of the image is what examiners want.

**Child:** What are the biggest traps in numerical problems?

**Mother:** Three things to watch out for. First, applying the wrong sign convention — always draw a quick diagram and assign signs before substituting. Second, confusing the mirror formula (1/v + 1/u = 1/f) with the lens formula (1/v - 1/u = 1/f) — note the plus versus minus. Third, in magnification, remember it is -v/u for mirrors but v/u for lenses. If you keep these three distinctions clear, you will avoid the most common errors.
