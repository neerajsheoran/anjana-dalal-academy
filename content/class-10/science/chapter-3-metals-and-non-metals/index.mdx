## Physical Properties of Metals

Look around your home. The steel utensils in the kitchen, the copper wiring inside the walls, the aluminium foil wrapping your roti, the gold chain your grandmother wears — all of these are **metals**. Metals share a set of physical characteristics that make them uniquely useful:

- **Lustre:** Freshly polished metals gleam and reflect light. This shining appearance is called metallic lustre — it is why silver jewellery sparkles.
- **Hardness:** Most metals are tough and resist being scratched. However, alkali metals like sodium and potassium are remarkably soft — you can slice through them with a butter knife.
- **Malleability:** Metals can be hammered or rolled into thin sheets without shattering. Gold is extraordinarily malleable — a single gram can be beaten into a sheet covering roughly one square metre.
- **Ductility:** Metals can be stretched into thin wires. Gold again leads — approximately 2 km of fine wire can be pulled from just one gram.
- **Thermal conductivity:** Metals transfer heat efficiently. Silver and copper are the finest heat conductors, which is why copper-bottomed cookware heats evenly. Lead and mercury, however, are notably poor at conducting heat.
- **Electrical conductivity:** Metals carry electric current well. Copper and aluminium dominate India's electrical wiring because they combine good conductivity with affordability.
- **Sonority:** Strike a metal with a hard object and it rings. Temple bells and school bells exploit this ringing quality.
- **Physical state:** Nearly every metal is solid at room temperature. The lone exception is **mercury**, which flows as a silvery liquid.

> **Key idea:** Metals are typically lustrous, hard, malleable, ductile, sonorous, and excellent conductors. Notable exceptions include liquid mercury, knife-soft sodium, and poorly conducting lead.

## Physical Properties of Non-metals

**Non-metals** occupy the other end of the spectrum:

- They generally lack lustre and appear dull (though **iodine** crystals have a metallic shine).
- They are **brittle** — tap a piece of sulphur and it crumbles. They cannot be hammered into sheets or drawn into wires.
- They are **poor conductors** of heat and electricity. The outstanding exception is **graphite**, which conducts electricity and is used in pencil leads and electrodes.
- At room temperature, non-metals exist as solids (carbon, sulphur) or gases (oxygen, nitrogen). **Bromine** is the only non-metal that is a liquid at room temperature.
- They do not produce a ringing sound — they are **non-sonorous**.

**Carbon's allotropes** beautifully illustrate how the same element can have wildly different properties depending on atomic arrangement:
- **Diamond** — each carbon atom bonds to four neighbours in a rigid 3D framework, making it the hardest known natural substance.
- **Graphite** — carbon atoms form flat hexagonal layers that slide over each other, making graphite soft, slippery, and able to conduct electricity.

> **Key idea:** Non-metals are generally brittle, dull, and poor conductors. Key exceptions: iodine is lustrous, graphite conducts electricity, and diamond is extremely hard.

## Chemical Properties of Metals

### Metals Reacting with Oxygen

Most metals combine with oxygen to form **metal oxides**, which are generally **basic** in character.

**Metal + Oxygen → Metal oxide**

- 2Cu(s) + O₂(g) → 2CuO(s) — copper turns black
- 4Al(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2Al₂O₃(s) — aluminium forms a protective oxide layer

Certain metal oxides, notably **aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃)** and **zinc oxide (ZnO)**, are **amphoteric** — they can react with both acids and bases:
- Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O
- Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O

The vigour of reaction with oxygen varies dramatically across metals:
- **Potassium and sodium** ignite spontaneously in open air, which is why they are stored submerged in kerosene oil.
- **Magnesium, aluminium, zinc** form a thin protective oxide coating that shields the bulk metal beneath.
- **Copper** slowly develops a black oxide layer but does not catch fire.
- **Silver and gold** remain unaffected by oxygen even when heated — their chemical nobility is why they are prized for jewellery.

> **Key idea:** Metal oxides are basic. Amphoteric oxides like Al₂O₃ and ZnO react with both acids and bases. Reactivity with oxygen ranges from violent (potassium) to non-existent (gold).

### Metals Reacting with Water

**Metal + Water → Metal oxide (or hydroxide) + Hydrogen gas**

The reaction intensity follows the reactivity order:
- **Potassium and sodium** explode on contact with cold water, generating enough heat to ignite the hydrogen produced.
  - 2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g) + heat
- **Calcium** reacts with cold water at a moderate pace. Hydrogen bubbles cling to the calcium piece, making it float.
  - Ca(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)
- **Magnesium** reacts only with hot water, not cold.
- **Aluminium, iron, zinc** react with steam but ignore cold or hot water.
  - 3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g)
- **Copper, silver, gold** show zero reaction with water under any conditions.

### Metals Reacting with Dilute Acids

**Metal + Dilute acid → Salt + Hydrogen gas**

Order of reactivity: Mg > Al > Zn > Fe. Copper sits below hydrogen in the reactivity series and therefore does not react with dilute HCl or H₂SO₄.

**Special note:** Hydrogen gas is not liberated when metals react with **nitric acid (HNO₃)** because HNO₃ is a powerful oxidising agent that converts H₂ to water.

**Aqua regia** — a freshly prepared mixture of 3 parts concentrated HCl and 1 part concentrated HNO₃ — is one of the few reagents capable of dissolving gold and platinum.

### Displacement Reactions with Salt Solutions

A more reactive metal can push a less reactive metal out of its salt solution:

Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

This is why when an iron key is left overnight in blue copper sulphate solution, the solution turns pale green and a reddish coating appears on the key.

> **Key idea:** The reactivity series ranks metals by decreasing reactivity: K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > H > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au. This order determines reactions with water, acids, and salt solutions.

## The Reactivity Series

The **reactivity series** (or activity series) arranges metals from most reactive at the top to least reactive at the bottom:

**K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > [H] > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au**

Key implications:
- Metals **above hydrogen** can displace hydrogen from dilute acids; those below cannot.
- A metal higher in the series can displace one lower from its salt solution.
- The most reactive metals (K, Na, Ca) are never found as free elements in nature — they always occur in compounds.
- The least reactive metals (Ag, Au) sometimes occur in native (free) form.

> **Key idea:** The reactivity series predicts displacement outcomes and guides how metals are extracted from their ores.

## Ionic Bonding — How Metals and Non-metals Combine

When metals and non-metals react, they do so by **transferring electrons**. Metals **give away** electrons, forming positively charged **cations**. Non-metals **accept** those electrons, forming negatively charged **anions**. The strong electrostatic pull between opposite charges holds them together.

**Sodium chloride (NaCl) formation:**
- Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ (sodium surrenders 1 electron)
- Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (chlorine accepts 1 electron)
- Na⁺ and Cl⁻ attract each other powerfully.

**Magnesium chloride (MgCl₂) formation:**
- Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ (magnesium surrenders 2 electrons)
- Each Cl gains 1 electron → 2Cl⁻

Compounds formed this way are called **ionic compounds** (or electrovalent compounds).

### Properties of Ionic Compounds

1. **Physical nature:** Solid, hard, yet brittle — they shatter under sharp blows.
2. **High melting and boiling points:** Enormous energy is needed to overcome the strong ionic attractions.
3. **Solubility:** Dissolve readily in water but not in organic solvents like petrol or kerosene.
4. **Electrical conductivity:** Conduct electricity when melted or dissolved in water (ions become free to move), but NOT in solid form (ions locked in a rigid lattice).

> **Key idea:** Ionic compounds form through electron transfer from metals to non-metals. They have high melting points, dissolve in water, and conduct electricity only when molten or in solution.

## Extracting Metals from Their Ores

### Minerals, Ores, and Gangue

- **Minerals:** Naturally occurring compounds or elements in the earth's crust.
- **Ores:** Minerals from which a metal can be extracted economically at scale.
- **Gangue:** Unwanted rocky impurities (sand, clay) mixed with the ore.
- **Enrichment (concentration) of ore:** Removing gangue before the actual extraction begins.

### Extraction Depends on Reactivity

**Low-reactivity metals** (Cu, Hg, Ag, Au):
- Often found as free metals or simple oxide/sulphide ores.
- Extracted by heating alone.
- Example: Cinnabar (HgS) is roasted in air to get mercury:
  2HgS(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2HgO(s) + 2SO₂(g), then 2HgO(s) → 2Hg(l) + O₂(g)

**Medium-reactivity metals** (Zn, Fe, Pb):
- Found as oxides, sulphides, or carbonates.
- **Roasting:** Heating sulphide ores in excess air converts them to oxides.
  2ZnS(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2ZnO(s) + 2SO₂(g)
- **Calcination:** Heating carbonate ores in limited air converts them to oxides.
  ZnCO₃(s) → ZnO(s) + CO₂(g)
- **Reduction with carbon (coke):** ZnO(s) + C(s) → Zn(s) + CO(g)

**Thermit reaction:** Fe₂O₃(s) + 2Al(s) → 2Fe(l) + Al₂O₃(s) + Heat
This spectacularly exothermic displacement reaction produces molten iron and is used to weld cracked railway tracks across the Indian Railways network.

**High-reactivity metals** (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al):
- Too reactive to be reduced by carbon.
- Extracted by **electrolysis** of their molten compounds.
- Example: Electrolysis of molten NaCl deposits sodium at the cathode and releases chlorine at the anode.

### Electrolytic Refining

To purify metals after extraction:
- **Anode:** Block of impure metal
- **Cathode:** Thin strip of pure metal
- **Electrolyte:** Solution of the metal's salt
- Pure metal dissolves from the anode, travels through the solution, and deposits onto the cathode.
- Insoluble impurities settle as **anode mud** at the bottom.

> **Key idea:** Extraction method matches reactivity: heat alone for low-reactivity metals, carbon reduction for mid-range metals, and electrolysis for highly reactive metals.

## Corrosion and Its Prevention

**Corrosion** slowly destroys metals through chemical reactions with environmental substances — oxygen, moisture, acids, and gases.

- **Iron:** Develops a flaky reddish-brown coating called rust (hydrated iron oxide). Both air and water must be present for rusting.
- **Silver:** Acquires a dark tarnish (silver sulphide) from sulphur compounds in the air.
- **Copper:** Gains a distinctive green patina (basic copper carbonate) from reacting with moist CO₂.

### Fighting Corrosion

- **Painting, oiling, greasing:** Creates a physical barrier blocking air and moisture.
- **Galvanisation:** Coating iron with a thin layer of zinc. Even if scratched, zinc corrodes preferentially, sacrificially protecting the iron beneath.
- **Electroplating and anodising:** Depositing a protective metal layer or thickening the natural oxide film.
- **Alloying:** Mixing metals to enhance properties.

### Alloys — Engineered Metal Mixtures

An **alloy** is a uniform (homogeneous) mixture of two or more metals, or a metal with a non-metal. Alloys are designed to combine the best features of their components:

- **Steel** (iron + 0.05% carbon) — much harder and stronger than pure iron
- **Stainless steel** (iron + chromium + nickel) — resists rust beautifully
- **Brass** (copper + zinc) — golden colour, used in temple bells and decorative items
- **Bronze** (copper + tin) — strong and corrosion-resistant, used for statues
- **Solder** (lead + tin) — melts at low temperature, perfect for joining electrical components
- **22-carat gold** (22 parts gold + 2 parts copper/silver) — harder than pure 24-carat gold, suitable for jewellery

When mercury is one component, the alloy is called an **amalgam** (used in dental fillings).

Alloys typically have **lower electrical conductivity** and **lower melting points** compared to the pure metals they contain.

> **Key idea:** Corrosion is combated through barriers (paint, galvanisation) or by alloying. Alloys tailor metal properties for specific applications — from stainless steel utensils to gold jewellery.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Tata Steel Jamshedpur — India's first integrated steel plant.** Iron ore from the Noamundi mines is railed in, mixed with coke and limestone, and reduced in a blast furnace at temperatures above 1500°C. The skeletal reaction Fe₂O₃ + 3C → 2Fe + 3CO that you balance in a 2-mark answer powers an entire city. Jamshedpur runs on this single textbook displacement-reduction reaction, producing steel for the Mumbai metro, the Vande Bharat coaches, and the rebar in your school building.

**SAIL Bhilai and Bokaro — the public-sector steel giants.** The Steel Authority of India operates massive plants in Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand using the same blast-furnace chemistry. Their rail track output supplies Indian Railways. The next time you ride a train from Mumbai to Delhi, the rails under you started as Fe₂O₃ ore reduced by coke — straight from your chapter on metals.

**Hindalco Renukoot and NALCO Odisha — India's aluminium hubs.** Aluminium is too high in the reactivity series for carbon to reduce, so these plants run the Hall-Heroult electrolysis on alumina dissolved in molten cryolite at around 950°C. The reason every aluminium plant is built near a power source — Renukoot near Rihand dam, NALCO near the Talcher coalfields — is the enormous electricity demand of electrolysis. Reactivity series, geography, and economics meet at a single furnace.

### Do it yourself — the 10-day rust prevention experiment

Iron rusting in the Mumbai monsoon or the Chennai humidity is the textbook example of corrosion. Test how different treatments protect a nail.

**Materials:** 5 identical iron nails (the cheap kind from a hardware shop), 5 small jars or glasses, water, some cooking oil, a piece of zinc wire or coin if available, transparent nail polish or a small bottle of paint, and a piece of plastic film.

**Setup the 5 conditions, one nail per jar:**

1. **Bare nail in water** — control. Half submerged so it sees both water and air.
2. **Nail coated thoroughly in oil**, half submerged in water.
3. **Nail painted with nail polish or paint**, half submerged in water.
4. **Nail wrapped tightly with the zinc wire or pressed against a zinc surface**, half submerged (this mimics galvanisation).
5. **Nail sealed in an airtight container with no water at all** — just air.

Observe and record every day for 10 days. Note the colour change, the area of rust, the smell, and the appearance of the water. Photograph on Day 1, Day 5, and Day 10.

*⏱ 30 minutes setup + 5 minutes daily for 10 days · 🧹 Low — keep jars on a tray to catch any leaks · 👨‍👩‍👧 Adult help for paint/nail polish handling and finding zinc*

After 10 days, rank the treatments by how well they protected the nail. Connect each result to the chapter — why did the oil-coated nail still rust slightly? Why did the zinc-wrapped nail stay cleanest? Why did the sealed-air-only nail not rust at all?

> **Show what you found:** Lay all five nails side by side and photograph them. Sketch each one in your notebook with a one-line caption explaining the chemistry. Show your dadu or papa — they have probably watched iron grills on the balcony rust through every Mumbai monsoon. Tell them one new thing you understood, like why painting the gate every two years is not just for looks but for sacrificial protection of the iron underneath. Ask them what they coat their tools with at home.

> **A question to keep asking all year:** *Every metal I see — a fan blade, a pressure cooker, a tap, a chain, a coin — is it pure or an alloy, and why did the designer choose that particular metal for that particular job?*
