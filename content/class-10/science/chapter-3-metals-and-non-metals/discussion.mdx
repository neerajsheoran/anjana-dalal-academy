## Physical Properties — Rules and Exceptions

**Child:** Maa, metals are shiny, hard, and conduct electricity — but there are so many exceptions! Mercury is a liquid, sodium is soft enough to cut with a knife, and lead barely conducts heat. How do I remember all this?

**Mother:** The trick is to learn the general rule first and then treat exceptions as a short separate list. CBSE examiners love testing exceptions — "name a metal that exists as a liquid at room temperature" (mercury), "name a non-metal that conducts electricity" (graphite), "name a non-metal with metallic lustre" (iodine). These are quick 1-mark questions that appear almost every year.

**Child:** Gold can be beaten into incredibly thin sheets — that is malleability, right?

**Mother:** Yes, beta. Gold is the champion of malleability — a single gram can be hammered into a sheet large enough to cover a small table. And silver is the best conductor of both heat and electricity. But we use copper and aluminium for wiring because they are far more affordable. India is expanding its electrical grid rapidly, and copper wire demand is enormous — this is where school chemistry connects to the real economy.

**Child:** Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon, yet diamond is the hardest substance and graphite is soft and slippery. How is that possible?

**Mother:** Because their atoms are arranged completely differently. In diamond, every carbon atom is bonded to four others in a three-dimensional cage — incredibly rigid. In graphite, carbon atoms form flat hexagonal sheets that slide over each other like playing cards in a deck. Same atoms, different architecture, completely different properties. This is a wonderful example of how structure determines behaviour.

## Chemical Properties and the Reactivity Series

**Child:** Maa, the reactivity series — K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, H, Cu, Hg, Ag, Au — I need to memorise this whole sequence?

**Mother:** You absolutely must. Here is a memory trick I used when I was in school: "Kaju Nahi Chahiye, Maa — Aam Le, Zara Fresh, Please Haan? Chuski Ho Sakti — Achhi Gold-wali." It is silly, but it sticks! The reactivity series tells you which displacement reactions are possible, how metals react with water and acids, and which extraction method to use.

**Child:** Metals above hydrogen react with dilute acids, and metals below hydrogen do not?

**Mother:** Correct. Copper, silver, and gold sit below hydrogen, so they will not produce hydrogen gas when dipped in dilute HCl or H₂SO₄. This is why your Nani's copper kalash can hold tamarind water without dissolving, but an iron kadhai would react. Well, actually copper does not react with dilute acids at all — iron does react slowly.

**Child:** What about metals reacting with water?

**Mother:** The pattern is beautiful in its consistency. Potassium and sodium react explosively with cold water — sodium dances on the water surface and sometimes catches fire. Calcium reacts steadily with cold water, producing gentle bubbles. Magnesium needs hot water. Aluminium and iron only react with steam. And copper, silver, gold — they simply refuse to react with water at all. Board exams regularly test this gradient.

## Extracting Metals from Ores

**Child:** So the extraction method depends on where the metal sits in the reactivity series?

**Mother:** Exactly. Think of it as three tiers. The least reactive metals like copper and mercury can be extracted by simply heating their ores — heat alone provides enough energy. Mid-range metals like iron and zinc need their ores to be first converted to oxides (by roasting or calcination) and then reduced with carbon or coke in a furnace. This is how the blast furnace at Tata Steel in Jamshedpur produces iron. And the most reactive metals like sodium and aluminium cannot be reduced by carbon at all — they require electrolysis of their molten compounds.

**Child:** NALCO in Odisha — that makes aluminium using electrolysis?

**Mother:** Yes, NALCO is one of Asia's largest aluminium producers. The Hall-Heroult process dissolves alumina in molten cryolite and passes electricity through it. This consumes enormous amounts of power, which is why aluminium plants are always built near hydroelectric stations or coalfields. When you study geography alongside chemistry, these connections become very clear.

**Child:** For corrosion prevention — galvanisation, painting, alloying — which one does the board exam ask about most?

**Mother:** Galvanisation and alloying come up most frequently. Galvanisation means coating iron with zinc — zinc corrodes first, sacrificing itself to protect the iron underneath. Alloying means mixing metals to engineer better properties. Stainless steel is iron mixed with chromium and nickel — it barely rusts. Brass is copper plus zinc. Bronze is copper plus tin. And remember that 22-carat gold is not pure gold — it is 22 parts gold mixed with 2 parts copper or silver to make it hard enough for jewellery.

## Ionic Compounds vs Covalent Compounds

**Child:** Maa, metals transfer electrons to non-metals to form ionic bonds. How is this different from covalent bonding where atoms share electrons?

**Mother:** Think of it as giving versus sharing. In ionic bonding, sodium completely hands over one electron to chlorine — sodium becomes Na⁺ and chlorine becomes Cl⁻. These oppositely charged ions attract each other strongly. In covalent bonding — which happens between two non-metals — atoms share electrons rather than transferring them. Water (H₂O) is covalent: each hydrogen shares one electron with oxygen.

**Child:** Drawing the electron-dot structure for NaCl — that is a board exam question, right?

**Mother:** A very common one worth 2-3 marks. Draw sodium with its single outer electron, chlorine with its seven outer electrons, show the electron transfer with an arrow, then indicate the charges: Na⁺ and Cl⁻. Practise the same for MgCl₂ and Na₂O as well. The examiner wants to see that you understand electron transfer and the resulting ionic charges.

## Exam Strategy

**Child:** How many marks does this chapter carry, Maa?

**Mother:** It is one of the weightier chapters — often 5 to 8 marks. The reactivity series, extraction methods, ionic bond formation with electron-dot structures, and properties of ionic compounds are the top-scoring areas. Questions comparing metals and non-metals, and questions on corrosion and alloys, also appear regularly.

**Child:** What should I prioritise?

**Mother:** Four things. First, memorise the reactivity series cold — it unlocks half the chapter. Second, know which extraction method goes with which reactivity tier and be able to explain why. Third, practise electron-dot structures for NaCl, MgCl₂, and Na₂O until you can draw them in your sleep. Fourth, prepare a table of at least five differences between ionic and covalent compounds. And always link the extraction method to the reactivity series — showing that logical thread impresses examiners.

**Child:** Does this chapter help with NEET preparation too?

**Mother:** Very much. The bonding concepts — ionic versus covalent — form the backbone of physical chemistry and organic chemistry in Classes 11 and 12. Understanding electron transfer, how ions form, and how ionic compounds differ from covalent compounds is essential for both NEET and JEE. India's steel, aluminium, and copper industries employ thousands of chemists who apply these exact principles in refining and quality control every day.
