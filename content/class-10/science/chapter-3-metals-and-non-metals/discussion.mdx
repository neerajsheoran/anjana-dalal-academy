## Chemical Properties and the Reactivity Series

**Meera:** Arjun, the reactivity series — K Na Ca Mg Al Zn Fe Pb H Cu Hg Ag Au. My memory trick is not working. Do you have a better one?

**Arjun:** The classic one — "Kaju Nahi Chahiye Mujhe, Aaj Zara Fine Plates Hai, Chai Hai, Sirf Aaye." Silly but it locked in for me after three days of repetition. Or you can split it into three blocks: K Na Ca Mg above water-reactive, Al Zn Fe Pb steam/acid-reactive, then H, then Cu Hg Ag Au noble. Three groups feels manageable.

**Meera:** And the displacement rule — above can displace below from a salt solution.

**Arjun:** Yes. The iron-nail-in-copper-sulphate practical is the classic example. Blue solution turns greenish, brown copper coats the nail. Last year a friend wrote "iron is below copper, that is why displacement happens." She lost 2 marks. The logic is reversed.

**Meera:** My uncle works at the Tata Steel rolling mill in Jamshedpur. He says they think about reactivity constantly — every alloy decision starts with where the metal sits in this series.

**Arjun:** Exactly. And aluminium plants like NALCO in Odisha and Hindalco in Renukoot run on the same logic. Aluminium is too reactive for carbon to reduce, so they use electrolysis of molten alumina dissolved in cryolite. Hall-Heroult process — that name is worth one mark in the extraction question.

**Meera:** That reminds me — board paper from 2022 asked which method is used for extraction of aluminium. Many wrote "reduction by coke." Wrong. Electrolytic reduction.

**Arjun:** That mistake cost a lot of students 2 marks. Reactivity series predicts the extraction method. Memorise the series, the rest falls out automatically.

## Extracting Metals from Ores

**Meera:** Arjun, the extraction process has three steps — concentration, reduction, refining. How deep does the board paper go?

**Arjun:** Usually focuses on reduction and refining. Concentration is a 1-mark recall — "what is gangue?" answer in one line. Reduction is where the marks are. They expect you to link the reactivity tier to the method — low-reactivity, heat alone; mid-reactivity, carbon reduction after roasting or calcination; high-reactivity, electrolysis.

**Meera:** Roasting versus calcination — what is the cleanest way to distinguish them?

**Arjun:** Roasting is for sulphide ores, heated in excess air to give the oxide and SO₂. Calcination is for carbonate ores, heated in limited air to give the oxide and CO₂. The equations are usually the 2-mark portion. I noted ZnS + O₂ → ZnO + SO₂ for roasting and ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂ for calcination.

**Meera:** And electrolytic refining of copper — anode impure, cathode pure, electrolyte CuSO₄, anode mud at the bottom. The Hindustan Copper plant at Khetri runs on this exact principle?

**Arjun:** Yes. And anode mud often contains silver and gold as valuable byproducts. The economics of copper refining sometimes depend on these byproducts. A pure chemistry textbook process driving a whole pricing model.

**Meera:** What about the thermit reaction? My physics teacher mentioned railway tracks.

**Arjun:** Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Fe + Al₂O₃ + heat. Massively exothermic. Indian Railways uses thermit welding to repair cracks in rails on remote sections. Aluminium reduces iron oxide, molten iron pours into the crack, sets, holds the track together. One reaction keeps thousands of kilometres of Indian rail safe.

## Ionic Compounds vs Covalent Compounds

**Meera:** Arjun, the electron-dot structure question — I always panic about which electrons to show.

**Arjun:** Show only the outermost shell electrons. For NaCl, sodium has one outer electron, chlorine has seven. Arrow from Na to Cl showing transfer. Mark charges Na⁺ and Cl⁻ on the products. That gets full marks.

**Meera:** And for MgCl₂?

**Arjun:** Magnesium has two outer electrons, donates one each to two chlorines. Two arrows, products are Mg²⁺ and 2Cl⁻. The trap is forgetting that magnesium must lose BOTH electrons, not just one. NCERT example on this is on the page just before the reactivity series — worth re-reading.

**Meera:** Properties of ionic compounds — high melting point, conducts in solution or molten, dissolves in water but not in petrol. That is a 5-mark table question?

**Arjun:** Often 5 marks. Five differences expected. I noted them as a five-row table. Physical state, melting point, conductivity in solid, conductivity in molten or solution, solubility in water versus organic solvents. Each difference contrasts ionic with covalent. Full table, full marks.

**Meera:** That sounds doable. I will copy your format.

## Exam Strategy

**Meera:** Arjun, how many marks does this whole chapter carry usually?

**Arjun:** 5 to 8. The heavy chapter for the chemistry section. Reactivity series unlocks half the chapter, electron-dot structures and ionic-covalent comparison together can pull 5 marks easily. Then extraction methods and corrosion fill the rest.

**Meera:** What is the single biggest mistake students make in this chapter?

**Arjun:** Not linking the reactivity series to extraction. They memorise the series, they memorise the extraction methods, but they fail to write the logic — "because sodium is highly reactive, carbon cannot reduce its oxide, so we use electrolysis." That linking sentence is what examiners want. Without it the answer is half-built.

**Meera:** And for NEET — does this chapter resurface?

**Arjun:** As metallurgy and chemical bonding. Class 11 chapter on "General Principles of Metallurgy" extends this exactly. Ionic bonding becomes a whole chapter in Class 11 chemistry. The basics we are drilling now — electron transfer, octet rule, ionic versus covalent — they reappear in every Class 12 organic and inorganic question.

**Meera:** And the industry side?

**Arjun:** Tata Steel Jamshedpur, SAIL Bhilai and Bokaro, Hindalco Renukoot, NALCO Odisha, Hindustan Copper Khetri. India's metals sector employs lakhs of people. The quality control labs at every one of these plants run reactivity-series chemistry every shift. We are studying the textbook version of what runs the country's industrial base.
