## The Nervous System

**Child:** Maa, I keep hearing that the neuron is the basic unit of the nervous system. But how does it actually send a message from one place to another?

**Mother:** Think of it like a relay race, beta. When something touches your skin, special cells called receptors detect it. They trigger a tiny chemical change at the tip of the neuron's dendrite, which creates an electrical pulse. This pulse zooms through the cell body and along a long fibre called the axon. When it reaches the end of the axon, it cannot jump directly to the next neuron — there is a small gap called the synapse. So the axon releases chemical substances called neurotransmitters that float across the gap and start a fresh pulse in the next neuron.

**Child:** So it is like passing a baton — electrical inside one neuron, then chemical at the gap, then electrical again in the next neuron?

**Mother:** Exactly. And here is something clever — the synapse works only in one direction, like a one-way valve. Signals can only go from the axon of one neuron to the dendrite of the next. This ensures messages travel in the correct order and do not get jumbled up.

## Reflex Actions

**Child:** Maa, yesterday I touched the hot kadhai by mistake and my hand pulled back before I even realised it was hot. How did my body react so fast?

**Mother:** That was a reflex action. Your body has a shortcut route for emergencies. The signal from the heat receptor in your finger travelled through a sensory neuron straight to your spinal cord — not your brain. In the spinal cord, a relay neuron quickly connected it to a motor neuron, which told your arm muscles to pull back. The whole thing happened in a fraction of a second.

**Child:** So my brain did not decide to move my hand?

**Mother:** No. Your brain learned about the burn only after your hand had already moved to safety. If the signal had to travel all the way up to your brain, get processed, and then come back, those extra milliseconds could mean a serious burn. The spinal cord handles it directly. That is why we call the pathway a reflex arc — receptor, sensory neuron, spinal cord, motor neuron, effector muscle.

**Child:** Is that also why someone with a spinal cord injury might not be able to pull their hand away from something hot?

**Mother:** Yes, beta. If the spinal cord is damaged at a certain point, reflex signals below that point cannot travel properly. Even voluntary movements below the injury may stop because the brain's commands also pass through the spinal cord.

## The Brain and Its Parts

**Child:** Maa, I know the brain has three main parts — fore-brain, mid-brain, and hind-brain. But what does each one actually do?

**Mother:** Let me explain with examples you can relate to. The fore-brain has the cerebrum — this is the biggest part, and it handles everything you do consciously. When you solve a maths problem, remember your friend's birthday, write an essay, or enjoy the taste of mango ice cream — that is all the cerebrum. Different areas of the cerebrum handle different senses — one area processes what you see, another what you hear, and so on.

**Child:** And the hind-brain?

**Mother:** The hind-brain has three parts. The cerebellum keeps your balance and coordinates smooth movements — when you ride your bicycle through our colony or catch a cricket ball, the cerebellum makes sure your muscles work together. The medulla oblongata quietly controls things you never think about — your heartbeat, your breathing, your blood pressure. And the pons acts like a bridge, passing messages between the cerebrum and the cerebellum.

**Child:** What about the mid-brain?

**Mother:** It handles some automatic reflexes — like when a loud horn honks and your head turns towards it without thinking. It connects the fore-brain to the hind-brain. The whole brain is protected by the skull, three protective membranes, and a cushioning fluid. Even so, head injuries need to be taken very seriously.

## Hormonal Control

**Child:** Maa, the nervous system sends electrical signals and is super fast. The endocrine system uses hormones — how is that different?

**Mother:** Think of it this way. The nervous system is like sending a WhatsApp message to one specific person — it is instant and reaches exactly who you want. The endocrine system is like putting an announcement on the school notice board — it is slower, everyone who walks past reads it, and the message stays up for a long time. Hormones travel through the blood and affect any organ that has the right receptors for that hormone.

**Child:** When I got scared by that dog on the street and my heart started racing — was that hormones?

**Mother:** Yes! Your adrenal glands released adrenaline into your blood. Adrenaline made your heart beat faster, your breathing speed up, and sent extra blood to your leg muscles so you could run. That is the fight-or-flight response. Other important hormones include insulin from the pancreas, which keeps your blood sugar in check, and thyroxin from the thyroid gland, which controls how fast your body uses energy.

**Child:** Dadi always says we must use iodised salt. Is that connected to hormones?

**Mother:** Very much. The thyroid gland needs iodine to make thyroxin. If you do not get enough iodine in your diet, the thyroid swells up trying to compensate — that swelling in the neck is called goitre. In many parts of India, especially hilly areas, the soil lacks iodine, so iodised salt is essential. And about diabetes — when the pancreas cannot produce enough insulin, sugar builds up in the blood. India has a very high number of diabetes patients, so understanding this is important for our health.

## Plant Hormones

**Child:** Maa, plants do not have a brain or nerves. So how do they know to grow towards sunlight?

**Mother:** Plants use hormones for all their coordination. The most important one is auxin. When sunlight hits a plant from one side, auxin moves to the shaded side. Auxin makes cells elongate, so the shaded side grows faster. The lit side grows slower. This unequal growth causes the stem to bend towards the light. We call this phototropism.

**Child:** So the plant is not really "choosing" to face the sun — it is just chemistry?

**Mother:** Precisely. And there are other plant hormones too. Gibberellins help stems grow taller and trigger flowering. Cytokinins promote cell division — they are found in areas where the plant is actively growing, like developing fruits. Abscisic acid does the opposite — it slows down growth and helps leaves fall off when the plant is stressed, like during a drought. And ethylene is a gas that ripens fruit — this is why Nani wraps raw mangoes in newspaper with a ripe banana. The ethylene from the banana speeds up the mango ripening.

**Child:** What about the chhui-mui plant that folds its leaves when I touch it? Is that also hormones?

**Mother:** That one is different. The chhui-mui, or touch-me-not plant, does not use growth for that movement. When you touch its leaves, cells at the base of each leaflet rapidly lose water. They shrink, and the leaf folds down. After some time, the cells refill with water and the leaves open again. It is a turgor pressure change, not a hormone-driven growth response.

## Exam Preparation Tips

**Child:** Maa, what are the most important things to study from this chapter for the board exam?

**Mother:** There are a few high-value areas. First, be able to draw and label a reflex arc clearly — this comes up very frequently and carries good marks. Second, know the differences between nervous and hormonal control — present them in a neat table. Third, learn the functions of each part of the brain — cerebrum, cerebellum, medulla, and their specific roles. Fourth, the plant hormones and different types of tropisms are regularly asked. And finally, understand the feedback mechanism — how insulin regulates blood sugar is a favourite example in exams.

**Child:** Should I memorise all five plant hormones?

**Mother:** Yes, know each one — auxin, gibberellins, cytokinins, abscisic acid, and ethylene — along with their functions. Also be ready to explain phototropism step by step using auxin redistribution. Practice drawing diagrams neatly and labelling them completely. Good diagrams can earn you full marks even if your written explanation is brief.
