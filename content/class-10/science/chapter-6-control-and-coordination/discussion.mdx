## The Nervous System

**Meera:** Arjun, I keep getting tangled up on how a nerve signal actually passes from one neuron to the next. Inside one neuron it is electrical, but at the synapse it becomes chemical?

**Arjun:** That is exactly the pattern. Receptor picks up the stimulus, generates an electrical pulse at the dendrite tip, the pulse races through the cell body and along the axon. At the axon terminal, the signal cannot jump the gap — it triggers the release of neurotransmitters. They diffuse across the synapse, land on the next neuron's dendrite, and spark a fresh electrical pulse.

**Meera:** So the synapse is like a one-way valve.

**Arjun:** Exactly — and that one-way nature is high-value in answers. NCERT specifically points out that signals can travel only from the axon of one neuron to the dendrite of the next, never backwards. That keeps messages in correct sequence. Examiners want this line word-for-word.

**Meera:** This is the kind of thing they study at NIMHANS in Bengaluru, right? Real neuroscience.

**Arjun:** Yes — and at AIIMS Delhi for neurology. If you go MBBS later, everything you learn in this chapter expands into a full neurophysiology unit in Class 11 and beyond.

## Reflex Actions

**Meera:** Arjun, last week at home Amma was frying puris and the oil splashed. My hand pulled back before I even registered the heat. That is the textbook reflex action, but how do I explain it in a 5-marker?

**Arjun:** Reflex arc — five parts in correct order. Receptor in the skin detects the heat. Sensory neuron carries the signal to the spinal cord. Relay neuron (or interneuron) in the spinal cord processes it. Motor neuron sends the response back. Effector muscle pulls your hand away.

**Meera:** And the brain finds out later?

**Arjun:** Right. The signal does reach the brain — but only after the action has already happened. If the brain had to be consulted first, you would already be burnt. The spinal cord handles it as a shortcut. That "saves precious time" line — write it explicitly, it carries marks.

**Meera:** What about someone with a spinal cord injury? Could that affect reflexes?

**Arjun:** Yes. If the spinal cord is damaged at a certain level, reflex signals below that point cannot complete the arc. Voluntary movement below the injury also stops because the brain's commands also travel through the spinal cord. This connects to public-health awareness — helmet laws exist precisely to protect the spinal cord.

## The Brain and Its Parts

**Meera:** Arjun, the brain has three main regions — fore-brain, mid-brain, hind-brain. The board loves asking the function of each part.

**Arjun:** Yes — almost every year. Fore-brain has the cerebrum. Largest, most wrinkled. Seat of thinking, reasoning, memory, voluntary actions. Different zones handle vision, hearing, smell, taste, touch.

**Meera:** And hind-brain?

**Arjun:** Three parts. Cerebellum — coordinates voluntary movements, maintains balance. Catching a cricket ball, riding a cycle through colony lanes — that is cerebellum. Medulla oblongata — controls involuntary actions like heartbeat, breathing, blood pressure, vomiting. Pons — relay station between cerebrum and cerebellum.

**Meera:** Mid-brain?

**Arjun:** Smaller, handles certain reflexes like turning your head towards a sudden sound. NCERT does not give too much detail on mid-brain, so do not overload your answer with it — just one clean line.

**Meera:** And the brain is protected by skull, meninges, and cerebrospinal fluid.

**Arjun:** Three layers of meninges, fluid acts like bubble wrap. This whole "brain protection" sub-question came as 2 marks last year. People who research the brain at NIMHANS work on injuries to these exact structures — concussions, strokes, all of it.

## Hormonal Control

**Meera:** Arjun, nervous system is electrical and fast. Endocrine system uses hormones in the blood. The comparison table — that has to come in the exam.

**Arjun:** Almost guaranteed. Five rows. Speed — milliseconds vs minutes-to-hours. Duration — brief vs sustained. Medium — electrical pulses vs chemical hormones in blood. Target — specific vs broad. Precision — highly localised vs widespread. Make this table neat and you have an easy 5 marks.

**Meera:** Adrenaline — last month a stray dog charged at me near the bus stop and my heart raced.

**Arjun:** Classic fight-or-flight. Adrenal glands sit on top of the kidneys, pump adrenaline into the blood. Heart rate up, breathing up, extra blood to leg muscles. Your body prepped you to run or fight. That story always works in answers — examiners love a relatable example.

**Meera:** Iodine and goitre — dadi always uses iodised salt. The thyroid swells if you do not get enough iodine, right?

**Arjun:** Right. Thyroid needs iodine to make thyroxin. Without it, the gland enlarges trying to compensate — goitre, that visible neck swelling. Common in hilly belts where soil iodine is low. AYUSH systems have traditional dietary recommendations for iodine-rich foods too, but iodised salt is the universal modern fix. India runs a national iodisation programme because of this.

**Meera:** And diabetes — pancreas not making enough insulin, blood sugar shoots up. India has one of the highest diabetes populations.

**Arjun:** Yes — and that is a high-value real-world connection to weave into a 3-marker about insulin's role. Pancreas senses glucose spike after a meal, releases insulin, cells absorb glucose, blood sugar drops. That self-correcting cycle is the feedback mechanism examiners love.

## Plant Hormones

**Meera:** Plants have no brain or nerves — but they still bend towards light. That is auxin, right?

**Arjun:** Yes. When light hits the stem from one side, auxin redistributes to the shaded side. Auxin makes cells elongate. Shaded side grows faster, lit side grows slower. The stem bends towards light. Phototropism. NCERT explains this step by step — copy that explanation almost word-for-word in your answer.

**Meera:** So the plant is not "choosing" — it is just chemistry.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Five plant hormones to know — auxin (cell elongation, phototropism), gibberellins (stem growth, flowering), cytokinins (cell division, found in fruits and seeds), abscisic acid (slows growth, promotes leaf fall in stress), ethylene (gas that ripens fruit).

**Meera:** Ethylene — nani wraps raw mangoes in newspaper with a ripe banana to ripen them faster.

**Arjun:** Perfect example for the answer. The ripe banana releases ethylene, the gas speeds up mango ripening. Examples like this lift a 3-marker into full-marks territory.

**Meera:** And chhui-mui — the touch-me-not — that fold is not auxin, right? It is something else.

**Arjun:** Different mechanism. Touching the leaf causes cells at the base of the leaflets to rapidly lose water — turgor pressure drops, the leaf droops. After a while, water refills and the leaf opens. It is a nastic movement, not a tropism, not a hormone-driven growth response. Mark this distinction — examiners catch students who confuse the two.

## Exam Preparation Tips

**Meera:** Arjun, what should top my last-week revision list?

**Arjun:** Five must-do items. One — reflex arc diagram, neatly labelled, with the five-component pathway. Two — nervous vs hormonal control comparison table. Three — functions of cerebrum, cerebellum, medulla side by side. Four — phototropism explained using auxin redistribution. Five — feedback mechanism using insulin and blood sugar.

**Meera:** Should I memorise all five plant hormones?

**Arjun:** Yes — name, gland or source, function. Two-mark direct questions love that. And honestly, this chapter is one of the friendliest in the Biology section for scoring full marks. Get the diagrams right and you are home.

**Meera:** Yaar, the diagrams scare me. I keep over-labelling.

**Arjun:** Use a pencil, draw clean, use a ruler for arrows. Label every part. NCERT-style diagrams. Examiners reward neatness and completeness. Believe me — a beautifully labelled reflex arc is the easiest 5 marks in the whole paper.
