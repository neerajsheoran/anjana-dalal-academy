## Ecosystems and Food Chains

**Meera:** Arjun, NCERT defines an ecosystem as biotic and abiotic components interacting in a region. The mango tree in my building's compound plus the koel, the squirrels, the ants, the soil microbes, sunlight, water — all of it together is one ecosystem.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Biotic equals living, abiotic equals non-living. And the organisms are classified by how they feed — producers, consumers, decomposers. That's the standard 3-marker.

**Meera:** Producers are autotrophs — green plants and some bacteria that photosynthesise. Consumers are heterotrophs — herbivores, carnivores, omnivores, parasites. Decomposers are fungi and bacteria breaking down dead matter.

**Arjun:** Right. And the food chain shows the path of energy through trophic levels. Grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → eagle. Each link is one trophic level.

**Meera:** And the 10 percent rule — only 10 percent of energy passes from one trophic level to the next. The other 90 percent is lost in respiration, movement, heat.

**Arjun:** That's the single most-tested concept in this chapter. NCERT specifically asks why food chains are limited to 3 or 4 levels — answer is the 10 percent rule. After four transfers, almost no energy remains to support another consumer.

**Meera:** Western Ghats food chains have around four trophic levels at most. The lion-tailed macaque at the top, fruits and insects below.

**Arjun:** Good example. Examiners love when you tie the 10 percent rule to a real ecosystem. One line is enough — don't over-explain.

## Biological Magnification

**Meera:** Arjun, the chapter on biomagnification feels personal. We eat fish from rivers like the Ganga which has so much industrial waste.

**Arjun:** It's the most relevant topic in this chapter. Non-biodegradable chemicals — DDT, mercury, certain pesticides — accumulate at each trophic level because organisms can't break them down or excrete them.

**Meera:** So if a farmer sprays pesticides on cotton, rain washes it into a river, plankton absorb tiny amounts, small fish eat thousands of plankton, large fish eat hundreds of small fish, and a fish-eating bird at the top ends up with concentration thousands of times higher than the water.

**Arjun:** That's the textbook chain. NCERT specifically asks about humans being at the top of many food chains, which is why we accumulate the highest concentration of pesticides.

**Meera:** ISRO sensors actually monitor pesticide-affected water bodies across India. And Sikkim was declared a fully organic state.

**Arjun:** Good examples for application questions. The 3-marker pattern is — define biomagnification, explain the mechanism with a four-link food chain, mention humans at the top. Each part one mark.

**Meera:** Can washing remove pesticides from vegetables?

**Arjun:** Surface residues, yes. But chemicals absorbed into the plant tissue can't be washed off. That's why organic farming and proper food safety regulation matter. Bring this in if a question asks about reducing biomagnification — it's a free mark.

## Ozone Layer Depletion

**Meera:** Arjun, the ozone layer chapter always feels distant — like it's happening to Antarctica, not to us.

**Arjun:** But NCAOR — the National Centre for Polar and Ocean Research in Goa — actually sends Indian scientists to Antarctica every year to monitor the ozone hole. The thinning is global, even if the worst spot is over Antarctica.

**Meera:** OK so ozone is O3, three oxygen atoms. It sits in the stratosphere and absorbs UV radiation that would otherwise cause skin cancer, cataracts, weakened immunity, and crop damage.

**Arjun:** Right. And formation — high-energy UV splits O2 into O atoms, free O atoms combine with O2 to make O3. The process happens naturally and continuously.

**Meera:** And CFCs — chlorofluorocarbons from old refrigerators, ACs, and spray cans — drift up to the stratosphere. UV breaks them apart, releases chlorine atoms. Each chlorine atom destroys thousands of ozone molecules.

**Arjun:** That's the chain reaction. The 1980s detected a dramatic thinning over Antarctica — the ozone hole. In 1987 the Montreal Protocol was signed, phasing out CFC production. India participated actively and the ozone layer is slowly recovering.

**Meera:** It's the textbook example of international cooperation that actually worked.

**Arjun:** And examiners use that exact phrase — "successful international agreement on environmental protection." 3 to 5-marker question. Define ozone, explain its protective role, describe CFC depletion mechanism, mention Montreal Protocol. Four points, four marks.

**Meera:** Modern refrigerators don't use CFCs anymore. The labels say HFC or HCFC.

**Arjun:** Right. Some of those still contribute to global warming, so scientists are still working on better refrigerants. Don't mix up ozone depletion with global warming in answers — they're two different problems with overlapping chemistry.

## Waste Management

**Meera:** Arjun, our building started two-bin segregation last month. Green bin for wet waste, blue bin for dry. But half the families still mix everything.

**Arjun:** Same building-level problem everywhere in India. Swachh Bharat tried to push this nationally and segregation rates are slowly improving. The exam asks why this distinction matters at all.

**Meera:** Because bacteria and fungi can decompose natural substances like cellulose, starch, proteins — vegetable peels, paper, cotton cloth all break down. But synthetic substances like plastic have molecular structures no natural enzyme can break.

**Arjun:** Right. The reason is evolutionary — natural decomposers evolved over millions of years to recognise natural molecules. Plastic was invented barely a hundred years ago. No enzyme exists for it.

**Meera:** That's why plastic waste in the Ganga lasts for centuries. We see videos of plastic in the river near Varanasi and Kanpur all the time.

**Arjun:** And biodegradable cutlery from sugarcane bagasse or cornstarch is one alternative. Some Indian startups are commercialising it now. Examiners are starting to ask about sustainable alternatives — it's a 2 or 3-marker question.

**Meera:** And the 3 R's — reduce, reuse, recycle.

**Arjun:** Reduce means consume less in the first place. Carry a steel water bottle instead of buying plastic. Reuse means find a second life — glass jars from pickle become spice storage. Recycle means proper processing of paper, metal, certain plastics into new products.

**Meera:** Even biodegradable waste causes problems if too much piles up — rotting, smell, methane, water pollution.

**Arjun:** Yes. That's the subtle point examiners ask in 5-markers — "biodegradable waste is harmless." False. In large quantities and improperly managed, it still produces greenhouse gases and water pollution.

## Exam Preparation Tips

**Meera:** Arjun, the chapter looks short but the question patterns are wide. What carries the most weight?

**Arjun:** Four areas. First, the 10 percent rule and energy flow through trophic levels — almost always a 3 or 5-marker. Draw a food chain, label the trophic levels, explain why only 10 percent transfers and why food chains stop at 3 or 4 levels.

**Meera:** Second?

**Arjun:** Biological magnification. Define it, draw the food chain, explain the mechanism, mention humans at the top. 3-marker pattern. Examiners sometimes give you a numerical of increasing concentrations — practise that.

**Meera:** Third?

**Arjun:** Ozone depletion. How ozone forms, how CFCs destroy it, Montreal Protocol. 3 to 5-marker. The CFC chain reaction with chlorine atoms attacking thousands of ozone molecules is the central mechanism.

**Meera:** And fourth?

**Arjun:** Biodegradable versus non-biodegradable waste. Four examples each, why decomposers can't break down synthetic materials, environmental impact, the 3 R's. 3-marker easy. Common diagrams to practise — labelled food chain with trophic levels, food web with multiple links, biological magnification with rising pesticide concentrations.

**Meera:** Total weightage from this chapter?

**Arjun:** 5 to 7 marks in the board paper. Less than the physics chapters but every mark here is recall-based — no numericals, just clean NCERT phrasing. Easiest marks in the entire paper if you've revised well.
