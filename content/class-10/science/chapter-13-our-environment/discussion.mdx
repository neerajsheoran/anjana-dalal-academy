## Ecosystems and Food Chains

**Child:** Maa, what exactly is an ecosystem? Is our garden an ecosystem?

**Mother:** Yes, beta! Our garden is a small ecosystem. The tulsi plant, the mango tree, the earthworms in the soil, the sparrows picking seeds, the butterfly sipping nectar, and even the fungi on the damp wall -- all of them interact with each other and with non-living things like sunlight, water, soil, and air. That web of interactions is an ecosystem.

**Child:** And the food chain shows who eats whom?

**Mother:** Exactly. A food chain is like a simple line: grass is eaten by the grasshopper, the grasshopper is eaten by the frog, the frog is eaten by the snake, and the snake is eaten by the eagle. Each step is called a trophic level. But in reality, the grasshopper also eats leaves of different plants, and the frog might eat other insects too. When you connect all these possibilities, you get a food web -- a tangled network of feeding relationships.

**Child:** Maa, you always say eating sabzi directly is better than eating the goat that ate the sabzi. Is that related to energy flow?

**Mother:** Very clever connection! This is the 10 percent rule. At each trophic level, only about 10 percent of the energy is passed on to the next. The rest is used up in breathing, moving, and keeping the body warm, or lost as heat. So when you eat dal and roti directly from plants, you are getting energy at the second trophic level. If you eat meat, you are at the third level -- 90 percent of the energy was already lost before it reached you. That is why producing one kilogram of chicken requires roughly 10 kilograms of grain.

**Child:** Is that why food chains are short -- only 3 or 4 levels?

**Mother:** Precisely. After 3 or 4 transfers, so little energy remains that it cannot support another level of consumers. It is like pouring water through a series of leaky buckets -- by the fourth bucket, almost nothing is left.

## Biological Magnification

**Child:** Maa, I read that farmers spray pesticides on crops to kill insects. But then do those chemicals end up in our food?

**Mother:** Unfortunately, yes -- and it gets worse the higher you go in the food chain. This is called biological magnification. Suppose a farmer near a lake sprays a pesticide. Rain washes it into the water. Tiny water plants absorb a small amount. Small fish eat lakhs of these plants and accumulate all that chemical. Bigger fish eat many small fish and concentrate it even further. A heron at the top of this chain ends up with an enormously high concentration -- sometimes lakhs of times more than what was originally in the water.

**Child:** That sounds scary. Can we wash it off from vegetables?

**Mother:** Washing helps remove surface residues, but chemicals that the plant has absorbed into its tissues cannot be washed away. That is one reason organic farming -- growing crops without synthetic pesticides -- is gaining popularity in India. States like Sikkim have been declared fully organic.

**Child:** Was DDT banned because of this?

**Mother:** DDT was banned for agricultural use in many countries after scientists found it was causing bird eggshells to become so thin that they cracked before hatching. In India, limited use of DDT is still allowed for malaria control in some regions, because malaria kills thousands of people. It is a difficult balancing act between public health and environmental protection.

## Ozone Layer Depletion

**Child:** Maa, what is the ozone layer and why does everyone worry about it?

**Mother:** High up in the atmosphere, there is a layer of ozone -- O3, a molecule with three oxygen atoms. It acts like an invisible shield, absorbing harmful ultraviolet radiation from the Sun. Without this shield, UV rays would reach the Earth's surface in dangerous amounts, causing skin cancer, eye damage, weakened immunity, and harm to crops and marine life.

**Child:** And CFCs from old fridges destroy this shield?

**Mother:** Yes. Chlorofluorocarbons were once used widely in refrigerators, air conditioners, and spray cans. When released, they slowly drift up into the stratosphere. There, UV radiation breaks them apart and releases chlorine atoms. Each chlorine atom can destroy thousands of ozone molecules in a chain reaction. By the 1980s, scientists detected a dramatic thinning of the ozone layer over Antarctica -- the so-called ozone hole.

**Child:** What did the world do about it?

**Mother:** In 1987, countries came together and signed the Montreal Protocol, agreeing to phase out CFC production. India was an active participant and has been recognised for its efforts. Manufacturers switched to CFC-free refrigerants, and the ozone layer has been slowly healing since then. It is one of the most successful examples of international cooperation for the environment.

**Child:** So our new fridge at home does not use CFCs?

**Mother:** Correct! Modern refrigerators use alternatives that do not harm the ozone layer. Though some of these alternatives contribute to global warming, scientists and engineers are continually working on even better substitutes.

## Waste Management

**Child:** Maa, why does the garbage truck come twice -- once for wet waste and once for dry waste?

**Mother:** Because biodegradable waste and non-biodegradable waste need completely different treatment. Wet waste -- vegetable peels, leftover dal, tea leaves, garden clippings -- is biodegradable. Bacteria and fungi can break it down into compost, which enriches the soil. Dry waste -- plastic bottles, polythene covers, old batteries, glass -- is non-biodegradable. These materials cannot be decomposed by any natural organism.

**Child:** Why can bacteria break down a banana peel but not a plastic bag?

**Mother:** Because enzymes in bacteria are extremely specific. Over millions of years, nature evolved enzymes that can break down natural substances like cellulose, starch, and proteins. But plastic was invented barely a hundred years ago. No organism has had time to evolve an enzyme for it. So plastic simply sits in the environment -- in landfills, rivers, oceans -- for hundreds of years.

**Child:** What about the 3 Rs -- reduce, reuse, recycle?

**Mother:** They are simple but powerful. Reduce means use less in the first place -- carry your own steel bottle instead of buying a plastic one. Reuse means find a second life for things -- glass jars from pickle can store spices. Recycle means send materials like paper, metal, and certain plastics to facilities that process them into new products. Even composting your kitchen waste at home is a form of recycling -- you turn food scraps into rich manure for your tulsi and tomato plants.

**Child:** But even biodegradable waste can cause problems if there is too much of it?

**Mother:** Absolutely. If a huge amount of food waste is dumped in one place, decomposers cannot keep up. The pile rots, produces a terrible smell, attracts flies and mosquitoes, and releases gases like methane. If dumped into a river, decomposing waste uses up the oxygen in the water, suffocating fish and other aquatic life. So even biodegradable waste needs to be managed properly.

## Exam Preparation Tips

**Child:** Maa, this chapter seems short but there is a lot to remember. What should I focus on?

**Mother:** Four key areas. First, the 10 percent rule and energy flow through trophic levels -- draw a food chain, label the trophic levels, and show the energy at each step. Second, biological magnification -- know the mechanism and be able to explain it with a numerical example of increasing concentrations. Third, ozone depletion -- understand how ozone forms, how CFCs destroy it, and what the Montreal Protocol achieved. Fourth, the difference between biodegradable and non-biodegradable waste with 3 or 4 examples of each and their environmental impact.

**Child:** Any diagrams I should practise?

**Mother:** A food chain with trophic levels clearly labelled, and a food web showing interconnected chains. Also practise a simple diagram of biological magnification showing rising pesticide concentrations from water to top predator. These diagrams can earn you full marks in the board exam.

**Child:** This chapter connects to real life a lot, does it not, Maa?

**Mother:** More than any other chapter, beta. Every time you segregate waste at home, carry a cloth bag to the shop, or choose to eat a plant-based meal, you are applying the science from this chapter. Understanding ecosystems, pollution, and waste management is not just for exams -- it shapes how we treat the only planet we have.
