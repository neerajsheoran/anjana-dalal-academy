## Introduction to Chemical Reactions

Think about what happens in your kitchen every morning. When your mother heats oil in a kadhai and drops mustard seeds into it, they crackle and pop — that is a chemical reaction. When you slice an apple and leave it on the table, it turns brown within minutes. When your grandmother prepares pickles with raw mangoes, lemon juice, and spices, the mixture slowly ferments over weeks, developing a tangy flavour. All these everyday happenings involve **chemical changes** — situations where the original substances transform into entirely new substances with different properties.

How can we tell that a chemical reaction has actually occurred? Look for these telltale signs:

- **Colour change** (a sliced apple turning brown, or a green copper roof turning bluish-green)
- **Gas formation** (bubbles appearing when you drop an Alka-Seltzer tablet into water)
- **Temperature change** (a heating pad becoming warm when activated)
- **Formation of a solid** in a solution (a cloudy substance appearing when two clear liquids are mixed)

Consider this: when you light a matchstick, the red phosphorus on the matchbox surface reacts with potassium chlorate on the matchstick head. The friction provides energy, and a flame appears. The wooden stick chars, producing ash and smoke — entirely different substances from what you started with.

> **Key idea:** A chemical reaction transforms one or more starting substances (called reactants) into entirely new substances (called products). We detect these transformations through observable clues like colour shifts, gas bubbles, temperature variations, or changes in physical state.

## Chemical Equations

A **chemical equation** is a shorthand way of describing what happens during a chemical reaction, much like a recipe tells you what ingredients go in and what dish comes out.

### Word Equations

The simplest format uses plain words:

**Zinc + Sulphuric acid → Zinc sulphate + Hydrogen gas**

Everything on the left side of the arrow represents the **reactants** — the substances you begin with. Everything on the right side represents the **products** — the new substances formed. The arrow means "reacts to produce."

### Using Chemical Formulae

We can write the same reaction more compactly using chemical symbols and formulae:

**Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂**

This version is called a **skeletal equation** — it shows the correct formulae but may not yet be balanced.

> **Key idea:** Chemical equations use formulae and symbols as a compact language to represent reactions, with reactants placed before the arrow and products placed after it.

## Balanced Chemical Equations

Imagine you are making rotis. If you start with 500 grams of atta (wheat flour), you cannot end up with 700 grams of rotis — the mass of what you produce cannot exceed the mass of what you started with. This common-sense principle is formalised in chemistry as the **law of conservation of mass**: matter is neither created nor destroyed during a chemical reaction. Every atom present at the start must be accounted for at the end.

A **balanced chemical equation** has equal numbers of each type of atom on both sides.

### How to Balance (Hit-and-Trial Method)

1. **Write the unbalanced (skeletal) equation** with correct formulae.
2. **Count atoms** of each element on both sides.
3. **Begin balancing** with the element that appears in the fewest formulae, adjusting **coefficients** (numbers placed before formulae).
4. **Never change subscripts** within a formula — only adjust the coefficient in front.
5. **Verify** that every element is balanced.
6. **Include state symbols**: (s) for solid, (l) for liquid, (g) for gas, (aq) for aqueous (dissolved in water).

**Worked example:** Balancing the reaction of aluminium with hydrochloric acid:

- Skeletal equation: Al + HCl → AlCl₃ + H₂
- Balanced equation: 2Al(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2AlCl₃(aq) + 3H₂(g)

Sometimes reaction conditions — such as heat (Δ), a catalyst name, or "sunlight" — are written above or below the arrow.

> **Key idea:** Balancing a chemical equation ensures that the law of conservation of mass is satisfied. Adjust only the coefficients, never the chemical formulae. Always include state symbols in your final answer.

## Types of Chemical Reactions

### Combination Reaction

When two or more substances unite to form a single product, we call it a **combination reaction**.

- **Quick lime reacting with water:**
  CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + Heat

  Construction workers in India use this reaction when preparing whitewash for walls. The reaction releases so much heat that if you touch the container, it feels scorching hot. Such heat-releasing reactions are called **exothermic reactions**.

- **Burning of charcoal:** C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)

- **Formation of ammonia:** N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g)

The whitewash on walls gradually hardens and develops a shine because calcium hydroxide slowly absorbs carbon dioxide from the air, forming calcium carbonate: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O.

> **Key idea:** In combination reactions, multiple reactants merge to form one product. Many such reactions release heat (exothermic).

---

### Decomposition Reaction

A **decomposition reaction** is the reverse of combination — a single compound splits into two or more simpler substances. Energy must be supplied to break apart the compound.

**Thermal decomposition** (heat-driven):
- 2FeSO₄(s) →(heat) Fe₂O₃(s) + SO₂(g) + SO₃(g)
  When you heat green ferrous sulphate crystals in a test tube, the crystals change colour to reddish-brown and pungent gases escape.

- CaCO₃(s) →(heat) CaO(s) + CO₂(g)
  This is the chemistry behind lime kilns found across Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu, where limestone is heated to produce quicklime for construction.

- 2Pb(NO₃)₂(s) →(heat) 2PbO(s) + 4NO₂(g) + O₂(g)
  Brown fumes of nitrogen dioxide appear when lead nitrate is heated.

**Electrolytic decomposition** (electricity-driven):
- 2H₂O(l) →(electricity) 2H₂(g) + O₂(g)
  Passing electric current through acidified water produces hydrogen and oxygen in a 2:1 volume ratio.

**Photolytic decomposition** (light-driven):
- 2AgBr(s) →(sunlight) 2Ag(s) + Br₂(g)
  Silver bromide decomposes when exposed to light — the basis of traditional film photography.

Since decomposition reactions need energy input, they are classified as **endothermic reactions**.

> **Key idea:** Decomposition reactions break a compound into simpler substances using energy in the form of heat, electricity, or light. They are endothermic in nature.

---

### Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions

**Exothermic reactions** give off energy (typically as heat):
- Burning of LPG in your kitchen stove: C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(g) + heat
- Cellular respiration: C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(aq) → 6CO₂(aq) + 6H₂O(l) + energy
- Composting of garden waste in a compost pit (the pile feels warm inside)

**Endothermic reactions** absorb energy from surroundings:
- Heating limestone in a kiln to make quicklime
- Breakdown of silver bromide by sunlight
- The cold feeling when ammonium nitrate dissolves in water (used in instant cold packs)

> **Key idea:** Exothermic reactions release energy to the surroundings, making things warmer. Endothermic reactions absorb energy, making things cooler. Respiration in living organisms is a vital exothermic process.

### Displacement Reaction

A **displacement reaction** occurs when a more chemically active element pushes out a less active element from its compound — like a stronger player taking the spot of a weaker one on a cricket team.

- **Zinc displacing copper:**
  Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
  When a shiny zinc strip is placed in a blue copper sulphate solution, the solution gradually loses its blue colour and turns colourless, while a reddish-brown layer of copper deposits on the zinc strip.

- **Iron displacing copper:**
  Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

- **Lead displacing copper:**
  Pb(s) + CuCl₂(aq) → PbCl₂(aq) + Cu(s)

These reactions work because zinc, iron, and lead are all more reactive than copper.

> **Key idea:** In displacement reactions, a more reactive element replaces a less reactive element from its compound in solution.

---

### Double Displacement Reaction

In a **double displacement reaction**, two compounds in solution swap their ionic partners, forming two new compounds.

**Na₂SO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)**

When you mix solutions of sodium sulphate and barium chloride, an insoluble white substance — barium sulphate — settles at the bottom of the container. This solid that separates from solution is called a **precipitate**, and the reaction is called a **precipitation reaction**.

Another example: mixing solutions of lead nitrate and potassium iodide produces a vivid yellow precipitate of lead iodide — a visually striking demonstration.

> **Key idea:** Double displacement reactions involve mutual exchange of ions between two dissolved compounds. When the reaction produces an insoluble product, it is specifically called a precipitation reaction.

## Oxidation and Reduction (Redox Reactions)

### Oxidation

**Oxidation** can be understood as:
- A substance **gaining oxygen**, OR
- A substance **losing hydrogen**

Example: When copper turnings are heated strongly in a china dish, the shiny brown copper gradually darkens as it combines with atmospheric oxygen:
2Cu(s) + O₂(g) → 2CuO(s)

### Reduction

**Reduction** is the opposite:
- A substance **losing oxygen**, OR
- A substance **gaining hydrogen**

Example: When hydrogen gas flows over heated copper oxide, the black powder transforms back into shiny copper:
CuO(s) + H₂(g) → Cu(s) + H₂O(g)

### Redox Reactions

In the reaction above, copper oxide **loses** oxygen (it is reduced) while hydrogen **gains** oxygen (it is oxidised). Since oxidation and reduction always happen together in the same reaction, we call such reactions **redox reactions** (reduction-oxidation reactions).

Further examples:
- ZnO(s) + C(s) → Zn(s) + CO(g) — Carbon is oxidised (gains O), zinc oxide is reduced (loses O)
- MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + 2H₂O + Cl₂ — HCl is oxidised, MnO₂ is reduced

> **Key idea:** Oxidation means gaining oxygen or losing hydrogen. Reduction means losing oxygen or gaining hydrogen. They always occur together — such paired reactions are called redox reactions.

## Effects of Oxidation in Everyday Life

### Corrosion

Walk past any old building in a coastal Indian city like Mumbai or Chennai and you will notice reddish-brown flakes on iron railings, greenish patches on copper fittings, and darkened silver ornaments in jewellery shops. This slow destruction of metals by environmental agents is called **corrosion**.

- **Iron** develops a flaky reddish-brown layer called **rust** (hydrated iron oxide) when exposed to moist air.
- **Copper** acquires a green coating (basic copper carbonate) over time.
- **Silver** tarnishes to a dull black (silver sulphide) from sulphur compounds in the air.

Corrosion costs India thousands of crores of rupees every year in damage to bridges, railway tracks, vehicles, and factory equipment. We fight corrosion by:
- Applying paint, oil, or grease
- **Galvanising** (coating iron with zinc)
- Chrome plating or anodising
- Making alloys (like stainless steel)

### Rancidity

Have you noticed that a packet of namkeen or bhujia smells odd if kept open for too long? The fats and oils in these snacks react with oxygen in the air, producing foul-smelling compounds. This spoilage is called **rancidity**.

Strategies to prevent rancidity:
- Adding **antioxidants** (like BHA or vitamin E) to packaged foods
- Storing food in **airtight containers**
- Filling snack packets with **nitrogen gas** instead of air (notice how puffy a packet of chips is!)
- Keeping oily foods **refrigerated**

> **Key idea:** Corrosion is the slow oxidation of metals by moisture and atmospheric gases. Rancidity is the oxidation of fats and oils in food. Both are everyday consequences of oxidation, and both can be prevented by limiting contact with oxygen.
