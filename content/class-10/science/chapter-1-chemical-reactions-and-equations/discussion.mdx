## Spotting Chemical Reactions Around Us

**Mother:** Beta, you know how when I make chhaunk for dal — heating oil and adding rai (mustard seeds) — the seeds pop and sputter? That crackling is a sign of a chemical reaction happening right in our kitchen.

**Child:** Maa, but how do I know something is a real chemical reaction and not just a physical change? Like when ice melts, that is just a physical change, right?

**Mother:** Exactly right. Physical changes can be reversed easily — ice melts to water, and water freezes back to ice. But look at what happens when I light a matchstick. The wood burns, producing ash and gas. You cannot turn ash back into a matchstick. That irreversibility is one clue. Other clues include a colour change, gas bubbles forming, the mixture getting hot or cold on its own, or a solid suddenly appearing in a clear liquid.

**Child:** Like when we put lemon juice on baking soda and it fizzes up? That fizzing is carbon dioxide gas, right?

**Mother:** Spot on! That fizz is CO₂ gas escaping. And you can feel the container getting slightly cool — that tells you the reaction is absorbing heat. You are already thinking like a scientist, beta.

## Writing and Balancing Chemical Equations

**Child:** Maa, our teacher says we have to "balance" equations. Why can we not just write the formulae and be done?

**Mother:** Think of it like this — if I am making 10 rotis and I start with 500 grams of atta, I should end up using exactly 500 grams of atta in those rotis, right? Nothing appears from thin air, and nothing vanishes. That is the law of conservation of mass — every atom you start with must be present in your products.

**Child:** So balancing is just making sure the atom count matches on both sides?

**Mother:** Yes! Use the hit-and-trial method. Write the skeletal equation first, count atoms of each element on both sides, then adjust the numbers in front of each formula — those are called coefficients. Never change the small numbers inside a formula. For instance, you cannot change H₂O to H₃O — that would be a completely different substance!

**Child:** What about the (s), (l), (g), and (aq) symbols? Do we really need those?

**Mother:** Absolutely. In your board exam, the marking scheme specifically gives marks for state symbols. The symbol (s) means solid, (l) means liquid, (g) means gas, and (aq) means dissolved in water. Writing them shows your examiner that you understand the physical states involved.

## Understanding Reaction Types

**Child:** Combination, decomposition, displacement, double displacement, redox — there are so many types, Maa. How do I tell them apart quickly?

**Mother:** Let me give you a simple framework, beta. Ask yourself these questions in order. First — how many reactants and products are there? If many substances join to make one, it is combination. If one substance breaks into many, it is decomposition. Second — if an element swaps places with another element in a compound, it is displacement. If two compounds swap partners, it is double displacement. Third — if oxygen or hydrogen is gained or lost, think redox.

**Child:** And the reactivity series tells us which metal can displace which, right?

**Mother:** Exactly. A more reactive metal kicks out a less reactive one. That is why when your Papa dipped an iron nail into copper sulphate solution for your school experiment, the blue solution became greenish and the nail got a brown copper coating. Iron is above copper in the reactivity series.

**Child:** Decomposition needs energy — heat, light, or electricity. Is that why they are endothermic?

**Mother:** Yes. Think of it this way — building something complex from simple parts releases energy, like when quick lime reacts with water and heats up your hand. But breaking something down requires you to put energy in. Heating limestone in those big kilns you see near Jodhpur — that is thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate. The sunlight breaking down silver compounds on old film rolls is photolytic decomposition. And splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen with electricity is electrolytic decomposition.

## Oxidation, Reduction, and Redox

**Child:** Maa, oxidation is gaining oxygen or losing hydrogen, and reduction is the opposite. But why do they always happen together?

**Mother:** Because the oxygen has to come from somewhere, beta. If one substance gains oxygen, another must be giving it up. In the reaction where hydrogen gas passes over hot copper oxide — CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O — the copper oxide loses its oxygen (it is reduced), and the hydrogen gains that oxygen (it is oxidised). They are two sides of the same coin, which is why we call the whole thing a redox reaction.

**Child:** And rusting and rancidity — those are everyday oxidation?

**Mother:** Yes. Look at the iron gate of our building — see those reddish-brown patches? That is rust, which is hydrated iron oxide. Iron slowly reacts with oxygen and moisture. And remember how that bottle of coconut oil Nani sent from Kerala started smelling funny after we left it open? That is rancidity — fats reacting with oxygen. That is exactly why chip packets are filled with nitrogen gas instead of air — nitrogen does not react with the fats.

## Preparing for Exams

**Child:** Maa, what kind of questions come from this chapter in the board exam?

**Mother:** This chapter typically carries 3 to 5 marks. The most common question type gives you a word equation and asks you to write the balanced chemical equation with state symbols. Another favourite is giving you an equation and asking you to identify the reaction type. Sometimes they combine both — "balance this equation AND identify the type."

**Child:** What mistakes should I watch out for?

**Mother:** Three big ones. First, getting the chemical formulae wrong — you must memorise valencies and common compound formulae. Second, forgetting state symbols — easy marks lost. Third, changing subscripts instead of coefficients while balancing. Remember, the subscript is part of the formula itself and must never be altered. Only the coefficient — the big number in front — changes during balancing.

## Chemistry in the Real World

**Child:** Maa, are these reactions actually used in real life, or is this only for exams?

**Mother:** Beta, every single reaction type is used in industry across India. When the workers at Tata Steel in Jamshedpur extract iron from its ore, they use displacement and reduction reactions. The cement factory near our town uses decomposition of limestone. The batteries in your Papa's phone run on redox reactions. Even the digestion of the paratha you ate for breakfast is a series of chemical reactions!

**Child:** So understanding reaction types helps predict what will happen when chemicals are mixed?

**Mother:** That is the real power of this chapter. Once you know the type, you can predict the products. A pharmaceutical scientist in Hyderabad designing new medicines uses exactly these principles. And for your future studies — whether NEET or JEE — recognising reaction types instantly is a skill that will serve you for years. Build it now and it will pay off enormously later.
