## Spotting Chemical Reactions Around Us

**Meera:** Arjun, my mother was making chhaunk for the dal yesterday — heating oil and dropping in mustard seeds. The seeds popped and the smell changed completely. Is that one chemical reaction or several happening together?

**Arjun:** Several, actually. The popping is moisture inside the seeds turning to steam, that part is physical. But the colour change and the new aroma — that is chemistry. NCERT lists colour change, gas evolution, temperature change, and precipitate formation as the four standard clues. The aroma falls under "new substance formed."

**Meera:** So in the board paper, if they ask "how do you know a chemical reaction has occurred," I should list those four signs?

**Arjun:** Yes, and ideally give one home example for each. Examiners like that. Mustard seeds in oil for gas evolution, lemon juice on baking soda for fizzing, an iron grill rusting in the Mumbai monsoon for colour change.

**Meera:** Last year a friend's paper got cut because she wrote "ice melting" as a chemical change.

**Arjun:** Classic trap. If you can reverse it without making something new, it is physical. The test is — does a new substance appear? Ash from a burnt matchstick can never become wood again. That is irreversible, so chemical.

## Writing and Balancing Chemical Equations

**Meera:** Arjun, in the prelim paper there was a balancing equation that took me ten minutes. Are we doing something wrong in our method?

**Arjun:** Most students balance in random order — they touch carbon, then oxygen, then hydrogen, then go back to carbon. The trick is to be systematic. NCERT actually walks through this in the worked example for Fe + H₂O.

**Meera:** What is your order?

**Arjun:** Metals first, then non-metals other than O and H, then oxygen, then hydrogen last. Hit-and-trial becomes much faster when you fix the sequence. And never touch the subscripts — only the coefficients in front.

**Meera:** Right. Last week I changed H₂O to H₃O while balancing — Sir crossed it out and wrote "different compound" in red ink.

**Arjun:** That mistake costs marks every year. Also, state symbols — (s), (l), (g), (aq) — the marking scheme gives a separate mark for them in the 3-mark equation question. People skip them and lose easy marks.

**Meera:** I noted that for revision. So balanced equation plus state symbols equals full credit.

## Understanding Reaction Types

**Meera:** Combination, decomposition, displacement, double displacement, redox. Five categories, and the board paper usually asks us to identify the type. How do you tell them apart fast?

**Arjun:** I made a small decision tree. First count reactants and products. Two going to one — combination. One going to two or more — decomposition. Then check if elements are swapping. One element kicking out another — displacement. Two compounds swapping partners — double displacement.

**Meera:** And redox is layered on top, right? It can happen in any of those.

**Arjun:** Exactly. If oxygen or hydrogen is gained or lost, it is redox in addition to whatever category it falls under. The 2023 board paper had a question where students had to label CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O as BOTH displacement and redox. Many missed the redox part.

**Meera:** The reactivity series tells us which displacement is possible. My uncle works in a steel rolling unit near Jamshedpur — he says the same logic decides which metal can extract which from its ore.

**Arjun:** Right. Tata Steel uses carbon to reduce iron ore because carbon sits below iron in extraction logic. But for aluminium they have to use electrolysis at Hindalco because carbon cannot reduce it.

**Meera:** So this chapter is not just exam stuff. It is literally how Indian metallurgy works.

## Oxidation, Reduction, and Redox

**Meera:** Arjun, I keep mixing up which substance is oxidised and which is reduced. Any trick?

**Arjun:** Tabulate it. For each reactant ask — did it gain O, lose O, gain H, or lose H? Then apply the definition. Gaining O or losing H is oxidation. Losing O or gaining H is reduction. NCERT example with CuO + H₂ — CuO loses oxygen so it is reduced, H₂ gains oxygen so it is oxidised.

**Meera:** And both happen at the same time, which is why it is called redox.

**Arjun:** Yes. You cannot have oxidation without reduction. The oxygen has to come from somewhere and go somewhere. They are mathematically linked.

**Meera:** What about real-life redox? My dadi keeps her Eno tin tightly sealed and says open packets of bhujia "go bad" within two days in our humid weather.

**Arjun:** That is rancidity — fats oxidising. And the iron grills on her balcony developing reddish-brown patches in the Mumbai monsoon — that is corrosion. Both are slow oxidation reactions happening at room temperature. Board paper definitely asks one of these two as a 2-mark question.

**Meera:** That is why chip packets are puffed with nitrogen — to keep oxygen out of contact with the oil?

**Arjun:** Exactly. And antioxidants like BHA and vitamin E are added to packaged foods for the same reason. PYQ from 2022 asked "why are chips packets filled with nitrogen?" — pure 2-mark application question.

## Preparing for Exams

**Meera:** Arjun, this chapter usually comes for how many marks?

**Arjun:** 3 to 5 marks typically. The most common question type is "balance this equation and identify the reaction type." Two marks for balancing, one for type, one for state symbols. Easy four marks if you are systematic.

**Meera:** And the common mistakes to avoid?

**Arjun:** Three big ones I have noted from sample papers. One — wrong chemical formulae because valencies are not memorised. Two — missing state symbols. Three — changing subscripts while balancing. Of these, valencies are pure memorisation. I made a flashcard set last weekend.

## Chemistry in the Real World

**Meera:** Arjun, sometimes I feel this is all just exam prep. Does anyone actually use these reactions outside school?

**Arjun:** Every major Indian industry uses them. Tata Chemicals at Mithapur runs decomposition reactions on a giant scale to make soda ash. Reliance refineries are built on combustion and substitution. The fertiliser plants at IFFCO use combination reactions for ammonia. Even the digestion of your dosa is a chain of redox reactions.

**Meera:** And for higher studies — NEET, JEE — does this chapter carry forward?

**Arjun:** Heavily. Reaction types are the foundation of physical chemistry in Class 11 and organic chemistry in Class 12. JEE asks redox at a much harder level — half-reactions, balancing in acidic and basic medium. If we get the Class 10 base right, the Class 11 jump becomes manageable.
