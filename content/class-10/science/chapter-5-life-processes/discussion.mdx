## Nutrition — Making and Getting Food

**Child:** Maa, the photosynthesis equation looks simple — CO₂ plus water plus sunlight gives glucose and oxygen. But our teacher says it is actually a complicated process?

**Mother:** It is, beta. What looks like one step is really two major stages happening inside the chloroplast. In the first stage — the light reaction — chlorophyll absorbs sunlight and uses that energy to split water molecules into hydrogen and oxygen. This also produces ATP and NADPH, which are like tiny energy packets. In the second stage — the dark reaction or Calvin cycle — these energy packets are used to convert CO₂ from the air into glucose. The light reaction happens in the thylakoid membranes, and the dark reaction happens in the stroma of the chloroplast.

**Child:** Stomata are the tiny pores that let CO₂ in and O₂ out. Do they stay open all the time?

**Mother:** No, and that is an important detail. Guard cells — the bean-shaped cells flanking each stoma — swell with water to open the pore and shrink to close it. During the day, most plants keep stomata open for gas exchange. At night, they close them to conserve water. Desert plants like cacti have a clever adaptation — they open stomata only at night when it is cool, absorb CO₂, and store it as an organic acid. During the day, they close the stomata to prevent water loss and use the stored CO₂ for photosynthesis. This is called CAM photosynthesis.

**Child:** For heterotrophs — saprophytic means digesting food outside the body, like fungi do?

**Mother:** Exactly. A mushroom growing on a decaying log secretes enzymes onto the dead wood, breaks it down externally, and absorbs the nutrients. Amoeba, on the other hand, engulfs food using pseudopodia — temporary arm-like extensions — and digests it inside a food vacuole. Paramoecium uses cilia to sweep food into its oral groove. Both Amoeba and Paramoecium diagrams are classic board exam questions, so practise drawing them neatly.

## The Human Digestive System

**Child:** Maa, what exactly happens to the paratha I ate for breakfast as it travels through my body?

**Mother:** Let us follow it step by step. In your mouth, your teeth crushed the paratha into small pieces while salivary amylase in your saliva started breaking down the starch in the atta into maltose sugar. Your tongue shaped everything into a soft ball and pushed it down your throat.

**Child:** Then it goes through the food pipe by peristalsis?

**Mother:** Yes, the oesophagus pushes it down through wave-like muscle contractions — even if you were standing on your head, peristalsis would still move food to your stomach. In the stomach, HCl creates a highly acidic environment that kills bacteria and activates pepsin, which starts breaking down the proteins from the ghee and dal you ate with the paratha. The stomach's own wall is protected from this acid by a layer of mucus.

**Child:** And in the small intestine — that is where bile and pancreatic juice come in?

**Mother:** Right. Bile from the liver does not contain enzymes, but it does something crucial — it breaks large fat globules into tiny droplets, like how dishwashing liquid breaks up oil in water. This is called emulsification, and it gives lipase enzymes a much larger surface area to work on. Pancreatic juice brings trypsin for proteins, lipase for fats, and amylase for any remaining starch. Intestinal juice completes the job, breaking everything into glucose, amino acids, and fatty acids — small enough to be absorbed.

**Child:** The villi absorb these nutrients?

**Mother:** Yes. The inner wall of the small intestine is covered with millions of tiny finger-like villi. Each villus has blood capillaries and a lymph vessel. Nutrients pass through the thin villus wall into the bloodstream. The small intestine is about 6 metres long in humans, and the combined surface area of all its villi is comparable to a tennis court — an extraordinary design for maximum absorption.

## Respiration — Breathing vs Energy Release

**Child:** Maa, our teacher told us that respiration and breathing are not the same thing. I always thought they were!

**Mother:** Many students confuse them, beta. Breathing is the physical act — inhaling air into your lungs and exhaling it out. Respiration is the chemical process happening inside your cells — glucose is broken down to release energy stored as ATP. Breathing provides the oxygen that cells need for aerobic respiration, but the actual energy release happens in the mitochondria, not in the lungs.

**Child:** Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and gives 38 ATP. Anaerobic gives only 2 ATP. Why such a huge difference?

**Mother:** In aerobic respiration, glucose is completely broken down — every bit of energy is extracted. In anaerobic respiration, glucose is only partially broken down. In yeast, the leftover energy stays locked in ethanol. In your muscles, it stays in lactic acid. That is why anaerobic respiration yields so much less energy.

**Child:** The lactic acid in muscles — is that why my legs burn after running?

**Mother:** Precisely. When you sprint, your muscles need energy faster than your blood can deliver oxygen. So they switch temporarily to anaerobic mode, producing lactic acid. That burning sensation is the lactic acid accumulating. After you stop and catch your breath, extra oxygen arrives to break down the lactic acid — this is called repaying the "oxygen debt." Athletes train specifically to push this threshold higher so they can run longer before their muscles start cramping.

**Child:** And respiration is basically the reverse of photosynthesis?

**Mother:** In terms of the overall equation, yes — photosynthesis stores energy in glucose, and respiration releases it. Together, they form a beautiful cycle that sustains nearly all life on Earth.

## Transportation and Excretion

**Child:** Maa, the heart has four chambers and pumps blood twice around the body in each cycle — that is double circulation?

**Mother:** Yes. The right side handles deoxygenated blood — the right ventricle sends it to the lungs, where CO₂ is released and O₂ is picked up. This oxygenated blood returns to the left atrium. Then the left ventricle, which has the thickest wall because it needs the most force, pumps this oxygen-rich blood to every part of the body. After delivering oxygen and collecting CO₂ and wastes, the now-deoxygenated blood returns to the right atrium. Two complete circuits — pulmonary and systemic — in one heartbeat cycle.

**Child:** Why is separating oxygenated and deoxygenated blood so important?

**Mother:** Because we are warm-blooded mammals, beta. Maintaining a constant body temperature of about 37°C requires a lot of energy, which means our cells need a continuous, undiluted supply of oxygen. If oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mixed — as happens partially in frogs and lizards with three-chambered hearts — less oxygen would reach the tissues, and energy production would drop. Our four-chambered heart keeps the two streams completely separate.

**Child:** In plants, xylem carries water up and phloem carries food — but they work differently?

**Mother:** Very differently. Xylem is like a one-way highway going upward. It uses physical forces — root pressure pushes from below, and transpiration pull sucks from above. The xylem vessels are made of dead cells, and the whole process is passive — the plant does not spend energy on it. Phloem, on the other hand, is like a two-way delivery service. It uses living sieve tube cells with companion cells, and it requires ATP energy to load sucrose into the tubes. Once loaded, osmotic pressure moves the food to wherever the plant needs it — growing tips, ripening fruits, or storage roots.

**Child:** And for excretion — nephrons filter the blood?

**Mother:** Yes, each kidney has about a million nephrons. Blood enters the glomerulus under high pressure, and small molecules — water, glucose, salts, urea — get squeezed out into Bowman's capsule. As this filtrate travels through the twisted tubule, all the useful stuff — glucose, amino acids, most water, and needed salts — gets reabsorbed back into the blood. What remains — urea, excess water, extra salts — becomes urine. The beauty of this system is its selectivity. And when kidneys fail, dialysis machines mimic this filtration externally to keep patients alive.

## Exam Preparation

**Child:** This chapter is so long, Maa. How should I study for it?

**Mother:** Focus on four areas. First, diagrams — the human digestive system, the heart with its four chambers and blood flow arrows, the nephron with its parts labelled, and a leaf cross-section showing stomata. These labelling questions appear almost every year. Second, learn the specific role of each enzyme and organ. Third, know the key differences: aerobic vs anaerobic respiration, arteries vs veins, xylem vs phloem. Fourth, practise 3-mark and 5-mark answers that combine process descriptions with diagrams.

**Child:** And for NEET?

**Mother:** Every topic in this chapter expands enormously in Classes 11 and 12. The digestive enzymes you learn now become a full unit on digestive physiology. The heart and circulation become the cardiovascular system with the cardiac cycle, ECG, and blood pressure regulation. Nephron function becomes renal physiology with counter-current mechanisms. Building a rock-solid foundation now means you will learn advanced biology much faster later.
