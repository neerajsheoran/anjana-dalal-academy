## What Are Life Processes?

Every living organism — from the tiniest bacterium in pond water to the largest banyan tree in a village square — must constantly maintain and repair itself to stay alive. The collection of functions that accomplish this ongoing maintenance is called **life processes**. Four fundamental life processes are:

1. **Nutrition** — acquiring and processing food for energy and raw materials
2. **Respiration** — breaking down food molecules to release usable energy
3. **Transportation** — moving substances (nutrients, gases, wastes) within the body
4. **Excretion** — removing harmful waste products generated by metabolism

These processes never stop — they continue even when you are fast asleep. At the molecular level, the constant movement and rearrangement of molecules is what distinguishes living things from non-living matter.

> **Key idea:** Life processes — nutrition, respiration, transportation, and excretion — are the essential maintenance activities that keep every organism alive by supplying energy, delivering materials, and clearing wastes.

## Nutrition

**Nutrition** is the process of obtaining food from the environment and converting it into energy and building blocks for growth and repair.

### Autotrophic Nutrition

**Autotrophs** — primarily green plants, algae, and some bacteria — manufacture their own food from simple inorganic ingredients using an external energy source. The dominant process is **photosynthesis**:

**6CO₂ + 6H₂O →(sunlight + chlorophyll)→ C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂**

Photosynthesis unfolds in three key steps:
1. **Light absorption:** Chlorophyll molecules in the **chloroplasts** of leaf cells capture sunlight energy.
2. **Water splitting:** This captured energy splits water molecules into hydrogen and oxygen. Oxygen exits through the stomata as a byproduct (this is the light-dependent reaction).
3. **Carbon fixation:** Carbon dioxide from the atmosphere is combined with hydrogen (using the energy stored in ATP and NADPH) to build glucose molecules (the light-independent or dark reaction).

**Stomata** — microscopic pores on leaf surfaces — serve as the entry point for CO₂ and the exit for O₂. Each stoma is flanked by a pair of **guard cells** that swell or shrink to open or close the pore, regulating gas exchange and water loss. Desert plants like cacti have evolved to open stomata at night (when it is cooler) to absorb CO₂, then use it for photosynthesis during the day — minimising water loss.

Glucose produced during photosynthesis is stored as **starch** for later use. Plants also absorb nitrogen (as nitrates), phosphorus, iron, and magnesium from the soil for synthesising proteins, DNA, and chlorophyll.

### Heterotrophic Nutrition

**Heterotrophs** cannot make their own food and must obtain it from other organisms. Three modes exist:

- **Saprophytic:** The organism secretes digestive enzymes onto dead organic matter, breaks it down externally, and absorbs the nutrients. Examples: fungi like bread mould, yeast, and mushrooms.
- **Parasitic:** The organism lives on or inside a host, drawing nutrition without killing it. Examples: cuscuta (amarbel — the yellow vine that wraps around other plants), ticks, lice, and tapeworms.
- **Holozoic:** The organism ingests whole food, digests it internally, absorbs the nutrients, and expels the waste. Examples: Amoeba, humans, cows.

**Amoeba** feeds by extending temporary finger-like projections called **pseudopodia** ("false feet") to surround and engulf food particles, trapping them in a **food vacuole** where digestion occurs.

**Paramoecium** is covered with tiny hair-like **cilia** that beat in coordinated waves to sweep food particles towards a fixed groove for ingestion.

> **Key idea:** Autotrophs produce food through photosynthesis using CO₂, water, sunlight, and chlorophyll. Heterotrophs obtain food from other organisms through saprophytic, parasitic, or holozoic means.

## Nutrition in Human Beings

The human digestive system is essentially a long tube — the **alimentary canal** — stretching from mouth to anus, with specialised organs along the way.

### Step-by-Step Digestion

1. **Mouth:** Teeth physically crush and grind food. Simultaneously, **salivary glands** release saliva containing the enzyme **salivary amylase**, which begins breaking down **starch** into simpler sugars (maltose). The tongue mixes everything into a soft ball called a bolus and pushes it towards the throat.

2. **Oesophagus (food pipe):** The bolus travels down through wave-like muscular contractions called **peristalsis** — alternating squeezing and relaxing of the tube walls, like squeezing toothpaste through a tube.

3. **Stomach:** A thick-walled muscular sac. Gastric glands in its inner lining secrete:
   - **Hydrochloric acid (HCl)** — creates a strongly acidic environment (pH ~1.5) to kill bacteria and activate enzymes
   - **Pepsin** — an enzyme that breaks proteins into smaller fragments
   - **Mucus** — coats the stomach wall to protect it from its own acid

4. **Small intestine:** The longest section (~6 metres), where the bulk of chemical digestion and absorption occurs. It receives:
   - **Bile** from the liver (stored in the gall bladder) — bile does not contain enzymes but **emulsifies fats** (breaks large fat droplets into tiny ones, increasing surface area for enzymes) and makes the medium alkaline
   - **Pancreatic juice** from the pancreas — contains **trypsin** (digests proteins), **lipase** (digests fats), and **amylase** (digests remaining starch)
   - **Intestinal juice** — completes the digestion of proteins, carbohydrates, and fats into their simplest absorbable forms

   The inner wall of the small intestine is lined with millions of tiny finger-shaped projections called **villi**. Each villus has a rich network of blood capillaries and a lymph vessel (lacteal). This enormous surface area enables rapid absorption of digested nutrients into the bloodstream.

5. **Large intestine:** Absorbs most of the remaining water and dissolved mineral salts from the undigested residue. The semi-solid waste (faeces) is stored temporarily.

6. **Anus:** Faeces are expelled from the body in a process called egestion.

> **Key idea:** Human digestion progressively simplifies complex food molecules through mechanical processes (chewing, peristalsis) and chemical processes (enzymes, bile, acid) into absorbable units. Villi in the small intestine provide a massive surface area for efficient nutrient absorption.

## Respiration

**Respiration** is the biochemical process of breaking down glucose to release energy, which is captured in molecules of **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate) — the universal energy currency of cells.

### How Glucose Is Broken Down

The first step occurs in the **cytoplasm**: glucose (a 6-carbon molecule) is split into two molecules of **pyruvate** (each with 3 carbons). This initial step does not require oxygen.

What happens next depends on whether oxygen is available:

**Aerobic respiration** (with oxygen — occurs in **mitochondria**):
Pyruvate → CO₂ + H₂O + large amount of energy (approximately 38 ATP molecules per glucose)

**Anaerobic respiration** (without oxygen — remains in cytoplasm):
- In **yeast**: Pyruvate → Ethanol + CO₂ + small amount of energy (2 ATP)
  This is **fermentation** — the process behind making bread, dosa batter, and alcoholic beverages.
- In **human muscle cells** (during intense exercise): Pyruvate → Lactic acid + small energy
  The accumulation of lactic acid is what causes the burning sensation and **cramps** in your legs after sprinting.

Aerobic respiration is far more efficient because glucose is completely dismantled, extracting maximum energy.

### The Human Respiratory System

Air enters through the **nostrils**, where tiny hairs and mucus filter out dust and warm the incoming air. It passes through the **pharynx**, **larynx**, and into the **trachea** (windpipe), which is kept open by C-shaped rings of cartilage. The trachea branches into two **bronchi**, one entering each lung. Inside the lungs, bronchi divide into increasingly smaller **bronchioles**, which terminate in clusters of tiny balloon-like sacs called **alveoli**.

**Alveoli** are the sites of gas exchange. Their walls are just one cell thick and are surrounded by a dense web of blood capillaries. Oxygen from inhaled air diffuses across the thin alveolar wall into the blood, while CO₂ diffuses from the blood into the alveoli to be exhaled. The combined surface area of all alveoli in both lungs is roughly 80 square metres — about the size of half a badminton court.

**Breathing mechanism:** When the **diaphragm** flattens and the rib muscles contract, the chest cavity expands, drawing air in (inhalation). When they relax, the cavity shrinks, pushing air out (exhalation).

**Haemoglobin**, the iron-containing pigment in red blood cells, picks up oxygen in the lungs and delivers it to body tissues. Low haemoglobin levels (anaemia) reduce the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity, causing tiredness and breathlessness.

> **Key idea:** Respiration converts glucose into ATP energy. Aerobic respiration (in mitochondria) yields far more ATP than anaerobic respiration. In the lungs, alveoli provide an enormous, thin-walled surface for efficient gas exchange between air and blood.

## Transportation in Human Beings

The human **circulatory system** — heart, blood, and blood vessels — is the body's internal delivery and collection network.

### Blood — The Transport Medium

**Blood** is a fluid connective tissue consisting of:
- **Plasma** — the straw-coloured liquid matrix carrying dissolved nutrients, CO₂, urea, hormones, and antibodies
- **Red blood cells (RBCs)** — packed with haemoglobin for oxygen transport
- **White blood cells (WBCs)** — the immune system's soldiers, fighting infections
- **Platelets** — tiny cell fragments that clump together at wound sites to form clots, preventing blood loss

### Blood Vessels

- **Arteries:** Carry blood **away** from the heart. They have thick, elastic walls to withstand the high pressure of each heartbeat. They do not have valves.
- **Veins:** Carry blood **towards** the heart. Their walls are thinner and they contain **valves** that prevent blood from flowing backward.
- **Capillaries:** Extremely thin-walled vessels (one cell thick) that connect arteries to veins. They are where actual exchange of substances between blood and body tissues takes place.

### The Heart and Double Circulation

The human heart is a muscular pump with **four chambers**: right atrium (RA), right ventricle (RV), left atrium (LA), and left ventricle (LV). One-way valves between chambers ensure blood flows in a single direction.

Blood completes **two circuits** in each full cycle — this is called **double circulation**:

1. **Pulmonary circuit:** RV pumps deoxygenated blood → Lungs (CO₂ released, O₂ absorbed) → Oxygenated blood returns to LA
2. **Systemic circuit:** LV pumps oxygenated blood → All body organs (O₂ delivered, CO₂ collected) → Deoxygenated blood returns to RA

Why is separation so important? **Mammals and birds** are warm-blooded — they maintain constant body temperature regardless of surroundings. This demands a high metabolic rate and therefore an efficient, uninterrupted supply of pure oxygenated blood. Mixing oxygenated and deoxygenated blood would dilute the oxygen content and reduce energy production.

**Blood pressure** is the force blood exerts on artery walls. A healthy reading is approximately 120/80 mm Hg (systolic/diastolic).

**Lymph** is a pale yellowish fluid resembling plasma that leaks out of capillaries into tissue spaces. It drains into lymphatic vessels and eventually returns to the blood. Lymph carries absorbed fats from the intestine and plays a role in immune defence.

> **Key idea:** Double circulation ensures that mammals receive only fully oxygenated blood for their high-energy lifestyles. The heart's four chambers and one-way valves maintain strict separation of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood.

## Transportation in Plants

Plants have a specialised **vascular system** with two types of conducting tissues: **xylem** and **phloem**.

### Xylem — Carrying Water and Minerals Upward

**Xylem** vessels and tracheids form continuous pipelines from roots to leaf tips.

Two forces drive water upward:
- **Root pressure:** Root cells actively pump mineral ions inward, creating a concentration gradient that pulls water in by **osmosis**. This generates an upward push, especially significant at night.
- **Transpiration pull:** Water evaporating from leaf surfaces through stomata creates a suction force at the top. Because water molecules stick to each other (cohesion), this suction pulls an entire continuous column of water upward through the xylem — like drinking through a straw. This is the dominant force during daytime.

**Transpiration** — the loss of water vapour from leaves — serves multiple purposes: driving water transport, absorbing minerals, and cooling the plant.

### Phloem — Distributing Food

**Phloem** tissue transports the products of photosynthesis — mainly sucrose — from leaves to all other plant parts. This movement is called **translocation**.

- Translocation occurs through **sieve tubes**, assisted by **companion cells**.
- Unlike xylem transport, phloem transport requires **energy** (ATP).
- Sucrose is actively loaded into sieve tubes, raising osmotic pressure, which draws water in and creates a pressure flow that pushes the food material along.
- Phloem transport is **bidirectional** — food moves upward to growing shoots and flowers, and downward to roots and storage organs, depending on the plant's needs.

> **Key idea:** Xylem moves water and minerals upward using root pressure and transpiration pull (a passive, physical process). Phloem moves food (sugars) bidirectionally through energy-dependent translocation.

## Excretion

**Excretion** is the process of identifying and removing harmful metabolic waste products from the body before they accumulate to toxic levels.

### Excretion in Humans

The human **excretory system** consists of:
- Two **kidneys** (bean-shaped organs in the abdomen)
- Two **ureters** (tubes connecting kidneys to the bladder)
- One **urinary bladder** (a storage sac)
- One **urethra** (the exit tube)

The kidney's functional unit is the **nephron**. Each kidney contains approximately one million nephrons. A nephron consists of:
- **Bowman's capsule** — a cup-shaped structure enclosing a knot of capillaries called the **glomerulus**
- A **tubular region** — where selective reabsorption takes place

**Urine formation involves three steps:**
1. **Filtration:** Blood entering the glomerulus is under high pressure. Small molecules (water, glucose, salts, urea) are forced through the capillary walls into Bowman's capsule. Large molecules (proteins) and blood cells remain in the blood.
2. **Selective reabsorption:** As the filtrate travels through the tubule, useful substances — glucose, amino acids, most salts, and a large fraction of water — are actively reabsorbed back into surrounding blood capillaries.
3. **Collection and excretion:** The remaining liquid (urine) — containing urea, excess salts, and surplus water — flows through collecting ducts into the ureters, then to the bladder, and is eventually expelled through the urethra.

The body adjusts how much water is reabsorbed depending on hydration levels and the amount of dissolved waste that needs to be flushed out.

**Haemodialysis (artificial kidney):** When kidneys fail, a dialysis machine filters the blood externally. Blood is passed through a system of semi-permeable tubes immersed in a dialysing fluid that has the same composition as normal blood plasma (minus the wastes). Urea and excess salts diffuse out of the blood into the fluid, while useful substances remain.

### Excretion in Plants

Plants handle waste differently from animals:
- **Gaseous wastes** — O₂ (from photosynthesis) and CO₂ (from respiration) — exit through **stomata**.
- **Excess water** is released by **transpiration**.
- Some waste products are stored safely in **cellular vacuoles** or in **leaves that eventually drop off** (this is one reason deciduous trees shed leaves).
- Certain wastes are deposited as **resins and gums** in old xylem tissue.
- Some waste compounds are released into the soil around the roots.

> **Key idea:** Kidneys purify blood through millions of nephrons using filtration and selective reabsorption. Plants lack dedicated excretory organs and instead use stomata, transpiration, leaf shedding, and vacuolar storage to manage waste.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Mumbai marathon training and the lactic acid burn.** Every January, thousands run the Tata Mumbai Marathon. First-time runners often hit a wall around the 5-km mark — legs cramping, breathing ragged. What they are feeling is exactly what NCERT calls anaerobic respiration kicking in — muscles needing energy faster than blood can deliver oxygen, so glucose breaks down only partially and lactic acid accumulates. Trained marathoners train specifically to raise this threshold. The chapter you are revising explains the science behind why a body that feels broken at kilometre 5 can run smoothly at kilometre 15 after months of training.

**Aravind Eye Hospital and the dialysis crisis in India.** India has one of the highest rates of kidney failure in the world, driven largely by uncontrolled diabetes and high blood pressure. When kidneys fail, patients need dialysis — a machine that mimics the nephron's filtration and selective reabsorption externally. Hospitals across India, from PGI Chandigarh to private dialysis centres in small towns, run lakhs of dialysis sessions every month. The bean-shaped organ you are drawing in your diagrams is at the centre of one of India's biggest public health stories.

**The kitchen-garden tomato that refused to grow.** Many Indian families try growing tomatoes in pots on the balcony or terrace. Sometimes the plant looks green and healthy but never bears fruit, or the leaves wilt mysteriously. Often the answer is in this chapter — xylem and phloem getting blocked by waterlogged soil, or stomata closing under heat stress to save water. Understanding transport in plants explains why the same plant thrives in one pot and dies in another.

### Try it at home — the 5-day pulse-and-breath log

*⏱ 10 minutes per day for 5 days · 🧹 zero mess · 👨‍👩‍👧 no adult help needed*

**What you'll need:**

- A watch or phone with a seconds display.
- A notebook with a simple table — Day, Resting Pulse, Resting Breaths/min, Post-Exercise Pulse, Post-Exercise Breaths/min, Recovery Time.
- A staircase, skipping rope, or open space for running on the spot.

**Steps:**

1. **Morning, before doing anything tiring.** Sit still for 2 minutes. Find your pulse at the wrist or neck. Count beats for 30 seconds, multiply by 2. Note resting pulse.
2. **Count your breaths for 30 seconds.** One inhale + exhale = one breath. Multiply by 2. Note resting breath rate.
3. **Exercise burst.** Run on the spot for 2 minutes, OR skip rope for 2 minutes, OR climb stairs continuously for 2 minutes. Push to the point where you are slightly breathless but not gasping.
4. **Immediately after stopping**, count pulse for 30 seconds and breaths for 30 seconds. Multiply both by 2. Note post-exercise values.
5. **Time how long it takes for your pulse to return to within 5 of resting.** This is your recovery time.
6. Repeat at the same time every day for 5 days. Try to do the exact same exercise each day.

**What to look for:**

- Does your resting pulse drop over the 5 days? Even slightly? Fitter people have lower resting pulse.
- Does your recovery time get shorter? This is the most sensitive sign of fitness improvement.
- Does your post-exercise breath rate spike higher on some days than others? Heat, sleep, hydration all affect it.

**Show what you found:** Show your table to your daadi (or dadu, mummy, or older cousin). Explain why your pulse went up after exercise — extra oxygen needed for aerobic respiration in your muscles. Ask if their pulse and breathing change differently. Watch them realise that the boring numbers in your notebook are the same physiology that keeps them alive every day.

**A question to keep asking all year:** *When my body does something automatic — sweating, shivering, breathing harder, feeling hungry — which life process is the body trying to balance?*
