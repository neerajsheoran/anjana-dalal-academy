## Introduction

Have you ever bitten into a raw amla and winced at its sharp sourness? Or noticed how soap feels slippery between your fingers? That tangy bite of amla comes from the **acids** it contains, while the slippery feel of soap is characteristic of **bases**. For centuries, people identified these substances simply by taste and touch. Today, we use special substances called **indicators** to identify acids and bases without tasting them.

**Litmus** is the oldest and most well-known natural indicator. It is a purple dye obtained from lichen plants. Acids turn blue litmus paper **red**, while bases turn red litmus paper **blue**. Other natural indicators include **turmeric** (haldi — turns brownish-red in bases), red cabbage juice, and the coloured juice from hibiscus (gudhal) petals.

**Synthetic indicators** like methyl orange (red in acid, yellow in base) and phenolphthalein (colourless in acid, pink in base) are manufactured in laboratories. Some substances change their **smell** in acidic or basic environments — onion juice and vanilla extract are examples of such **olfactory indicators**.

> **Key idea:** Indicators are substances that behave differently (change colour or odour) in the presence of acids versus bases, allowing us to identify the nature of a solution without tasting it.

## Chemical Properties of Acids and Bases

### Acids Reacting with Metals

When an acid meets a metal, they produce a **salt** and **hydrogen gas**.

**Acid + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen gas**

For instance, when dilute hydrochloric acid is added to zinc granules in a test tube, effervescence occurs. The gas collected can be tested by bringing a burning matchstick near the mouth of the tube — if it burns with a characteristic "pop" sound, the gas is hydrogen.

### Bases Reacting with Metals

Certain metals also react with bases to liberate hydrogen gas:

**2NaOH(aq) + Zn(s) → Na₂ZnO₂(s) + H₂(g)**

The product sodium zincate is formed. However, not every metal behaves this way with bases.

> **Key idea:** Both acids and bases can react with certain metals to produce hydrogen gas, though the salts formed are different in each case.

---

### Acids with Metal Carbonates and Hydrogencarbonates

Every metal **carbonate** and **hydrogencarbonate** reacts with acids to produce the corresponding salt, water, and carbon dioxide gas.

**Metal carbonate / Metal hydrogencarbonate + Acid → Salt + H₂O + CO₂**

Examples:
- Na₂CO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
- NaHCO₃(s) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)

How do we confirm that the gas is CO₂? Pass it through **lime water** — if the lime water turns milky, the gas is carbon dioxide:

Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)

But here is an interesting twist: if you keep bubbling excess CO₂, the milkiness vanishes because the insoluble CaCO₃ converts to soluble calcium hydrogencarbonate:

CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g) → Ca(HCO₃)₂(aq)

> **Key idea:** The reaction of acids with carbonates and hydrogencarbonates always produces CO₂, which can be detected using the lime water test (milky appearance due to CaCO₃ formation).

---

### Neutralisation Reaction

When an **acid** meets a **base**, they cancel each other out, producing a **salt** and **water**. This mutual cancellation is called a **neutralisation reaction**.

**Base + Acid → Salt + Water**

Example: NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)

### Metallic Oxides with Acids

**Metal oxides** react with acids just as bases do — producing salts and water. This confirms that metallic oxides are **basic** in character.

CuO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CuCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l)

### Non-metallic Oxides with Bases

**Non-metal oxides** react with bases to produce salts and water, behaving like acids. Therefore, non-metallic oxides are **acidic** in nature.

Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)

> **Key idea:** Metal oxides are basic; non-metal oxides are acidic. Both follow the pattern of neutralisation when they react with their chemical opposites.

## What Do All Acids and Bases Have in Common?

### Acids in Water

All acids generate **hydrogen ions H⁺(aq)** when dissolved in water. These H⁺ ions cannot exist independently — they latch onto water molecules to form **hydronium ions (H₃O⁺)**:

HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻

These H⁺ ions are the reason behind every acidic property — sour taste, reaction with metals, colour change of indicators. Substances like glucose and alcohol contain hydrogen atoms but do not release H⁺ ions in water, which is why they are not classified as acids.

Acids in solution **conduct electricity** because the ions present can carry electric current.

### Bases in Water

All bases generate **hydroxide ions OH⁻(aq)** in water. Bases that dissolve in water are specifically called **alkalis**.

NaOH(s) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

### Why Dry HCl Does Not Act as an Acid

Dry HCl gas cannot change the colour of dry litmus paper because without water, HCl cannot split into ions. No H⁺ ions means no acidic behaviour.

> **Key idea:** The defining feature of acids is the production of H⁺(aq) ions, and of bases the production of OH⁻(aq) ions, in water. Without water, neither acids nor bases show their characteristic properties.

---

## Dilution of Acids and Bases

Dissolving an acid or base in water is a highly **exothermic** process — it releases substantial heat.

**Safety rule:** Always add **acid to water** with continuous stirring. Never pour water into a concentrated acid. If water is added to acid, the intense heat generated can cause the mixture to boil and splash violently, causing severe burns. When acid is added to a large volume of water, the water absorbs the heat safely.

**Dilution** means reducing the concentration of H₃O⁺ or OH⁻ ions per unit volume by adding more water.

> **Key idea:** When diluting acids, always add acid to water, never the reverse. This prevents dangerous splashing caused by the exothermic dissolution process.

## The pH Scale — Measuring Acid and Base Strength

The **pH scale** provides a numerical measure of how acidic or basic a solution is. The name "pH" originates from the German word "potenz" (meaning power or strength). The scale runs from **0 to 14**:

- **pH = 7**: Neutral (pure water)
- **pH below 7**: Acidic (the lower the number, the stronger the acid)
- **pH above 7**: Basic/alkaline (the higher the number, the stronger the base)

A **universal indicator** is a blend of multiple indicators that displays a spectrum of colours corresponding to different pH values, making it easy to estimate pH visually.

**Strong acids** like HCl and H₂SO₄ release many H⁺ ions (very low pH). **Weak acids** like acetic acid (vinegar) and citric acid (nimbu) release fewer H⁺ ions (pH closer to 7 but still below it).

### pH in Everyday Life

**Human body:** Our blood maintains a pH between 7.0 and 7.8. Even slight deviations can have serious health consequences.

**Acid rain:** When rain water pH drops below 5.6, it becomes acid rain. In India, acid rain has contributed to the yellowing of the Taj Mahal's marble, as the sulphuric acid in rain reacts with calcium carbonate in marble.

**Agriculture:** Different crops thrive in different soil pH ranges. If soil becomes too acidic, farmers add quicklime (CaO), slaked lime (Ca(OH)₂), or chalk (CaCO₃) to neutralise it.

**Stomach and digestion:** Our stomach lining secretes HCl to aid digestion. When excess acid causes acidity or heartburn, we take **antacids** — mild bases like magnesium hydroxide (found in Milk of Magnesia) — to neutralise the extra acid.

**Dental health:** Bacteria in our mouth produce acids after we eat sugary or starchy food. When the pH drops below 5.5, tooth enamel (made of calcium phosphate) starts dissolving. Brushing with toothpaste, which is mildly basic, neutralises these mouth acids.

**Insect stings:** A honeybee sting injects formic acid (methanoic acid), causing pain and swelling. Applying a paste of baking soda (a mild base) neutralises the acid and provides relief.

> **Key idea:** The pH scale (0–14) quantifies acidity and basicity. pH governs critical biological, agricultural, and industrial processes — from blood chemistry to soil treatment to dental care.

## Salts

### Families of Salts

Salts are ionic compounds produced when acids react with bases. Salts sharing the same cation (positive ion) or the same anion (negative ion) belong to the same **salt family**.

Examples: NaCl and Na₂SO₄ both contain Na⁺ — they belong to the sodium family. NaCl and KCl both contain Cl⁻ — they belong to the chloride family.

### pH of Salts

The pH of a salt solution depends on the strengths of the acid and base that formed it:

- **Strong acid + Strong base** → Neutral salt (pH ≈ 7). Example: NaCl
- **Strong acid + Weak base** → Acidic salt (pH < 7). Example: NH₄Cl
- **Weak acid + Strong base** → Basic salt (pH > 7). Example: Na₂CO₃

> **Key idea:** A salt's pH depends on whether its parent acid and base were strong or weak. Not all salts are neutral.

## Useful Chemicals Derived from Common Salt

**Common salt (NaCl)** serves as the raw material for manufacturing several important chemicals.

### Sodium Hydroxide — The Chlor-Alkali Process

Passing electricity through concentrated brine (NaCl solution) decomposes it:

**2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + Cl₂(g) + H₂(g)**

This industrial process is called the **chlor-alkali process** ("chlor" for chlorine, "alkali" for NaOH). Chlorine gas collects at the anode, hydrogen gas at the cathode, and NaOH solution forms near the cathode.

### Bleaching Powder

**Bleaching powder** — Ca(ClO)₂ — is made by treating dry slaked lime with chlorine gas:

2Ca(OH)₂ + 2Cl₂ → Ca(ClO)₂ + CaCl₂ + 2H₂O

Applications: whitening cotton and linen in textile mills, bleaching wood pulp in paper factories, disinfecting drinking water in municipal supplies, and acting as an oxidising agent.

### Baking Soda (Sodium Hydrogencarbonate)

**Baking soda (NaHCO₃)** is a gentle, non-corrosive basic salt.

Thermal decomposition: 2NaHCO₃ →(heat) Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂

Uses:
- Making **baking powder** (a mixture of baking soda and a mild edible acid like tartaric acid). When heated, the CO₂ released creates air pockets that make bread and cake soft and spongy.
- As an active ingredient in **antacid tablets** for relieving stomach acidity.
- In **soda-acid fire extinguishers** — the CO₂ produced smothers flames.

### Washing Soda (Sodium Carbonate Decahydrate)

**Washing soda (Na₂CO₃.10H₂O)** is prepared by recrystallising sodium carbonate from its solution:

Na₂CO₃ + 10H₂O → Na₂CO₃.10H₂O

Uses: manufacturing glass, soap, and paper; producing borax; as a household cleaning agent; softening hard water by removing calcium and magnesium ions.

> **Key idea:** Common salt is the starting point for producing NaOH (via the chlor-alkali process), bleaching powder, baking soda, and washing soda — all chemicals with widespread industrial and household applications.

## Water of Crystallisation

**Water of crystallisation** refers to the specific number of water molecules chemically bound within one formula unit of a crystalline salt.

- **Copper sulphate pentahydrate**: CuSO₄.5H₂O appears as bright blue crystals. When heated, it loses its water molecules and turns into a white powder (anhydrous CuSO₄). Adding water to this white powder restores the blue colour — a reversible change that beautifully demonstrates water of crystallisation.

- **Gypsum**: CaSO₄.2H₂O (two water molecules per formula unit).

### Plaster of Paris

Heating **gypsum** at 373 K drives off part of its water, producing **Plaster of Paris** — calcium sulphate hemihydrate:

CaSO₄.2H₂O →(373 K) CaSO₄.½H₂O + 1½H₂O

When Plaster of Paris is mixed with water, it absorbs moisture and hardens back into gypsum:

CaSO₄.½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄.2H₂O

This setting property makes it invaluable for making casts for broken bones, crafting decorative figurines and toys, creating moulds, and smoothing wall surfaces.

Plaster of Paris must be stored in **sealed moisture-proof containers** because even atmospheric humidity can cause it to harden prematurely, rendering it useless.

> **Key idea:** Water of crystallisation is chemically bonded water in a crystal's structure that determines its colour and form. Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄.½H₂O) sets hard by absorbing water to re-form gypsum — which is why it must be kept absolutely dry during storage.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**The Indian antacid market.** Walk into any chemist shop and you will see ten brands competing — Eno, Digene, Gelusil, Pudin Hara, Aciloc. Most of them are simply different combinations of magnesium hydroxide, aluminium hydroxide, or sodium bicarbonate, sometimes with a flavouring acid added to make them fizz. The chemistry is identical to what you balance on paper — base neutralises HCl in the stomach. India sells more than Rs. 4,000 crore of antacid products every year. Every sip is a Class 10 neutralisation reaction.

**KVK soil pH fixes for Indian farmers.** Krishi Vigyan Kendras across India test the pH of farmers' fields before each sowing season. If the soil is too acidic — common in high-rainfall regions of Kerala, Assam, and the North-East — they advise farmers to apply quicklime (CaO) or slaked lime (Ca(OH)₂) to raise the pH to the crop's preferred range. Tea estates in Assam, paddy fields in Kerala, and tomato farms in Maharashtra all rely on this pH correction. One textbook reaction quietly supports a Rs. 30 lakh crore agricultural economy.

**Municipal water disinfection.** The chlorinated water that reaches your tap is treated with bleaching powder — Ca(ClO)₂ — at the water treatment plant. The slight smell of chlorine you sometimes notice is the chemistry of the chlor-alkali process reaching your kitchen. Cities like Mumbai, Bengaluru, and Chennai treat billions of litres a day using exactly this reaction. Without it, waterborne diseases like cholera and typhoid would explode.

### Do it yourself — the red cabbage pH lab

Make your own universal indicator from red cabbage and test **10 substances** from your home.

**To prepare the indicator:** Chop a handful of red cabbage. Boil it in 2 cups of water for 10 minutes. Let it cool. Strain off the purple liquid into a glass jar. That is your indicator.

**Then test these substances** (add 5-10 drops of indicator to about a tablespoon of each, or dip the substance into a small amount of indicator):

1. Lemon juice
2. Vinegar (sirka)
3. Eno or baking soda solution
4. Tap water
5. Soap solution
6. Tomato juice
7. Curd / dahi water
8. Milk
9. Toothpaste paste in water
10. A pinch of common salt dissolved in water

The indicator should go red/pink in acid, purple in neutral, and green/yellow in strong base. Record the colour and your estimated pH range for each. Compare with the textbook values for as many as you can find.

*⏱ 1 hour preparation + 1 hour testing · 🧹 Medium — purple liquid stains fingers and counters · 👨‍👩‍👧 Adult help for boiling the cabbage and handling the gas stove*

> **Show what you found:** Snap a photo of all 10 tubes/glasses lined up — it makes a beautiful colour gradient. Sketch the rank order in your chemistry notebook from most acidic to most basic. Show your dadu or mummy — they have been using sirka, soda, and chunna by instinct for decades. Tell them one new thing you understood, like why pickles need a specific acid level or why dahi tastes more sour the longer it sits.
