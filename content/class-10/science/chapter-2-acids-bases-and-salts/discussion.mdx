## Recognising Acids and Bases

**Child:** Maa, our teacher said we must never taste chemicals in the lab. But then how do people figure out whether something is acidic or basic?

**Mother:** Great question, beta. Long ago, people did rely on taste — the sourness of tamarind told them it was acidic, and the bitter, slippery feel of wood ash mixed with water told them it was basic. But tasting chemicals is dangerous, so scientists developed indicators. Litmus paper is the simplest — blue litmus turns red in acid, red litmus turns blue in base. And you know haldi? When you accidentally spill dal on a white kurta, the yellow turmeric stain turns reddish-brown when you wash it with soap — that is because soap is basic and turmeric changes colour in bases.

**Child:** What about phenolphthalein and methyl orange? When do we use those?

**Mother:** Phenolphthalein stays colourless in acids and turns bright pink in bases. Methyl orange does the opposite — it is red in acid and yellow in base. For your CBSE exam, just remember the colour changes clearly. In higher classes, you will learn how to choose the right indicator for titrations depending on the acid-base combination.

## How Acids and Bases React

**Child:** Maa, when I dropped a piece of zinc into the test tube with dilute HCl in the school lab, it started fizzing like crazy. What was happening?

**Mother:** The zinc was reacting with the hydrochloric acid. The fizzing was hydrogen gas being released. The equation is Zn plus H₂SO₄ gives ZnSO₄ plus H₂. If you had brought a lit matchstick near the mouth of the test tube, you would have heard a pop sound — that is the classic test for hydrogen gas. But remember, not all metals react this way. Gold and platinum are so unreactive that acids do not affect them — which is why your Dadi's gold bangles still shine after forty years.

**Child:** And acids with baking soda — that produces CO₂ and turns lime water milky?

**Mother:** Exactly. When you add nimbu juice or vinegar to a spoonful of baking soda at home, that vigorous fizz is CO₂. Pass it through lime water and it turns milky because calcium carbonate forms. Now here is a tricky part that examiners love — if you pass excess CO₂ through the milky lime water, the milkiness disappears! That happens because the insoluble CaCO₃ converts to soluble Ca(HCO₃)₂. Keep this in mind for your exam.

**Child:** Neutralisation — that is when acid plus base gives salt plus water, right? Is it always exothermic?

**Mother:** Always. When the H⁺ ions from the acid meet the OH⁻ ions from the base, they form water, releasing heat. This is why when your stomach produces too much acid and you feel that burning sensation, taking an antacid like Digene (which contains Mg(OH)₂) helps — the base neutralises the excess acid. And this same principle is why farmers add chunna (quicklime) to acidic soil before the sowing season.

## Understanding the pH Scale

**Child:** pH goes from 0 to 14, with 7 being neutral. But why is this number so important, Maa?

**Mother:** Because pH controls almost every chemical and biological process around us, beta. Think about your own body — your blood must stay between pH 7.35 and 7.45. Even a small change outside this range can be life-threatening. Your stomach, on the other hand, operates at about pH 1.2 — extremely acidic, because that acidity is needed for pepsin to digest proteins.

**Child:** The Taj Mahal getting damaged by acid rain — is that also a pH problem?

**Mother:** Precisely. Factories around Mathura and Agra used to release SO₂ and NO₂ gases. These dissolved in rainwater to form sulphuric and nitric acids, bringing the rain pH below 5.6. This acid rain reacted with the marble (CaCO₃) of the Taj Mahal, slowly corroding it. The Supreme Court even ordered those factories to switch to cleaner fuels. So you see, chemistry is not just about exams — it directly affects our heritage and environment.

**Child:** Should I memorise pH values of common substances?

**Mother:** Know the important ones: gastric juice is about pH 1, lemon juice around 2.5, vinegar about 3, black coffee around 5, milk about 6.5, pure water exactly 7, baking soda solution about 8.5, Milk of Magnesia around 10, and concentrated NaOH about 14. This helps you answer comparison-type questions confidently.

## Important Salts and Their Uses

**Child:** Common salt, baking soda, washing soda, bleaching powder, Plaster of Paris — they are all connected to NaCl somehow?

**Mother:** Most of them trace back to it. The chlor-alkali process electrolyses brine to give NaOH, Cl₂, and H₂. The chlorine gas is used to make bleaching powder by reacting it with slaked lime. Baking soda is NaHCO₃ — I use it in the kitchen to make dosa batter fluffy. When heated, baking soda becomes washing soda (Na₂CO₃), which is used in glass factories and for softening hard water. Plaster of Paris comes from gypsum rather than NaCl, but it is equally important — doctors use it for bone casts, and artisans in Rajasthan use it for making beautiful figurines.

**Child:** The water of crystallisation — CuSO₄ has 5 water molecules and looks blue, but turns white when heated?

**Mother:** Yes, and that is a favourite demonstration in board practical exams. The blue crystals of CuSO₄.5H₂O lose their water when heated and become a white powder. Add a few drops of water, and the blue colour returns instantly. The specific number of water molecules — 5 for copper sulphate, 10 for washing soda, 2 for gypsum — these must be committed to memory.

## Exam Preparation Tips

**Child:** How many marks does this chapter usually carry, Maa?

**Mother:** Typically 5 to 6 marks in the chemistry portion. Expect questions on acid-base reactions with equations, the pH scale with real-life applications, and the preparation and uses of important salts.

**Child:** What should I focus on most?

**Mother:** Three things, beta. First, write balanced equations for every reaction — acid plus metal, acid plus carbonate, neutralisation, the chlor-alkali process. Always include state symbols. Second, understand the pH scale deeply — know what happens biologically and chemically at different pH values. Third, memorise the chemical names, formulae, preparation methods, and at least two uses of each important salt. Examiners love "state two uses of..." type questions.

## Chemistry Around Us

**Child:** It feels like acids and bases are everywhere — in the kitchen, in medicines, even in farming.

**Mother:** They truly are. When I add a pinch of meetha soda to rajma to soften it faster, that is NaHCO₃ creating a slightly basic environment. When you brush your teeth at night, the mildly basic toothpaste neutralises the acids bacteria have produced from the mithai you ate. The chlorinated water that comes from our municipal supply has been treated with bleaching powder to kill harmful germs.

**Child:** For higher studies like NEET, does this chapter connect to biology too?

**Mother:** Directly. The pH of your blood, stomach juice, and intestinal fluid determines which enzymes work and how efficiently. Pepsin in the stomach works best at pH 2, while trypsin in the intestine needs pH 8. Buffer solutions — which you will study later — maintain these precise pH values in living systems. India's pharmaceutical industry, one of the largest in the world, depends on acid-base chemistry for drug formulation. What you learn now in Class 10 is truly the foundation for everything that follows.
