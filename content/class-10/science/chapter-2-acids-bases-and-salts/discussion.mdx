## Recognising Acids and Bases

**Meera:** Arjun, in the lab last week I almost forgot the litmus colours mid-experiment. Blue to red is acid, red to blue is base — but in the prelim I second-guessed myself.

**Arjun:** Many people do. The way I lock it in — acid attacks, so it "reds" things, like a wound. Base soothes, so it "blues" them. Silly but it works in the exam hall.

**Meera:** What about turmeric? My mother said when haldi dal stains a kurta and you rub soap on it, the stain turns reddish-brown.

**Arjun:** Yes, haldi is a natural indicator — turns reddish-brown in base. Soap is mildly basic. NCERT mentions it specifically because it is the most relatable Indian example. Examiners love asking olfactory and natural indicators in the 2-mark slot.

**Meera:** Phenolphthalein and methyl orange always confuse me though. Which is which?

**Arjun:** I memorise it as a pair. Phenolphthalein is the pink one — colourless in acid, pink in base. Methyl orange is the warmer pair — red in acid, yellow in base. For revision I noted them on opposite pages of my notebook so they never blur together.

**Meera:** That actually helps. I will copy that layout.

## How Acids and Bases React

**Meera:** Arjun, the zinc plus dilute HCl experiment — fizzing meant hydrogen, and the pop test confirms it. But I forgot to write the equation properly in the practical record.

**Arjun:** Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂. Two HCls because each provides one H, and you need two H to make H₂. That coefficient is the trap. The same logic for Zn + H₂SO₄ — only one H₂SO₄ because each provides two H. Balance and state symbols both get marked.

**Meera:** And then the carbonate reaction — sodium carbonate plus HCl gives CO₂. I remember the lime water test turning milky.

**Arjun:** Right. But the part most students miss is what happens with excess CO₂. The milkiness disappears because CaCO₃ becomes soluble Ca(HCO₃)₂. The 2024 board paper asked exactly that — "why does the milkiness disappear when CO₂ is passed in excess?" Two-mark question, half the kids skipped the second half.

**Meera:** I will note that. And neutralisation — acid plus base. My nani uses Eno for acidity. That is the same chemistry?

**Arjun:** Same. Eno is sodium bicarbonate plus citric acid. When dissolved in water, it neutralises the excess HCl in your stomach. The fizz is CO₂ being released. So one product fights stomach acid, demonstrates a carbonate-acid reaction, AND a neutralisation — three textbook reactions in one glass.

**Meera:** That is genuinely a useful way to remember it.

## Understanding the pH Scale

**Meera:** Arjun, the pH scale — I get that 0 to 14 with 7 neutral. But why is the human body so picky about pH?

**Arjun:** Because enzymes only work in a narrow pH window. Blood pH must stay between 7.35 and 7.45. Even 7.2 is medically dangerous. Stomach is around 1.2 because pepsin only works in strong acid. Intestine is alkaline because trypsin needs that. Different enzymes, different pH zones.

**Meera:** And acid rain — the Taj Mahal damage — that is a pH problem too?

**Arjun:** Direct pH problem. SO₂ and NO₂ from factories near Mathura dissolve in rainwater to form H₂SO₄ and HNO₃. Rain pH drops below 5.6 and the rain becomes corrosive. CaCO₃ in marble reacts with the acid. The Supreme Court ordered the Mathura refineries to clean up specifically because of this. India's heritage protection has chemistry at its core.

**Meera:** My cousin is preparing for NEET. She said acid-base chemistry comes up in biology too.

**Arjun:** Constantly. Buffer solutions in blood, pH-dependent enzyme activity, kidney filtration, soil chemistry for crops. The KVK extension officers in our district help farmers test soil pH and add chunna if it is too acidic for the crop they want. Class 10 chapter, lifelong applications.

**Meera:** I need to memorise the pH values for common substances then.

**Arjun:** Yes. Gastric juice 1, lemon 2.5, vinegar 3, black coffee 5, milk 6.5, water 7, baking soda 8.5, Milk of Magnesia 10, conc NaOH 14. The board paper often gives you four substances and asks you to arrange in increasing acidity. Pure memorisation marks.

## Important Salts and Their Uses

**Meera:** Arjun, common salt, baking soda, washing soda, bleaching powder, Plaster of Paris — five salts, five preparations, ten uses. It is a lot.

**Arjun:** I made a single-page table. Salt, formula, preparation reaction, two uses. One row per salt. The whole chapter's salt portion fits on one A4 sheet. I will share it.

**Meera:** That would help. And the chlor-alkali process — that is the master reaction linking many of them?

**Arjun:** Yes. Brine plus electricity gives NaOH at the cathode, Cl₂ at the anode, H₂ also at cathode. NaOH goes to soap and paper. Cl₂ goes to bleaching powder, PVC, water treatment. H₂ goes to vanaspati ghee. One reaction, three product chains. The Tata Chemicals plant at Mithapur and the Grasim plant at Nagda both run it on giant scale.

**Meera:** Water of crystallisation always trips me up. How many water molecules go with which salt?

**Arjun:** Three you must memorise — CuSO₄·5H₂O blue, Na₂CO₃·10H₂O washing soda, CaSO₄·2H₂O gypsum. The classic practical question is the colour change when blue copper sulphate is heated and then water is added back. Examiners want you to write both observations and both equations.

**Meera:** And Plaster of Paris is a hemihydrate — half a water molecule per formula unit?

**Arjun:** Yes, CaSO₄·½H₂O. Heated from gypsum at 373 K. When mixed with water it absorbs the water back and hardens into gypsum again. That hardening is why doctors use it for bone casts. Storage rule — keep absolutely dry or it sets in the bag itself.

## Exam Preparation Tips

**Meera:** Arjun, this chapter typically — how many marks?

**Arjun:** 5 to 6. The acid-base properties usually take a 3-mark equation question. The pH scale takes a 2-mark application. Salts take a 2 to 3-mark factual or preparation question. With careful prep you can get all of it.

**Meera:** Where do students lose marks most often?

**Arjun:** Three places. One — not balancing equations and skipping state symbols. Two — confusing the colour changes of phenolphthalein and methyl orange. Three — forgetting the formula of bleaching powder, Ca(ClO)₂, or writing the chlor-alkali equation incorrectly. I drilled these from the NCERT in-text exercises.

## Chemistry Around Us

**Meera:** Sometimes when I am revising I feel like this chapter is just kitchen and bathroom chemistry made formal.

**Arjun:** That is actually a good frame. Toothpaste is mildly basic to neutralise mouth acid from bacteria. Antacid tablets are Mg(OH)₂ or Al(OH)₃. Eno is bicarbonate. Chunna painted on tree trunks is calcium hydroxide for pest control. Municipal water in our city is disinfected with bleaching powder. Every house runs on this chapter.

**Meera:** And for NEET — buffer chemistry, blood pH, gastric pH, enzyme pH — all of it grows from here?

**Arjun:** Directly. Class 11 chemistry has equilibrium and pH calculations, Class 12 has electrochemistry. The neutralisation reactions we balance now reappear there as ionic equations. India's pharmaceutical industry — Cipla, Sun Pharma, Dr. Reddy's, all the Hyderabad units — every drug formulation needs precise pH control. So this is not just textbook stuff; it is a Rs. 2 lakh crore industry.
