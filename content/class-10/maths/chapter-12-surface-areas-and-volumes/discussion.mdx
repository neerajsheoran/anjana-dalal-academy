## Combination of Solids

**Child:** Maa, I never noticed before, but so many things around us are made of simple shapes joined together. A sharpened pencil is a cylinder with a cone tip. A dosa cone is literally a cone!

**Mother:** That is the beauty of this chapter, beta. Once your eyes open to it, you see geometry everywhere. A matka is like a sphere with a short cylindrical neck. An ice cream cone with a scoop on top is a cone plus a hemisphere. Even the columns of our old temples are cylinders topped with decorative shapes.

**Child:** Do real engineers think this way too?

**Mother:** Absolutely. When ISRO designs a rocket like the PSLV, the nose cone is a cone, the main body is a cylinder, and the fuel tanks can be modelled as cylinders with hemispherical caps. Every surface area and volume must be calculated precisely for heat shielding, fuel capacity, and structural strength.

## Surface Area of Combined Solids

**Child:** When I place a cone on top of a hemisphere, do I just add both total surface areas?

![Cone joined on top of a hemisphere, with the hidden circular junction area crossed out, showing only the visible CSA of each part](combined-solid-hidden-surface.png)

**Mother:** No, and this is the single most important concept in this chapter. When two solids are joined, the surfaces at the junction become internal -- they are hidden from view. For a cone sitting on a hemisphere, the flat circular base of the cone and the flat circular face of the hemisphere merge together. Both are hidden. You only count the curved surface area of the cone plus the curved surface area of the hemisphere.

**Child:** So the formula is CSA of cone plus CSA of hemisphere?

**Mother:** Exactly. The general principle is: add up all surfaces that remain visible from outside. The fused surfaces are internal and must be excluded. Students who mechanically add total surface areas without removing the hidden parts will always get wrong answers.

**Child:** What if the joined parts have different radii?

**Mother:** In CBSE problems, the joining surfaces almost always have the same radius -- a cone of radius 5 cm sits perfectly on a hemisphere of radius 5 cm. If they were different, there would be an exposed annular ring at the junction, but you will not see that in board exams.

## Volume of Combined Solids and Conversion Problems

**Child:** For volume, it is simpler -- I just add up the individual volumes without subtracting anything?

**Mother:** Correct. Volume is purely additive. The volume of a cone on a hemisphere equals the volume of the cone plus the volume of the hemisphere. There are no hidden volumes to worry about.

**Child:** And the recasting problems -- melting one shape and making another -- those are based on volume staying the same?

**Mother:** Yes, beta. When a goldsmith melts a gold sphere and reshapes it into a wire (which is a very long, thin cylinder), the volume of the sphere equals the volume of the wire. Set the two volume formulas equal and solve for the unknown measurement. Similarly, when you melt a metal cuboid into coins, divide the cuboid's volume by one coin's volume to find the number of coins.

**Child:** The water pipe problem is the same idea?

**Mother:** Exactly the same. The volume of water flowing per second through the pipe equals pi times the pipe's inner radius squared times the flow speed. The volume needed to fill the tank to a certain height equals pi times the tank's radius squared times the desired height. Equate total flow to required volume and solve for time.

## Frustum of a Cone

**Child:** A frustum is basically a bucket shape -- a cone with the pointy top chopped off?

**Mother:** That is a perfect description. When you slice a cone with a cut parallel to its base, the lower piece is a frustum. Buckets, gamlaa (flower pots), and lampshades are all frustums. The formulas involve two radii -- r1 for the wider base and r2 for the narrower opening.

**Child:** The volume formula has three terms inside -- r1 squared, r2 squared, and r1 times r2. That is hard to memorise.

**Mother:** Here is a way to remember: the formula is derived by subtracting the smaller cone from the larger one. You do not need the derivation for boards, but knowing it helps the formula stick. Notice that if the smaller radius r2 shrinks to zero, the formula reduces to the standard cone volume formula: (1/3) pi r1 squared times h. That is a good sanity check.

**Child:** And the slant height uses r1 minus r2 instead of just r?

**Mother:** Yes. It is analogous to the cone's slant height formula but with the difference of the two radii replacing the single radius. You need this slant height for the curved surface area calculation.

## Board Exam Strategy

**Child:** What types of questions come most often from this chapter, Maa?

**Mother:** Three main types. First, finding the surface area or volume of a combined solid -- worth 4 to 5 marks. Second, conversion problems where one shape is melted into another -- worth 3 to 4 marks. Third, frustum problems involving buckets or containers -- worth 3 to 4 marks. Overall, this chapter can carry 5 to 6 marks.

**Child:** What mistakes should I avoid?

**Mother:** Four common errors. First, using diameter instead of radius -- always halve the diameter before substituting. Second, including hidden surfaces when computing the total surface area of a combined solid. Third, unit conversion blunders -- remember that 1 litre is 1000 cubic centimetres and 1 cubic metre is 1000 litres. Fourth, arithmetic mistakes with pi -- keep pi as 22/7 throughout your working and simplify fractions only at the end.

**Child:** Is this chapter useful in real life beyond exams?

**Mother:** Tremendously so. Civil engineers calculate how much concrete goes into cylindrical pillars, how much paint covers a dome, and how much water a tank holds. Pharmaceutical companies compute capsule volumes for accurate drug dosing. Packaging designers use these formulas to minimise material while maximising content. In a rapidly growing country like India, where new buildings, water tanks, and bridges go up every day, these calculations are part of everyday professional work.
