## The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

**Child:** Maa, we already did prime factorisation in Class 6 and 7. Why is it coming back in Class 10 as this "Fundamental Theorem"?

**Mother:** Because earlier you only learnt the skill of breaking numbers into primes. Now the theorem tells you something much deeper -- that this breakdown is always possible and always gives the same result. No matter how you split a number, the final set of primes is identical. That guarantee of uniqueness is what makes it "fundamental."

**Child:** But why does uniqueness matter? It seems obvious that you would get the same primes.

**Mother:** Think about it this way. Every time you make a UPI payment from Papa's phone, the transaction is protected by encryption. That encryption depends on multiplying two huge prime numbers together. If prime factorisation were not unique -- if the same product could come from different sets of primes -- the entire security system would break. Hackers could find alternative factorisations and steal money. Uniqueness is what keeps digital India safe.

**Child:** That is amazing! So number theory protects our bank accounts?

**Mother:** Exactly. Mathematicians at research centres across India work on making encryption stronger, and it all traces back to this theorem about primes.

**Child:** How do they ask about this theorem in the board exam?

**Mother:** Usually as a 1-mark question: "State the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic." Write it precisely -- "Every composite number can be expressed as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique, apart from the order in which the prime factors occur." Do not skip the word "unique" -- that is the whole point.

## HCF and LCM by Prime Factorisation

**Child:** Maa, I keep mixing up the HCF and LCM rules. When do I pick the smallest power and when do I pick the greatest?

**Mother:** Here is how I remember it. HCF stands for Highest Common Factor, but ironically you take the **smallest** powers -- because you want only what is genuinely common to both numbers. LCM is about the Least Common Multiple, but you take the **greatest** powers -- because you need to cover everything from both numbers. Think of HCF as the overlap and LCM as the umbrella.

**Child:** And HCF times LCM equals the product of the two numbers -- does that always work?

**Mother:** Only for exactly two numbers. For three or more numbers, that formula fails. Students lose marks every year by applying it blindly to three numbers. Also, always verify your answer: compute HCF times LCM and check that it equals a times b. That verification step shows the examiner you understand the relationship.

**Child:** What about the problems where two people run around a circular track?

**Mother:** Those are LCM problems. Suppose Meera takes 15 minutes per lap and Arjun takes 20 minutes. They meet again at the start after LCM of 15 and 20, which is 60 minutes. The Indian Space Research Organisation uses similar calculations for satellite orbits -- when will two satellites return to the same relative position?

## Proving Irrationality

**Child:** The proof that root 2 is irrational uses contradiction. I follow each step, but how do I write it perfectly in the exam so I do not lose marks?

**Mother:** This proof is worth 3-4 marks and appears almost every year. Follow this exact structure. First, assume root 2 is rational, meaning root 2 equals a over b where a and b are coprime. Second, square both sides to get a squared equals 2 times b squared. Third, conclude that 2 divides a, so write a as 2k. Fourth, substitute back to get b squared equals 2 times k squared, which means 2 divides b as well. Fifth, point out the contradiction -- both a and b are divisible by 2, but we assumed they had no common factor.

**Child:** For root 3, I just replace 2 with 3 everywhere?

**Mother:** Yes, the skeleton is identical for any prime p. The crucial theorem you must cite is Theorem 1.2 -- if a prime p divides a squared, then p divides a. Many students forget to mention this theorem and lose a mark. Write it out clearly before you use it.

## Irrationality of Expressions

**Child:** How do I prove that something like 7 plus root 5 is irrational?

**Mother:** This is a shorter proof, usually worth 2 marks. Assume 7 plus root 5 is rational, equal to a over b. Then root 5 equals a over b minus 7, which simplifies to a rational number. But root 5 is irrational -- contradiction. Finished.

**Child:** So I need to first establish that root 5 is irrational?

**Mother:** Correct. In the exam, if the question only asks about 7 plus root 5, you can simply write "Since root 5 is irrational (proved earlier)..." and proceed. But if the question says "prove root 5 is irrational, and hence prove 7 plus root 5 is irrational," you must write both proofs completely. Read the question carefully -- the CBSE marking scheme deducts marks for incomplete reasoning.

## Board Exam Strategy

**Child:** What are the mistakes students make most often in this chapter?

**Mother:** Three big ones. First, forgetting to state that a and b are coprime in irrationality proofs -- without that assumption, the contradiction does not work. Second, swapping the HCF and LCM rules -- remember, smallest common powers for HCF, greatest for LCM. Third, using the product formula for three numbers when it only works for two.

**Child:** How many marks does this chapter carry?

**Mother:** Typically 3 to 5 marks. Expect one irrationality proof and one HCF-LCM computation. If you master these two question types, this chapter becomes easy marks.

**Child:** Does any of this connect to competitive exam preparation?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Number theory -- the study of whole numbers -- is one of the oldest and most beautiful branches of mathematics. The great Indian mathematician Ramanujan made astonishing discoveries about prime numbers and number patterns. The Fundamental Theorem and Euclid's Algorithm are the starting points for modular arithmetic, which appears in Olympiad problems and JEE. As Ramanujan showed the world, even the simplest numbers can hold the deepest secrets.
