## Introduction

Building on what you discovered about real numbers in Class 9, this chapter takes you deeper into the hidden structure of whole numbers. Two powerful mathematical ideas form the backbone of everything we explore here:

- **Euclid's Division Algorithm** -- a systematic way to understand how integers divide each other
- **The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic** -- the idea that every whole number has a unique "fingerprint" made of prime numbers

With these tools, we can calculate the HCF of large numbers efficiently, prove that numbers like **root 2**, **root 3**, and **root 5** cannot be written as fractions, and predict the decimal behaviour of rational numbers.

---

## The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

From your earlier classes, you know how to break a number into its prime building blocks. For instance:

- 3 = 3
- 12 = 2 x 2 x 3
- 385 = 5 x 7 x 11

Here is the deeper question: if you pick any composite number at random -- say a number with thousands of digits -- can you always break it down into primes? And will you always get the same set of primes no matter how you go about it?

The answer to both questions is **yes**. This is the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic.

> **Key idea:** Every composite number can be written as a product of prime numbers, and this prime factorisation is **unique** -- the same primes appear with the same powers every time, regardless of the order you write them in.

### Understanding Uniqueness

Consider the number 23100. No matter which path you take to factorise it, you always arrive at:

- 23100 = 2^2 x 3 x 5^2 x 7 x 11

You might write 3 x 7 x 11 x 2^2 x 5^2 or any other rearrangement, but the collection of primes and their powers never changes.

### Building a Factor Tree

The factor tree is a visual method to reach the prime factorisation:

1. Start with your number at the top
2. Split it into any two factors
3. Continue splitting each branch until every leaf is a prime
4. Collect all the primes from the leaves

**Example:** Find the prime factorisation of 180.

- 180 = 2 x 90
- 90 = 2 x 45
- 45 = 3 x 15
- 15 = 3 x 5
- So, **180 = 2 x 2 x 3 x 3 x 5 = 2^2 x 3^2 x 5**

## HCF and LCM Using Prime Factorisation

The Fundamental Theorem gives us an elegant way to compute **HCF** (Highest Common Factor) and **LCM** (Least Common Multiple).

### The Rules

- **HCF** = Multiply together the **lowest power** of every prime that appears in **both** factorisations
- **LCM** = Multiply together the **highest power** of every prime that appears in **either** factorisation

### Worked Example 1: Find HCF and LCM of 8 and 36

**Step 1:** Write the prime factorisations.
- 8 = 2^3
- 36 = 2^2 x 3^2

**Step 2:** For HCF, take the lowest power of each common prime.
- Only common prime: 2. Lowest power: 2^2
- HCF = **4**

**Step 3:** For LCM, take the highest power of every prime.
- LCM = 2^3 x 3^2 = 8 x 9 = **72**

**Verification:** HCF x LCM = 4 x 72 = 288 = 8 x 36. Verified!

> **Key idea:** For any two positive integers a and b: **HCF(a, b) x LCM(a, b) = a x b**. This golden relationship lets you find one quantity from the other!

### Worked Example 2: Find HCF of 84 and 360, then find their LCM

**Step 1:** Prime factorise.
- 84 = 2^2 x 3 x 7
- 360 = 2^3 x 3^2 x 5

**Step 2:** HCF = 2^2 x 3 = **12**

**Step 3:** LCM = (84 x 360) / HCF = 30240 / 12 = **2520**

### Worked Example 3: Find HCF and LCM of 8, 48, and 90

**Step 1:** Prime factorise.
- 8 = 2^3
- 48 = 2^4 x 3
- 90 = 2 x 3^2 x 5

**Step 2:** HCF = 2^1 = **2** (only 2 is common to all three, and its smallest power is 1)

**Step 3:** LCM = 2^4 x 3^2 x 5 = 16 x 9 x 5 = **720** (highest powers of all primes involved)

> **Key idea:** For **three** numbers, the formula HCF x LCM = product of the three numbers does **NOT** hold. It only works for exactly two numbers.

## Applications of the Fundamental Theorem

### Can 8^n Ever End with the Digit 0?

If 8^n ends in 0, it must be divisible by both 2 and 5. But:

- 8^n = (2^3)^n = 2^(3n)

The only prime factor is 2. There is **no factor of 5** anywhere. By the uniqueness guarantee of the Fundamental Theorem, no 5 can ever appear.

**Therefore, 8^n can never end with the digit zero.**

### Can 12^n End with the Digit 0?

Similarly, 12^n = (2^2 x 3)^n = 2^(2n) x 3^n. Once again, there is no prime factor of 5, so **12^n also cannot end with digit zero**.

### Showing Numbers Are Composite

**Example:** Show that 5 x 13 x 17 + 17 is a composite number.

- 5 x 13 x 17 + 17 = 17(5 x 13 + 1) = 17 x 66 = 17 x 2 x 3 x 11

Since this number has multiple prime factors (and is therefore not prime), it is **composite**.

### Jogging Track Problem

**Problem:** Priya completes one lap of a jogging track in 15 minutes and Arjun completes one lap in 20 minutes. If they start together from the same point, after how many minutes will they meet again at the starting point?

**Solution:** They will meet after **LCM(15, 20)** minutes.

- 15 = 3 x 5
- 20 = 2^2 x 5
- LCM = 2^2 x 3 x 5 = 4 x 3 x 5 = **60 minutes**

## Revisiting Irrational Numbers

A number is **irrational** if it cannot be expressed as p/q, where p and q are integers with q not equal to zero.

Familiar irrational numbers include: root 2, root 3, root 7, pi

### Important Theorem

**Theorem 1.2:** If p is a prime number and p divides a^2 (where a is a positive integer), then p must also divide a.

This theorem, which follows from the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, is the key tool for proving irrationality.

---

## Proving Root 2 Is Irrational

We use **proof by contradiction** -- assume the opposite of what we want to prove, and show that it leads to an impossible situation.

**Step 1:** Suppose, for the sake of argument, that root 2 is rational.

**Step 2:** Then root 2 = a/b, where a and b are coprime integers (they share no common factor except 1) and b is not zero.

**Step 3:** Squaring both sides: 2 = a^2/b^2, which gives **a^2 = 2b^2**.

**Step 4:** Since 2 divides a^2, by Theorem 1.2, **2 must divide a**. Write a = 2k for some integer k.

**Step 5:** Substituting back: 2b^2 = (2k)^2 = 4k^2, so **b^2 = 2k^2**.

**Step 6:** Now 2 divides b^2, so by Theorem 1.2, **2 divides b**.

**Step 7:** But we just showed 2 divides both a and b. This **contradicts** our assumption that a and b are coprime!

**Conclusion:** Our assumption was wrong. Therefore, **root 2 is irrational**.

---

## Proving Root 3 Is Irrational

The argument follows the identical blueprint, replacing 2 with 3.

**Step 1:** Assume root 3 = a/b where a and b are coprime integers.

**Step 2:** Squaring: 3b^2 = a^2. So 3 divides a^2, meaning 3 divides a.

**Step 3:** Writing a = 3k: 3b^2 = 9k^2, which gives b^2 = 3k^2. So 3 divides b.

**Step 4:** Both a and b are divisible by 3 -- this contradicts the coprimality assumption.

**Conclusion: root 3 is irrational.**

> **Key idea:** For any prime number p, root p is irrational. The proof always works the same way: assume rationality, show p divides both the numerator and denominator, and reach a contradiction.

## Irrationality of Expressions Involving Irrational Numbers

### Fundamental Results

- The **sum or difference** of a rational number and an irrational number is always **irrational**.
- The **product or quotient** of a non-zero rational number and an irrational number is always **irrational**.

### Worked Example: Prove 7 + root 5 is irrational

**Step 1:** Assume 7 + root 5 is rational. Then 7 + root 5 = a/b for some coprime integers a, b.

**Step 2:** Rearranging: root 5 = a/b - 7 = (a - 7b)/b.

**Step 3:** Since a and b are integers, (a - 7b)/b is rational. This would make root 5 rational.

**Step 4:** But root 5 is irrational -- **contradiction!**

**Conclusion: 7 + root 5 is irrational.**

### Worked Example: Prove 5 root 3 is irrational

**Step 1:** Assume 5 root 3 is rational. Then 5 root 3 = a/b for coprime integers a, b.

**Step 2:** Rearranging: root 3 = a/(5b).

**Step 3:** Since a, b, and 5 are integers, a/(5b) is rational. This would make root 3 rational.

**Step 4:** But root 3 is irrational -- **contradiction!**

**Conclusion: 5 root 3 is irrational.**

## Chapter Summary

1. **The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic:** Every composite number has a unique prime factorisation (ignoring the ordering of factors).

2. **Theorem 1.2:** If p is a prime and p divides a^2, then p divides a (where a is a positive integer).

3. **Irrationality proofs:** By combining the Fundamental Theorem with proof by contradiction, we can establish that root 2, root 3, root 5, and similar numbers are irrational.

4. **HCF and LCM by prime factorisation:**
   - HCF = product of the lowest powers of all common prime factors
   - LCM = product of the highest powers of all prime factors appearing in either number
   - For two numbers a and b: HCF(a, b) x LCM(a, b) = a x b

5. **Key relationships for three numbers p, q, r:**
   - LCM(p, q, r) x HCF(p, q, r) does NOT equal p x q x r
   - Special formulas involving pairwise HCFs and LCMs apply instead
