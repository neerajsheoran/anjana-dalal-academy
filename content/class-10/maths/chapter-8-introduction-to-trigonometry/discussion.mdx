## The Six Trigonometric Ratios

**Child:** Maa, I keep getting confused between which ratio is which. Sin, cos, tan and then their reciprocals -- how do I sort them out?

**Mother:** Use the phrase "Some Old Houses Can Always Hide Their Old Age," beta. The first letters give you: S-O-H, C-A-H, T-O-A. Sin = Opposite/Hypotenuse, Cos = Adjacent/Hypotenuse, Tan = Opposite/Adjacent. For reciprocals, cosec flips sin, sec flips cos, and cot flips tan.

**Child:** But "opposite" and "adjacent" change depending on which angle I am looking at?

**Mother:** Exactly. The hypotenuse is always fixed -- it is the side facing the right angle. But "opposite" and "adjacent" swap when you switch your focus to the other acute angle. This is actually why the complementary angle formulas work -- sin of an angle equals cos of the complementary angle, because what is opposite for one is adjacent for the other.

**Child:** Maa, you said Indian mathematicians worked on this centuries ago?

**Mother:** Yes! Aryabhata, in the 5th century CE, developed the concept of "ardha-jya" -- literally "half-chord" -- which is essentially our modern sine. The word "sine" itself traces back through Arabic and Latin to the Sanskrit word "jya." Indian mathematicians were true pioneers of trigonometry, and this is something to be genuinely proud of.

## Standard Angle Values

**Child:** The table of values for 0, 30, 45, 60, and 90 degrees -- do I need to memorise the whole thing, Maa?

**Mother:** You only need the sine row, beta. Use the beautiful pattern: root 0 over 2, root 1 over 2, root 2 over 2, root 3 over 2, root 4 over 2. That gives you 0, half, 1 over root 2, root 3 over 2, and 1. Cosine values are the same list in reverse order. And tan is just sin divided by cos -- you can work it out on the spot.

**Child:** That is such a neat trick! And cosec, sec, cot are just reciprocals?

**Mother:** Yes. But be alert -- sin 0 is zero, so cosec 0 is undefined because you cannot divide by zero. Similarly, cos 90 is zero, making sec 90 and tan 90 undefined. Board exams sometimes specifically test whether you know which values do not exist.

**Child:** What if I forget the table in the middle of the exam?

**Mother:** Start with an equilateral triangle of side 2 units. Draw a perpendicular from one vertex to the opposite side. You get a right triangle with hypotenuse 2, short side 1, and height root 3. The 30-degree angle is at the vertex, the 60-degree angle is at the base. Read off the ratios directly. For 45 degrees, use an isosceles right triangle with equal sides of 1 unit.

## Complementary Angles

**Child:** sin of 90 minus P equals cos P -- so sin 50 equals cos 40, Maa?

**Mother:** Precisely. And tan 70 equals cot 20, sec 65 equals cosec 25, and so on. Whenever you see an angle and its complement in the same expression, use these identities to simplify. A classic board question is something like cos 55 over sin 35 -- since 55 and 35 are complementary, cos 55 equals sin 35, so the fraction equals 1.

**Child:** How about converting cot 80 to an angle below 45?

**Mother:** cot 80 equals cot(90 - 10) which equals tan 10. Similarly, sin 75 equals cos 15. These conversions are standard 1-mark questions. The key is the word "co" -- cosine is the complement of sine, cotangent is the complement of tangent, cosecant is the complement of secant.

## Trigonometric Identities

**Child:** The three identities -- sin squared P plus cos squared P equals 1, then 1 plus tan squared P equals sec squared P, and 1 plus cot squared P equals cosec squared P -- they all come from the Pythagoras theorem, Maa?

**Mother:** Every single one. Take the Pythagorean equation for a right triangle and divide by a different side squared each time. Divide by the hypotenuse squared -- you get Identity 1. Divide by the adjacent side squared -- Identity 2. Divide by the opposite side squared -- Identity 3. Three outfits, same person underneath.

**Child:** When proving identities in the exam, should I work on both sides at once?

**Mother:** Best approach: pick the messier side and simplify it step by step until it matches the cleaner side. If you feel stuck, convert everything to sin and cos -- that strategy works almost universally. Also keep an eye out for algebraic identities like a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)(a+b).

**Child:** These proofs carry good marks?

**Mother:** Typically 3-4 marks per proof. Write every step clearly, noting which identity or formula you applied. CBSE examiners award step marks, so even a partially correct proof earns partial credit. Practise at least 15 different identity proofs before the exam.

## Board Exam Tips

**Child:** What are the most common question types, Maa?

**Mother:** Four types dominate. First, evaluating numerical expressions using standard values -- like compute sin 30 cos 60 plus cos 30 sin 60. Second, proving identities -- usually one 3-4 mark question. Third, complementary angle simplification problems. Fourth, finding all ratios when one ratio is given.

**Child:** Any mistakes I should particularly guard against?

**Mother:** Never write sin P as "sin multiplied by P" -- it is a single function applied to the angle. Do not confuse sin^2 P (meaning sin P, whole thing squared) with sin of P^2. And always specify the angle -- "sin" by itself has no value. These notational blunders cost marks unnecessarily.

**Child:** How does this chapter connect to higher studies?

**Mother:** Trigonometry is one of the most heavily weighted topics in JEE Mathematics. In Class 11, you will extend these ratios to all angles using the unit circle, study trigonometric equations, and meet inverse trigonometric functions. In Class 12, trigonometric substitutions appear in integral calculus. The identities and standard values you master now form the bedrock for all of that. Aryabhata's work on sine tables started a mathematical tradition that has now lasted over fifteen centuries -- and you are part of it.
