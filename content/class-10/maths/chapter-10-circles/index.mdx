## Introduction

From your earlier studies, you already have a solid understanding of circles -- their definition, chords, arcs, sectors, and segments. This chapter zooms in on one fascinating idea: the **tangent** to a circle.

Picture a railway track running alongside a circular reservoir. At one particular spot, the track just barely grazes the boundary of the reservoir without cutting through it. That single point of contact illustrates the concept of a tangent perfectly.

When we place a straight line and a circle in the same plane, three distinct scenarios emerge:

1. **No meeting point** -- the line stays entirely away from the circle (a non-intersecting line)
2. **Two meeting points** -- the line slices through the circle (this line is a **secant**)
3. **Exactly one meeting point** -- the line just brushes against the circle (this line is a **tangent**)

---

## Tangent to a Circle

A **tangent** to a circle is a straight line that meets the circle at **precisely one point**. This single point is known as the **point of contact** (or point of tangency).

![Circle with a tangent line touching at one point, a secant crossing at two points, and a non-intersecting line, all labeled](tangent-secant-non-intersecting-line.png)

### Building Intuition

Consider a secant -- a line that crosses a circle at two points, say A and B. Now imagine sliding the secant in such a way that points A and B move toward each other. The moment A and B collapse into a single point, the secant transforms into a tangent.

> **Core insight:** A tangent can be thought of as the limiting position of a secant when its two intersection points merge into one.

---

## Theorem 1: Tangent is Perpendicular to Radius

**Theorem 10.1:** At any point on a circle, the tangent is **perpendicular** to the radius drawn to that point.

### Proof

Given: A circle centred at O with tangent line MN touching the circle at point P.
To prove: OP is perpendicular to MN.

**Step 1:** Select any arbitrary point R on line MN, where R is different from P.

**Step 2:** Because MN is a tangent, it touches the circle only at P. Therefore R must lie **outside** the circle (if R were on or inside the circle, MN would intersect the circle at more than one point).

**Step 3:** Since R is outside the circle, OR > OP (the distance from the centre to an exterior point exceeds the radius).

**Step 4:** This inequality holds for **every** point R on MN except P. Hence OP is the **shortest distance** from O to line MN.

**Step 5:** The shortest distance from a point to a line is always along the perpendicular. Therefore, **OP is perpendicular to MN**.

> **Core insight:** At any point on a circle, exactly one tangent exists, and it forms a 90-degree angle with the radius at that point.

### Everyday Illustration

Think about a potter's wheel spinning on a flat surface. The flat table (tangent) always meets the circular base of the wheel at a right angle to the spoke (radius) pointing straight down to the contact point.

## Number of Tangents from a Point

How many tangents you can draw depends entirely on **where the point sits** relative to the circle:

| Location of Point | Number of Tangents |
|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Inside** the circle | **0** (impossible to draw any tangent) |
| **On** the circle | **1** (exactly one tangent) |
| **Outside** the circle | **2** (exactly two tangents) |

When two tangents emerge from an external point, the distance measured from that external point to each point of contact is called the **tangent length**.

---

## Theorem 2: Equal Tangent Lengths

**Theorem 10.2:** Two tangents drawn from an **external point** to a circle have **equal length**.

### Proof

![Circle with centre O, external point P, and two tangents PQ and PR drawn to the circle showing equal lengths](equal-tangent-lengths-from-external-point.png)

Given: A circle centred at O, an external point P, with tangents PA and PB touching the circle at A and B respectively.
To prove: PA = PB.

**Step 1:** Draw line segments OP, OA, and OB.

**Step 2:** Since PA is a tangent and OA is a radius, angle OAP = 90 degrees (Theorem 10.1). Likewise, angle OBP = 90 degrees.

**Step 3:** Now examine right triangles OAP and OBP:
- OA = OB (both are radii of the same circle)
- OP = OP (shared hypotenuse)

**Step 4:** By the **RHS (Right angle-Hypotenuse-Side) congruence rule**, triangle OAP is congruent to triangle OBP.

**Step 5:** By CPCT (Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles), **PA = PB**.

> **Core insight:** From any external point, the two tangents to a circle are always equal in length. Moreover, the line joining the external point to the centre bisects the angle formed between the two tangents.

### Bonus Result

From the congruence above, we also obtain: **angle OPA = angle OPB**. This tells us that OP acts as the **angle bisector** of the angle APB. In other words, the centre always lies on the bisector of the angle between the two tangents.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Concentric Circles and Tangent Chords

Two circles share the same centre O. A chord of the outer circle happens to touch the inner circle. Prove that this chord is bisected at the point where it touches the inner circle.

**Solution:** Let the chord be CD, touching the inner circle at point M.

- CD is a tangent to the inner circle at M
- OM is the radius of the inner circle to the contact point
- By Theorem 10.1, OM is perpendicular to CD
- A perpendicular dropped from the centre of a circle to a chord **bisects** the chord
- Hence, **CM = DM**

### Example 2: Relationship Between Tangent Angle and Central Angle

Two tangents SA and SB are drawn from external point S to a circle centred at O. Show that angle ASB = 2 times angle OAB.

Let angle ASB = alpha.

Since SA = SB (equal tangent lengths), triangle SAB is isosceles.
Therefore, angle SAB = angle SBA = (180 - alpha)/2 = 90 - alpha/2

Also, angle OAS = 90 degrees (tangent perpendicular to radius).

So angle OAB = angle OAS - angle SAB = 90 - (90 - alpha/2) = **alpha/2**

Therefore, angle ASB = 2 times angle OAB. Proved!

### Example 3: Computing Tangent Length Using Pythagoras

From a point M, a tangent is drawn to a circle. The tangent length is 40 cm and M is 41 cm from the centre. Determine the radius.

Let the radius = r. Since the tangent and radius are perpendicular at the contact point:

By Pythagoras: 41^2 = 40^2 + r^2
1681 = 1600 + r^2
r^2 = 81
**r = 9 cm**

### Example 4: Tangent Length from Chord Data

AB is a chord of length 12 cm in a circle of radius 10 cm. Tangents at A and B meet at point T. Find TA.

**Step 1:** Join OT. By symmetry, OT is perpendicular to AB and bisects it at point M.
So AM = BM = 6 cm.

**Step 2:** OM = root(OA^2 - AM^2) = root(100 - 36) = root(64) = 8 cm.

**Step 3:** Using the relationship in the right triangle OAT:
TA/OA = AM/OM
TA/10 = 6/8
**TA = 60/8 = 7.5 cm**

## Important Properties for Board Exams

### Tangents at the Endpoints of a Diameter are Parallel

When tangents are drawn at both ends of a diameter, they turn out to be **parallel** to each other (both are perpendicular to the same diameter, so they must be parallel).

### Circumscribed Quadrilateral Property

If a quadrilateral PQRS is drawn to **circumscribe** a circle (meaning all four sides are tangents to the circle), then:

**PQ + RS = PS + QR**

This works because the tangent segments from each vertex to the circle are equal. When you sum opposite pairs of sides, the equal tangent segments pair up perfectly.

### A Circumscribed Parallelogram Must Be a Rhombus

If a **parallelogram** circumscribes a circle, it is necessarily a **rhombus**. Here is why: In a parallelogram, opposite sides are equal, so PQ = RS and PS = QR. Combined with PQ + RS = PS + QR, we get 2PQ = 2PS, meaning PQ = PS. All four sides are therefore equal -- making it a rhombus.

### Supplementary Angles Between Tangents and at Centre

The angle between two tangents from an external point and the angle formed at the centre by the radii to the contact points are **supplementary** (they sum to 180 degrees).

If angle ASB = alpha, then angle AOB = 180 - alpha.

## Chapter Summary

1. A **tangent** to a circle is a line that meets it at exactly one point.

2. **Theorem 10.1:** The tangent at any point of a circle is **perpendicular** to the radius at the point of contact.

3. **Theorem 10.2:** Tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are **equal** in length.

4. From a point **inside** a circle, zero tangents can be drawn. From a point **on** the circle, exactly one. From a point **outside**, exactly two.
