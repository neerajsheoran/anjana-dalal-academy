## Introduction

In Class 9, you learnt how to organise data into frequency distributions, draw histograms and frequency polygons, and compute the mean, median, and mode of ungrouped data.

This chapter extends those ideas to **grouped data**. You will learn:

- Three techniques to calculate the **mean** of grouped data
- How to determine the **mode** of grouped data
- How to determine the **median** of grouped data
- How to construct **cumulative frequency curves (ogives)**

These measures are collectively known as **measures of central tendency** -- they capture the "typical" or "central" value in a dataset.

---

## Mean of Grouped Data

The **mean** (or arithmetic average) is computed by dividing the sum of all observations by the total number of observations.

For grouped data, since individual values are unknown, we use the **class mark** (midpoint of each class interval) to represent all values in that class.

**Class mark = (Upper limit + Lower limit) / 2**

### Method 1: Direct Method

**Formula:**

**Mean = (Sum of f_i x x_i) / (Sum of f_i)**

where f_i is the frequency and x_i is the class mark of each class.

### Worked Example

Calculate the mean of the following distribution:

| Class | Frequency (f_i) | Class mark (x_i) | f_i x x_i |
|-------|-----------------|-------------------|-----------|
| 5-15 | 4 | 10 | 40 |
| 15-25 | 5 | 20 | 100 |
| 25-35 | 10 | 30 | 300 |
| 35-45 | 8 | 40 | 320 |
| 45-55 | 6 | 50 | 300 |
| 55-65 | 2 | 60 | 120 |
| **Total** | **35** | | **1180** |

Mean = 1180 / 35 = **33.71** (approx.)

### Method 2: Assumed Mean Method

When the class marks and frequencies are large, arithmetic gets cumbersome. We simplify by selecting an **assumed mean** (a), typically a class mark near the centre of the distribution.

**Formula:**

**Mean = a + (Sum of f_i x d_i) / (Sum of f_i)**

where **d_i = x_i - a** (the deviation of each class mark from the assumed mean)

This method shrinks the numbers you have to multiply.

### Method 3: Step-Deviation Method

We go one step further by dividing each deviation by the class width h.

**Formula:**

**Mean = a + [(Sum of f_i x u_i) / (Sum of f_i)] x h**

where **u_i = (x_i - a) / h** and h is the class width.

> **Core insight:** All three methods produce the **identical answer**. The assumed mean and step-deviation methods simply make the computation lighter when numbers are large.

## Mode of Grouped Data

The **mode** is the value that appears most frequently. For grouped data, the **modal class** is the class interval with the highest frequency.

### Formula

**Mode = l + [(f1 - f0) / (2f1 - f0 - f2)] x h**

where:
- **l** = lower boundary of the modal class
- **f1** = frequency of the modal class
- **f0** = frequency of the class **before** the modal class
- **f2** = frequency of the class **after** the modal class
- **h** = class width

> **Core insight:** The mode formula fine-tunes the lower boundary of the modal class by considering how much the modal frequency dominates its neighbouring frequencies.

### Worked Example

Determine the mode:

| Class | 5-15 | 15-25 | 25-35 | 35-45 | 45-55 |
|-------|------|-------|-------|-------|-------|
| Frequency | 6 | 14 | **26** | 24 | 10 |

The modal class is **25-35** (highest frequency = 26).

- l = 25, f1 = 26, f0 = 14, f2 = 24, h = 10

Mode = 25 + [(26 - 14) / (2 x 26 - 14 - 24)] x 10
= 25 + [12 / (52 - 38)] x 10
= 25 + [12/14] x 10
= 25 + 8.57
= **33.57** (approx.)

### When Is Mode Most Useful?

Mode is the right measure when you need the **most common** value. For instance:
- The most popular shoe size at a Bata store
- The most frequently ordered dish at a restaurant
- The most common salary bracket in a company

## Median of Grouped Data

The **median** is the value that splits the data into two equal halves when arranged in order.

### Formula

**Median = l + [(n/2 - cf) / f] x h**

where:
- **l** = lower boundary of the median class
- **n** = total frequency
- **cf** = cumulative frequency of the class **before** the median class
- **f** = frequency of the median class
- **h** = class width

### Identifying the Median Class

1. Compute **n/2**
2. Build the **cumulative frequency** column
3. The class whose cumulative frequency **first exceeds** n/2 is the **median class**

### Worked Example

Find the median:

| Class | Frequency | Cumulative Frequency |
|-------|-----------|---------------------|
| 10-20 | 4 | 4 |
| 20-30 | 6 | 10 |
| 30-40 | 10 | 20 |
| 40-50 | 14 | 34 |
| 50-60 | 8 | 42 |
| 60-70 | 3 | 45 |

Total n = 45, so n/2 = 22.5.

The cumulative frequency that first exceeds 22.5 is **34** (for class 40-50). So the median class is **40-50**.

- l = 40, n/2 = 22.5, cf = 20, f = 14, h = 10

Median = 40 + [(22.5 - 20) / 14] x 10 = 40 + [2.5/14] x 10 = 40 + 1.79 = **41.79** (approx.)

## Relationship Between Mean, Median, and Mode

For distributions that are moderately asymmetric (skewed), an empirical relationship holds:

**3 Median = Mode + 2 Mean**

This approximation lets you estimate one measure from the other two.

## Cumulative Frequency and Ogives

### What Is Cumulative Frequency?

The **cumulative frequency** of a class is the running total of frequencies up to and including that class.

- **Less than type:** Cumulative frequency grows as classes increase
- **More than type:** Cumulative frequency shrinks as classes increase

### What Is an Ogive?

An **ogive** (pronounced "oh-jive") is the graph of cumulative frequency plotted as a smooth curve.

### Two Types of Ogives

![Graph showing less than ogive (rising curve) and more than ogive (falling curve) intersecting at the median point](less-than-more-than-ogive-median.png)

**1. Less Than Ogive:**
- X-axis: upper class boundaries
- Y-axis: cumulative frequencies (less than type)
- Join the points with a smooth rising curve

**2. More Than Ogive:**
- X-axis: lower class boundaries
- Y-axis: cumulative frequencies (more than type)
- Join the points with a smooth falling curve

### Finding Median from Ogives

**Approach 1:** Using one ogive
- Draw the "less than" ogive
- Mark n/2 on the y-axis
- Draw a horizontal line to meet the curve
- Drop a perpendicular to the x-axis
- The x-value at the foot is the **median**

**Approach 2:** Using both ogives
- Plot both ogives on the same axes
- They intersect at a single point
- The x-coordinate of this intersection is the **median**

> **Core insight:** The median can be read off directly from ogive graphs, providing a powerful visual alternative to the formula.

## Comparing Mean, Median, and Mode

| Measure | What It Represents | Best Used When |
|---------|-------------------|----------------|
| **Mean** | The arithmetic average | All values matter equally and there are no extreme outliers |
| **Median** | The middle value | Extreme values (outliers) are present that would distort the mean |
| **Mode** | The most frequent value | You want the most common or popular value |

---

## Important Notes for Board Exams

1. **Class intervals must be continuous** before applying these formulas. If they are not (e.g., 1-10, 11-20), adjust by 0.5 to make them continuous (0.5-10.5, 10.5-20.5).

2. The three methods for mean always give the **same result** -- pick whichever the question specifies or whichever feels easiest.

3. For **mode**, correctly identify the modal class (highest frequency).

4. For **median**, carefully build the cumulative frequency column before locating the median class.

5. The relationship **3 Median = Mode + 2 Mean** is an approximation, not an exact identity.
