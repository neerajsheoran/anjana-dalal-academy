## Introduction

Back in Class 9, you explored **congruence** -- when two shapes are identical in both shape and size. Now, imagine a rangoli pattern drawn in two different sizes on the floor -- the smaller one and the larger one look the same, but one is bigger. This is the idea behind **similarity**: same shape, possibly different size.

Have you ever wondered how architects at an Indian firm create a tiny model of a 30-storey apartment building? Or how cartographers fit the entire map of Rajasthan onto a single page? They rely on the mathematical principle of **similarity of figures** -- keeping proportions intact while changing the scale.

---

## Similar Figures

Two figures are **similar** if they share the same shape, even though their sizes may differ.

### Everyday Examples of Similarity

- All **circles** are similar -- a coin and a dinner plate are both round
- All **squares** are similar -- a chess board tile and a carrom board are both square-shaped
- All **equilateral triangles** are similar -- every equilateral triangle has the same set of angles (60 degrees each)

> **Key idea:** Every congruent pair is also similar, but similarity does not guarantee congruence. Congruence is simply similarity with an added condition -- that the size must also match.

### When Do Two Polygons Qualify as Similar?

Two polygons with the same number of sides are similar when both of these hold:
1. Each pair of **corresponding angles is equal**, AND
2. Each pair of **corresponding sides is in the same ratio** (i.e., proportional)

Both conditions are necessary. Consider:
- A **rectangle** and a **square** both have 90-degree angles at every corner, but their side ratios differ -- so they are NOT similar
- A **square** and a **rhombus** both have all four sides equal, but a rhombus can have non-right angles -- so they are NOT similar

**Special note about triangles:** Triangles are unique among polygons. For triangles, satisfying just one of the two conditions automatically ensures the other holds as well.

## Basic Proportionality Theorem (Thales' Theorem)

This foundational result connects parallel lines with proportional division of triangle sides.

**Theorem (BPT):** When a line is drawn parallel to one side of a triangle, it cuts the remaining two sides in an equal ratio.

Suppose in triangle PQR, a line ST is drawn parallel to QR, with S on PQ and T on PR. Then:

![Triangle ABC with line DE parallel to BC, where D is on AB and E is on AC, showing proportional segments AD, DB, AE, EC](basic-proportionality-theorem-diagram.png)

**PS/SQ = PT/TR**

### Outline of the Proof

1. In triangle PQR, draw ST parallel to QR
2. Connect QT and RS
3. Construct TN perpendicular to PQ and SM perpendicular to PR
4. Observe that triangles QST and RST share the same base ST and lie between the same pair of parallels ST and QR, so their areas are equal
5. Express Area(PST)/Area(QST) = PS/SQ and Area(PST)/Area(RST) = PT/TR
6. Since Area(QST) = Area(RST), we conclude **PS/SQ = PT/TR**

### Converse of BPT

If a line cuts two sides of a triangle so that the resulting segments are in the **same ratio**, then that line must be **parallel** to the third side.

> **Key idea:** Whenever you spot a line parallel to one side of a triangle in a problem, BPT should be your first instinct. Conversely, if you are given equal ratios of segments on two sides, conclude that the dividing line is parallel to the third side.

### Worked Example

In triangle PQR, line ST is parallel to QR with S on PQ and T on PR. Given PS = 6, SQ = 9, and PT = 10, determine TR.

By BPT: PS/SQ = PT/TR
6/9 = 10/TR
TR = 10 x 9/6 = **15**

## Similarity of Triangles

Triangles enjoy a privileged position among polygons -- checking similarity for triangles requires fewer conditions than for other shapes.

### Criteria for Similarity of Triangles

#### 1. AAA Similarity Criterion

If every pair of corresponding angles in two triangles is equal, the triangles are similar.

Since the three angles of any triangle always add up to 180 degrees, matching two pairs of angles forces the third pair to match as well. This gives us:

#### 2. AA Similarity Criterion

![Two triangles with corresponding angles marked equal, showing similar triangles with proportional sides](aa-similarity-criterion-diagram.png)

If two angles in one triangle match two angles in another triangle, the triangles are similar.

> **Key idea:** AA is the go-to similarity test in most problems. Establishing just two equal angle pairs is enough to declare similarity!

#### 3. SSS Similarity Criterion

If the three pairs of corresponding sides in two triangles are in the same ratio, the triangles are similar.

In other words, if PQ/XY = QR/YZ = RP/ZX, then triangle PQR ~ triangle XYZ.

#### 4. SAS Similarity Criterion

If one pair of corresponding angles is equal and the two pairs of sides that form those angles are in the same ratio, the triangles are similar.

In other words, if angle P = angle X and PQ/XY = PR/XZ, then triangle PQR ~ triangle XYZ.

---

## Writing Similarity Statements Correctly

When we write **triangle PQR ~ triangle XYZ**, the vertex order carries meaning! It tells us exactly which vertices correspond:

- P corresponds to X
- Q corresponds to Y
- R corresponds to Z

This means:
- Angle P = Angle X, Angle Q = Angle Y, Angle R = Angle Z
- PQ/XY = QR/YZ = RP/ZX

## Worked Examples on Similarity

### Example 1: Applying the AA Criterion

In triangles PQR and LMN, angle P = angle L = 45 degrees and angle Q = angle M = 75 degrees. Are they similar?

Two pairs of corresponding angles are equal, so by **AA criterion**, triangle PQR ~ triangle LMN.

Also, angle R = angle N = 180 - 45 - 75 = 60 degrees.

### Example 2: The Street Light and Shadow

Priya is 1.2 m tall and walks away from a street lamp that stands 4.8 m high, at a speed of 0.8 m/s. What is the length of her shadow after 5 seconds?

**Step 1:** Distance Priya has walked = 0.8 x 5 = 4 m

**Step 2:** Let shadow length = s metres.

**Step 3:** The triangle formed by the street lamp and the full extent of the shadow is similar to the triangle formed by Priya and her shadow (AA criterion -- both contain a right angle and share the angle at the tip of the shadow).

**Step 4:** (4 + s)/s = 4.8/1.2 = 4

**Step 5:** 4 + s = 4s, so 3s = 4, giving **s = 4/3 m (approximately 1.33 m)**

### Example 3: The Pole and the Minaret Shadow

A vertical bamboo pole 4 m tall casts a shadow of 3 m on the ground. At the same instant, a nearby minaret casts a shadow of 21 m. Calculate the height of the minaret.

The sun's rays arrive at the same angle for both objects, making the two shadow-triangles similar.

Height of minaret / 21 = 4 / 3
Height of minaret = 4 x 21 / 3 = **28 m**

## Areas of Similar Triangles

**Theorem:** The ratio of the areas of two similar triangles equals the **square of the ratio** of their corresponding sides.

If triangle PQR ~ triangle XYZ, then:

**Area(PQR) / Area(XYZ) = (PQ/XY)^2 = (QR/YZ)^2 = (RP/ZX)^2**

### Worked Example

If triangle PQR ~ triangle XYZ and PQ/XY = 4/7, find the ratio of their areas.

Area(PQR)/Area(XYZ) = (PQ/XY)^2 = (4/7)^2 = **16/49**

### This Square-of-Ratio Rule Also Holds For:

- Ratio of squares of corresponding **altitudes**
- Ratio of squares of corresponding **medians**
- Ratio of squares of corresponding **angle bisectors**

> **Key idea:** With similar triangles, whenever you move from a linear measurement (sides, altitudes, medians) to area, remember to square the ratio. Sides in ratio 3:5 give areas in ratio 9:25.

## Pythagoras Theorem

One of the most celebrated results in all of mathematics, known to civilisations across the world for thousands of years.

**Theorem:** In any right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.

![Right triangle ABC with squares drawn on each side, showing that the area of the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the areas of the squares on the other two sides](pythagoras-theorem-visual-proof.png)

If triangle PQR has a right angle at Q, then:

**PR^2 = PQ^2 + QR^2**

### Proof Using Similarity

1. Draw QS perpendicular to PR (where angle Q = 90 degrees)
2. In triangle PQS and triangle PQR:
   - Angle P is common
   - Angle PSQ = Angle PQR = 90 degrees
   - So triangle PQS ~ triangle PQR (AA)
   - This gives: PS/PQ = PQ/PR, i.e., **PQ^2 = PS x PR** ... (1)

3. Similarly, triangle QSR ~ triangle PQR
   - This gives: SR/QR = QR/PR, i.e., **QR^2 = SR x PR** ... (2)

4. Adding (1) and (2):
   PQ^2 + QR^2 = PS x PR + SR x PR = PR(PS + SR) = PR x PR = **PR^2**

### Converse of Pythagoras Theorem

If in a triangle, the square of one side equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides, then the angle opposite that side is a **right angle**.

That is, if PR^2 = PQ^2 + QR^2, then angle Q = 90 degrees.

> **Key idea:** The Pythagoras theorem applies exclusively to right-angled triangles. Its converse provides a quick test: given three side lengths, check whether the square of the longest side equals the sum of the squares of the other two.

### Worked Example: Checking for a Right Triangle

A triangle has sides measuring 8 cm, 15 cm, and 17 cm. Is it right-angled?

Check: 17^2 = 289. And 8^2 + 15^2 = 64 + 225 = 289.

Since 17^2 = 8^2 + 15^2, the converse of Pythagoras theorem confirms the triangle is right-angled, with the right angle opposite the 17 cm side.

### Worked Example: The Leaning Ladder

A painter places a 13 m ladder against a wall. The top of the ladder touches the wall at a point 12 m above the ground. How far from the wall is the foot of the ladder?

Let the distance from wall = d.

By Pythagoras theorem: 13^2 = 12^2 + d^2
169 = 144 + d^2
d^2 = 25
**d = 5 m**

## Additional Results Connected to Pythagoras Theorem

1. In an **obtuse-angled triangle** PQR with angle Q obtuse, if PS is perpendicular to QR produced:
   PR^2 = PQ^2 + QR^2 + 2 x QR x QS

2. In an **acute-angled triangle** PQR, if PS is perpendicular to QR:
   PR^2 = PQ^2 + QR^2 - 2 x QR x QS

## Chapter Summary

1. Two figures with the same shape but not necessarily the same size are called **similar** figures.

2. All congruent figures are similar, but the reverse is not always true.

3. Two polygons are similar if (i) corresponding angles are equal AND (ii) corresponding sides are proportional.

4. **Basic Proportionality Theorem (BPT):** A line parallel to one side of a triangle divides the other two sides in equal ratio.

5. **Converse of BPT:** A line that divides two sides of a triangle proportionally is parallel to the third side.

6. **Similarity criteria for triangles:**
   - **AA:** Two pairs of equal angles
   - **SSS:** All three pairs of sides in the same ratio
   - **SAS:** One pair of equal angles with the including sides proportional

7. The ratio of **areas** of similar triangles equals the **square** of the ratio of their corresponding sides.

8. **Pythagoras Theorem:** In a right triangle, hypotenuse^2 = sum of squares of the other two sides.

9. **Converse of Pythagoras Theorem:** If the square of one side equals the sum of squares of the other two, the triangle has a right angle.
