## Graphical Interpretation

**Child:** Maa, how does drawing two lines on a graph help us solve equations?

**Mother:** Every linear equation with two variables becomes a straight line on the coordinate plane. Two equations give you two lines. If they cross at a point, the coordinates of that crossing point are the solution. If the two lines are actually the same line, then every point on that line solves both equations -- infinitely many solutions. If they are parallel and never meet, there is no solution at all.

**Child:** So "consistent" just means the lines meet somewhere?

**Mother:** Yes. Consistent means at least one solution exists -- either a single intersection or infinitely many overlapping points. Inconsistent means no solution -- parallel lines. The ratio test comparing a1/a2, b1/b2, and c1/c2 lets you classify the system without touching graph paper.

**Child:** That ratio test sounds very useful for MCQ-type questions.

**Mother:** Absolutely. In CBSE board objective questions, this test gives you the answer in seconds. If a1/a2 does not equal b1/b2, the lines cross. If all three ratios are equal, the lines overlap. If the first two ratios match but the third does not, the lines are parallel. Practise this until it becomes automatic.

## Substitution Method

**Child:** Between substitution and elimination, which should I prefer during the exam?

**Mother:** It depends on the structure of the equations. Substitution is ideal when one variable is already isolated or nearly isolated -- for instance, if one equation says x equals 11 minus 4y, just plug that into the other equation. With elimination, you are better off when neither variable separates easily, but the coefficients are convenient for matching.

**Child:** What does it mean if substitution gives me something like 0 equals 0?

**Mother:** That means both equations are secretly the same line -- infinitely many solutions exist. If you instead get something impossible like 7 equals 0, then no solution exists. Write your conclusion clearly: "The system has infinitely many solutions" or "The system is inconsistent."

**Child:** Maa, the age and digit word problems always confuse me. How do I set them up?

**Mother:** The key is disciplined translation from Hindi or English into algebra. For age problems, define variables as present ages and use phrases like "five years ago" by subtracting 5. For digit problems, if the tens digit is x and the units digit is y, the number is 10x plus y. The reversed number is 10y plus x. Write each sentence as a separate equation. Government competitive exams like SSC and banking tests use exactly the same kind of logical reasoning.

## Elimination Method

**Child:** In elimination, how do I choose which variable to get rid of?

**Mother:** Look at the coefficients. If one variable already has matching or easily matchable coefficients, eliminate that one. For instance, if one equation has 4x and the other has 8x, just multiply the first by 2 and subtract. The goal is to keep the arithmetic simple.

**Child:** The savings problem where each friend saves Rs 1500 -- that was a nice real-life application.

**Mother:** Yes, and ratio-based problems like that are favourites in CBSE boards. The crucial step is setting up the equations correctly from the given ratios. If incomes are in ratio 7 to 5, write them as 7x and 5x, not as 7 and 5. Similarly for expenditures. The savings condition then gives you two equations in two unknowns.

## Equations Reducible to Linear Form

**Child:** Some questions have 1/x and 1/y in them. Those are not linear, are they?

**Mother:** Not in x and y, but if you let u equal 1/x and v equal 1/y, they become linear in u and v. Solve the new system for u and v, then flip back: x equals 1/u and y equals 1/v. This substitution trick appears in JEE problems as well, so mastering it now gives you a head start.

**Child:** Are there other substitutions I should watch for?

**Mother:** Yes. Sometimes the equations involve expressions like 1/(x + y) or 1/(x - y). Treat each such expression as a brand new variable. CBSE has asked questions of this type in multiple previous years -- they are not rare.

## Board Exam Strategy

**Child:** How many marks does this chapter usually carry?

**Mother:** Typically 3 to 5 marks. You can expect either a word problem where you form and solve equations, or a graphical question asking you to plot two lines and identify the solution. For graphical questions, use a sharp pencil, choose a sensible scale, and plot at least three points per line for accuracy.

**Child:** What are the common mistakes I should avoid?

**Mother:** Three frequent errors. First, sign mistakes when subtracting equations during elimination -- be extra careful with negatives. Second, forgetting to verify your solution by plugging it back into both original equations -- this verification step earns you marks. Third, in word problems, not stating what your variables represent. Always begin with "Let x represent..." and "Let y represent..." CBSE examiners look for this.

## Connecting to Higher Mathematics

**Child:** Systems of equations seem very fundamental. Do they get more complicated later?

**Mother:** In Class 12, you will solve systems of three equations in three unknowns using matrices and determinants. In engineering and data science, systems of thousands or even millions of equations are solved by computers -- this branch is called linear algebra, and it powers everything from Google Search to weather forecasting. The two-variable systems you master now are the foundation for all of that.

**Child:** India's technology companies must use these methods regularly?

**Mother:** Every single day. Machine learning engineers at companies like Flipkart, Ola, and Zomato solve enormous systems of linear equations to build recommendation engines and route optimisation algorithms. The abstract skills of formulating and solving equations translate directly into real-world technological capability. Build a strong foundation now and the applications will follow naturally.
