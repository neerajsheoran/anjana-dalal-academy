## What Makes an Equation Quadratic

**Child:** Maa, sometimes an equation looks cubic but turns out to be quadratic after simplification. How should I handle that in the exam?

**Mother:** Always simplify first. Expand every bracket, gather like terms, and check which is the highest power remaining. If x^3 terms cancel out and x^2 survives as the highest power, the equation is quadratic. For instance, (x + 3)^3 = x^3 + 9 looks cubic at first glance, but when you expand and cancel x^3 from both sides, you get a clean quadratic.

**Child:** And if even the x^2 terms cancel?

**Mother:** Then the equation is not quadratic -- it becomes linear. The essential condition is that the coefficient of x^2 must be non-zero. Your textbook states this explicitly: a must not equal zero in ax^2 + bx + c = 0.

**Child:** Our teacher mentioned that Brahmagupta worked on quadratic equations in ancient India. Is that true?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Brahmagupta, who lived in Rajasthan in the 7th century, gave systematic rules for solving quadratic equations. Later, Sridharacharya in the 11th century derived what we now call the quadratic formula. India's contributions to algebra are extraordinary and often underappreciated.

## Solving by Factorisation

**Child:** When should I use factorisation and when should I use the quadratic formula?

**Mother:** If the question specifically says "solve by factorisation," you must use that method. Otherwise, either approach works. Factorisation is quicker when the roots are nice rational numbers. The formula is reliable when the roots are messy or irrational.

**Child:** For splitting the middle term, I need two numbers whose product is ac and sum is b. What if ac turns out to be negative?

**Mother:** Then one number will be positive and the other negative. For example, in 3x^2 - 7x + 2 = 0, ac equals 6 and b equals -7. You need product 6 and sum -7 -- those are -1 and -6. After doing 25-30 practice problems, spotting these pairs becomes almost instant.

**Child:** And in word problems, I should reject negative roots when the variable represents something physical?

**Mother:** Yes, with a clear written statement. Write "Since breadth cannot be negative, x = -12.5 is rejected." That explicit rejection is part of the marking scheme -- skipping it costs you marks.

## The Quadratic Formula and Discriminant

**Child:** Maa, I think of the discriminant D = b^2 - 4ac like a weather forecast. It tells you what to expect before you start solving.

**Mother:** That is a nice way to think about it. When D is positive, you get two distinct real roots -- clear skies with two paths. When D is zero, both roots are the same -- calm and steady. When D is negative, no real roots exist -- a storm, meaning the problem has no real solution.

**Child:** What about the question "find the value of k for equal roots"?

**Mother:** Set D equal to zero and solve for k. This is a very standard 2-3 mark question. But be careful -- sometimes one of the k values makes the x^2 coefficient zero, which would destroy the quadratic nature of the equation. You must reject that value.

**Child:** Like kx(x - 3) + 9 = 0. If k equals zero, it is not quadratic anymore.

**Mother:** Exactly. Always verify that your answer keeps the equation genuinely quadratic. This subtle check is what separates a good answer from a perfect one.

## Word Problems

**Child:** The word problems in this chapter feel tougher than in Chapter 3.

**Mother:** That is because you are now setting up quadratic equations rather than linear ones, which requires more careful thinking. The common types are: area problems where length times breadth gives a quadratic, age problems where the product of ages leads to a quadratic, consecutive number problems, and speed-distance-time problems where the relationship produces a quadratic.

**Child:** The bus problem where it takes 3 hours less at 10 km/h faster -- that was challenging.

**Mother:** The approach is always the same. Define the unknown as x, express the given conditions as equations, simplify to standard form, solve, and interpret. For the bus problem, time equals distance over speed. The condition "3 hours less" translates to 360/x minus 360/(x + 10) equals 3. Cross-multiply and simplify to get the quadratic. Practice this translation step until it feels natural.

## Board Exam Tips

**Child:** How much does this chapter carry in the board exam?

**Mother:** Typically 4-5 marks. Expect one word problem requiring you to form and solve the equation, and one nature-of-roots or find-k question. The word problem usually requires both setting up the equation and solving it completely.

**Child:** What mistakes should I be careful about?

**Mother:** Four things. First, always write the equation in standard form before solving -- ax^2 + bx + c = 0 with everything on one side. Second, when applying the formula, watch the sign of b carefully -- if b is -8, then -b is +8. Third, always verify your roots by substituting them back into the original equation. Fourth, in word problems, define the variable clearly at the start and state the final answer in words. Do not just write "x = 15." Write "The breadth of the field is 15 metres."

**Child:** Is the quadratic equation used in real-life engineering?

**Mother:** Constantly. The trajectory of a ball in a cricket match follows a parabolic path described by a quadratic equation. Bridge arches, satellite dish antennas, and headlight reflectors are all parabolic shapes. Even business analysts use quadratic equations for profit maximisation. When ISRO calculates a rocket's trajectory, quadratic and higher-degree equations are central to the computation. What you study in this chapter is genuinely the mathematical language that engineers and scientists use daily.
