## Introduction

In Class 9, you explored **experimental (empirical) probability** -- you physically performed experiments (like rolling a die 50 times) and recorded what happened. The probability was based on actual observations.

In this chapter, you study **theoretical (classical) probability** -- probability computed through **logical reasoning** rather than actual experiments. This is the approach used in board exams, competitive exams, and most real-world applications.

The formal study of probability has a fascinating history. The Italian mathematician **Gerolamo Cardano** (16th century) wrote one of the earliest treatises on it. Later, **Pierre-Simon Laplace** (1749-1827) developed much of the classical theory we use today.

---

## Basic Concepts

### Experiment and Outcomes

A **random experiment** is an action whose result cannot be predicted with certainty. Each possible result is called an **outcome**.

**Examples:**
- Spinning a coin: outcomes are Head (H) and Tail (T)
- Rolling a die: outcomes are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6
- Picking a card from a shuffled pack: outcomes are any of the 52 cards

### Equally Likely Outcomes

Outcomes are **equally likely** when no single outcome is favoured over another.

- A balanced coin: Head and Tail are equally likely
- A fair die: each face 1 through 6 is equally likely
- A bag with 5 green and 2 yellow marbles: drawing green is more likely than yellow -- these outcomes are **not** equally likely at the individual marble level, but each marble is equally likely

> **Core insight:** The probability formula taught in this chapter works ONLY when all outcomes are equally likely.

### Event

An **event** is any collection of outcomes.

- "Rolling an odd number on a die" is an event (outcomes 1, 3, 5)
- "Getting tails on a coin" is an event (outcome T)

An **elementary event** consists of exactly one outcome. The probabilities of all elementary events always sum to **1**.

---

## The Probability Formula

The **theoretical probability** of an event E is:

**P(E) = Number of outcomes favourable to E / Total number of equally likely outcomes**

This definition assumes all outcomes are equally likely.

> **Core insight:** Probability is a ratio -- favourable outcomes divided by total outcomes. It measures how likely an event is.

---

## Fundamental Rules of Probability

### Rule 1: Probability Always Lies Between 0 and 1

**0 <= P(E) <= 1** for every event E.

- P(E) = 0 means the event is **impossible** (can never occur)
- P(E) = 1 means the event is **certain** (will always occur)
- Values between 0 and 1 indicate varying degrees of likelihood

### Rule 2: Complementary Events

**P(E) + P(not E) = 1**

which gives: **P(not E) = 1 - P(E)**

"Not E" (denoted E-bar) is the **complement** of E -- it represents all outcomes that are not in E.

**Example:** If the probability that a train arrives on time is 0.85, the probability it is late is 1 - 0.85 = **0.15**.

### Rule 3: Sum of Elementary Event Probabilities

The probabilities of all elementary events always add up to **1**.

## Worked Examples: Coins

### Example 1: Flipping One Coin

Possible outcomes: {H, T}. Total = 2.

- P(Head) = 1/2
- P(Tail) = 1/2
- Verification: 1/2 + 1/2 = 1

### Example 2: Flipping Two Coins

Possible outcomes: {HH, HT, TH, TT}. Total = 4.

- P(at least one tail) = 3/4 (favourable: HT, TH, TT)
- P(exactly one tail) = 2/4 = 1/2 (favourable: HT, TH)
- P(no tail) = 1/4 (favourable: HH)
- P(both tails) = 1/4 (favourable: TT)

### Example 3: Flipping Three Coins

Possible outcomes: {HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT}. Total = 8.

- P(all identical) = 2/8 = 1/4 (favourable: HHH, TTT)
- P(not all identical) = 1 - 1/4 = **3/4**

## Worked Examples: Dice

### Single Die

Possible outcomes: {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. Total = 6.

- P(rolling a 3) = 1/6
- P(even number) = 3/6 = 1/2 (favourable: 2, 4, 6)
- P(prime number) = 3/6 = 1/2 (favourable: 2, 3, 5)
- P(number above 3) = 3/6 = 1/2 (favourable: 4, 5, 6)
- P(number below 7) = 6/6 = **1** (certain event)
- P(rolling a 9) = 0/6 = **0** (impossible event)

### Two Dice

When two dice are rolled together, each die shows 1-6. Total outcomes = 6 x 6 = **36**.

![6 by 6 grid showing all 36 outcomes when two dice are thrown, with highlighted cells for sum equals 7](two-dice-sample-space-grid.png)

The 36 outcomes as ordered pairs (die1, die2):
(1,1), (1,2), ..., (1,6), (2,1), (2,2), ..., (6,6)

**Frequently asked probabilities:**

| Event | Favourable outcomes | Count | Probability |
|-------|-------------------|-------|-------------|
| Sum = 7 | (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1) | 6 | 6/36 = 1/6 |
| Sum = 9 | (3,6), (4,5), (5,4), (6,3) | 4 | 4/36 = 1/9 |
| Sum = 2 | (1,1) | 1 | 1/36 |
| Sum = 12 | (6,6) | 1 | 1/36 |
| Sum = 14 | none | 0 | 0 (impossible) |
| Same number on both | (1,1), (2,2), ..., (6,6) | 6 | 6/36 = 1/6 |

## Worked Examples: Balls and Marbles

### Example: Bag of Coloured Balls

A pouch holds 5 green, 3 orange, and 2 white balls. One ball is drawn at random.

Total = 5 + 3 + 2 = 10

- P(green) = 5/10 = 1/2
- P(orange) = 3/10
- P(white) = 2/10 = 1/5

Verification: 1/2 + 3/10 + 1/5 = 5/10 + 3/10 + 2/10 = 10/10 = 1

### Example: Faulty Light Bulbs

A box contains 15 defective bulbs mixed with 135 working bulbs. One is drawn at random.

Total = 15 + 135 = 150

- P(working bulb) = 135/150 = **9/10**
- P(defective bulb) = 15/150 = 1/10

## Worked Examples: Real-Life Situations

### Birthday Problem

Two classmates Arun and Meera -- what is the probability they share the same birthday?

Assuming 365 days in a year:
- Total possibilities for Meera's birthday = 365
- Favourable outcomes for different birthday = 364

P(different birthdays) = 364/365
P(same birthday) = 1 - 364/365 = **1/365**

### Drawing Without Replacement

A bag has 25 balls, 5 of which are red.

First draw: P(red) = 5/25 = 1/5

If the first ball drawn is NOT red and is NOT put back:
- Remaining = 24 balls (5 red, 19 non-red)
- P(second ball is non-red) = 19/24

### Complementary Events in Sports

Virat and Rohit play a badminton match. P(Virat wins) = 0.55.

P(Rohit wins) = 1 - 0.55 = **0.45**

(Since one player must win, these are complementary events.)

## Numbers from a Container

A jar has tokens numbered 1 to 80. One is picked at random.

- P(single-digit number) = 9/80 (numbers 1 to 9)
- P(perfect square) = 8/80 = 1/10 (squares: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64 = 8 numbers)
- P(divisible by 4) = 20/80 = 1/4 (multiples: 4, 8, 12, ..., 80 = 20 numbers)

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Listing outcomes incorrectly.** For two coins, the outcomes are HH, HT, TH, TT (4 outcomes), NOT "two heads, one of each, two tails" (3 outcomes). HT and TH are distinct!

2. **Mixing up "at least one" and "exactly one."** "At least one head" means one or more heads (HH, HT, TH). "Exactly one head" means only HT and TH.

3. **Getting a probability outside [0,1].** If your answer is negative or exceeds 1, something has gone wrong.

4. **Applying the formula when outcomes are not equally likely.** The formula only works when every outcome has the same chance.

## Chapter Summary

1. **Theoretical probability** of event E:
   P(E) = (Number of favourable outcomes) / (Total number of equally likely outcomes)

2. The probability of a **certain event** is **1**.

3. The probability of an **impossible event** is **0**.

4. For any event E: **0 <= P(E) <= 1**

5. An **elementary event** has exactly one outcome. All elementary event probabilities sum to 1.

6. **Complementary events:** P(E) + P(not E) = 1, so **P(not E) = 1 - P(E)**

7. Key outcome counts to remember:
   - 1 coin: 2 outcomes
   - 2 coins: 4 outcomes
   - 3 coins: 8 outcomes
   - 1 die: 6 outcomes
   - 2 dice: 36 outcomes
   - 1 card from a pack: 52 outcomes
