## Key Concepts

- This chapter applies trigonometric ratios to find **heights and distances** that cannot be measured directly
- **Angle of elevation**: angle from the horizontal looking **upward** to an object
- **Angle of depression**: angle from the horizontal looking **downward** to an object
- Both angles are always measured from the **horizontal**, never the vertical
- Angle of elevation from A to B = angle of depression from B to A (alternate interior angles)
- In most problems, **tan** is the most useful ratio (vertical height = opposite, horizontal distance = adjacent)
- When the observer's height is given, the angle is from the **eyes** -- add observer's height to get total height
- Two-triangle problems require two equations solved simultaneously
- Shadow problems: use the sun's altitude angle with tan to relate height and shadow length
- For two buildings: angles of depression from one rooftop to the top and bottom of another building give two right triangles

## Problem-Solving Steps

1. Draw a clear, labelled diagram
2. Identify the right triangle(s)
3. Mark known sides and angles
4. Choose the correct ratio (sin, cos, or tan)
5. Solve the equation

## Important Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Line of sight** | Straight line from observer's eye to the object |
| **Angle of elevation** | Angle between horizontal and upward line of sight |
| **Angle of depression** | Angle between horizontal and downward line of sight |

## Remember These

- Always **sketch the diagram first** -- most errors come from incorrect diagrams
- Angle of depression from the top = angle of elevation from the bottom (alternate angles with parallel horizontals)
- If the observer is 1.8 m tall and the calculated height above the eyes is h, the **total height = h + 1.8**
- In shadow-length-change problems, set up two equations using the two angles and eliminate the unknown shadow length
- When two angles are given from the same point to two different heights, subtract or use two separate triangles
