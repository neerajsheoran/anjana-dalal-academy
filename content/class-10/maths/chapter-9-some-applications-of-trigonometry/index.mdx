## Introduction

In Chapter 8, you built your toolkit of trigonometric ratios. Now it is time to put them to work! This chapter is all about measuring **heights and distances** that are impossible to reach with a ruler or tape.

How does a surveyor figure out the height of a mobile tower? How does a lighthouse keeper estimate how far a fishing boat is from shore? They use trigonometry! Every problem in this chapter boils down to spotting a right triangle, identifying the known and unknown sides, and picking the right ratio.

---

## Key Terminology

### Line of Sight

The **line of sight** is the imaginary straight line running from the observer's eye to the point being observed on an object.

### Angle of Elevation

When you tilt your gaze **upward** to see something -- the tip of a flagpole, a bird sitting on a tree -- the angle formed between the **horizontal** and your line of sight is the **angle of elevation**.

Imagine standing in a cricket ground and looking up at the stadium floodlights. The angle your eyes sweep upward from the horizontal is the angle of elevation.

### Angle of Depression

![Diagram showing angle of elevation (looking up from ground to building top) and angle of depression (looking down from building top to ground) with horizontal lines and line of sight](angle-of-elevation-depression-diagram.png)

When you look **downward** from a height -- a pilot spotting a runway, a watchman in a tower observing the gate below -- the angle between the **horizontal** and your line of sight is the **angle of depression**.

> **Key idea:** Both angles are always measured from the **horizontal**, never from the vertical. Also, the angle of elevation from point A looking up at point B is exactly equal to the angle of depression from B looking down at A (because they are alternate interior angles formed by parallel horizontal lines and the line of sight as transversal).

## Solving Heights and Distances Problems

### Step-by-Step Method

1. **Sketch a clear diagram** incorporating all given data
2. **Locate the right triangle(s)** within the diagram
3. **Label** the known sides and angles
4. **Select the appropriate trigonometric ratio** based on which sides are known and which are required
5. **Solve** the resulting equation

### Choosing the Right Ratio

| Known sides | Needed side | Use |
|-------------|------------|-----|
| Opposite and Hypotenuse | - | sin |
| Adjacent and Hypotenuse | - | cos |
| Opposite and Adjacent | - | tan |

> **Key idea:** In the majority of height-and-distance problems, **tan** is the most useful ratio, because the typical scenario involves a vertical height (opposite) and a horizontal distance (adjacent).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Height of a Temple Gopuram

Standing 20 m from the base of a temple gopuram, Meera observes the top at an angle of elevation of 60 degrees. How tall is the gopuram?

**Diagram:** Right triangle with base = 20 m, angle of elevation = 60 degrees, height = ?

**Solution:**
tan 60 = height / 20
root(3) = height / 20
Height = 20 root(3) m (approximately 34.64 m)

### Example 2: The Electrician's Ladder

An electrician needs to reach a point on a 7 m pole that is 4.2 m above the ground. His ladder makes a 60-degree angle with the ground. Find the ladder's length and its distance from the pole.

**Height to reach:** 4.2 m

**Finding ladder length:**
sin 60 = 4.2 / ladder length
root(3)/2 = 4.2 / ladder length
Ladder length = 4.2 x 2 / root(3) = 8.4 / root(3) = **4.85 m** (approx.)

**Finding distance from pole:**
tan 60 = 4.2 / distance
root(3) = 4.2 / distance
Distance = 4.2 / root(3) = **2.42 m** (approx.)

### Example 3: Observer Height Matters

Suresh is 1.8 m tall and stands 20.2 m away from a clock tower. The angle of elevation of the top of the tower from his eyes is 45 degrees. Find the tower's height.

**Crucial detail:** The angle is measured from Suresh's **eyes**, not from the ground!

tan 45 = height above eyes / 20.2
1 = height above eyes / 20.2
Height above eyes = 20.2 m

Total tower height = 20.2 + 1.8 = **22 m**

### Example 4: Two Angles of Elevation (Antenna on a Building)

![Right triangle diagram showing a building with a flagstaff on top, observer at point P, with two angles of elevation 30 degrees to the building top and 45 degrees to the flag top](two-angles-of-elevation-flag-building.png)

From a point on the ground, the angle of elevation of the top of a 12 m building is 30 degrees. An antenna mounted on the building has an angle of elevation of 45 degrees from the same point. Find the antenna's height and the observer's distance from the building.

**Step 1:** Find distance from building.
tan 30 = 12 / distance
1/root(3) = 12 / distance
Distance = 12 root(3) = **20.78 m** (approx.)

**Step 2:** Find antenna height.
Let antenna height = h. Total height = (12 + h).
tan 45 = (12 + h) / (12 root(3))
1 = (12 + h) / (12 root(3))
12 + h = 12 root(3)
h = 12(root(3) - 1) = 12(1.732 - 1) = **8.78 m** (approx.)

## Shadow Problems

### Example 5: Changing Shadow Length

A flagpole's shadow stretches 30 m longer when the sun's altitude drops from 60 degrees to 30 degrees. Determine the flagpole's height.

Let height = h, shorter shadow = s.

**When sun's altitude is 60 degrees:**
tan 60 = h/s, so root(3) = h/s, giving h = s root(3) ... (1)

**When sun's altitude is 30 degrees:**
tan 30 = h/(s + 30), so 1/root(3) = h/(s + 30) ... (2)

Substituting h = s root(3) from (1) into (2):
s root(3) = (s + 30)/root(3)
3s = s + 30
2s = 30, so s = 15

h = 15 root(3) = **25.98 m** (approx.)

## Two Buildings / Angle of Depression Problems

### Example 6: Tall Building and Short Building

From the rooftop of a multi-storey building, the angles of depression of the top and bottom of a 6 m building are 30 degrees and 45 degrees respectively. Find the taller building's height and the gap between the two buildings.

Let the taller building's height = H, and the distance between buildings = d.

**From the bottom of the shorter building:** tan 45 = H/d, so d = H ... (1)

**From the top of the shorter building:** Height difference = (H - 6)
tan 30 = (H - 6)/d
1/root(3) = (H - 6)/d ... (2)

Substituting d = H from (1):
1/root(3) = (H - 6)/H
H = root(3)(H - 6) = root(3)H - 6 root(3)
H(root(3) - 1) = 6 root(3)
H = 6 root(3) / (root(3) - 1)

Rationalising: H = 6 root(3)(root(3) + 1) / (3 - 1) = 6(3 + root(3)) / 2 = **3(3 + root(3))** m

### Example 7: Width of a Canal

From a footbridge 4 m above a canal, the angles of depression of the two opposite banks are 30 degrees and 45 degrees. Find the canal's width.

**Left bank:** tan 30 = 4/d1, so d1 = 4 root(3) m
**Right bank:** tan 45 = 4/d2, so d2 = 4 m

Width = d1 + d2 = 4 root(3) + 4 = **4(root(3) + 1) m**

## Common Problem Types for Board Exams

### Type 1: Direct Height Calculation
Given: distance from base and angle of elevation.
Find: height using tan.

### Type 2: Two Angles from the Same Spot
Given: two objects at different heights, one observation point.
Find: individual heights or distances.

### Type 3: Walking Observer
Given: angles of elevation measured from two different positions.
Find: height or the distance walked.

### Type 4: Angle of Depression
Given: height and angle of depression.
Find: horizontal distance.

### Type 5: Two-Building Scenario
Given: angles of depression from one building to top and bottom of another.
Find: heights and distances.

## Important Tips

1. Always sketch a **clear, labelled diagram** before solving.

2. The **angle of depression** from point A equals the **angle of elevation** from the ground point to A (alternate interior angles with parallel horizontals).

3. When the observer's height is specified (e.g., "a 1.6 m tall girl"), the angle is from the **eyes**. Add the observer's height to the calculated value to get the full height.

4. Essential values to know by heart:
   - tan 30 = 1/root(3)
   - tan 45 = 1
   - tan 60 = root(3)
   - root(3) approximately equals 1.732

5. Problems with **two right triangles** need two equations solved simultaneously.

## Chapter Summary

1. The **line of sight** connects the observer's eye to the point being viewed on the object.

2. The **angle of elevation** is formed when looking upward -- measured between the horizontal and the line of sight.

3. The **angle of depression** is formed when looking downward -- measured between the horizontal and the line of sight.

4. Heights and distances are calculated using **trigonometric ratios** applied to the right triangles formed by the line of sight, horizontal, and vertical.
