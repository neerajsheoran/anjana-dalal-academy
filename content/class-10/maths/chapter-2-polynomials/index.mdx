## Introduction

In Class 9, you encountered polynomials and learnt to classify them by degree. Let us quickly refresh the essentials before diving into the new ideas.

The **degree** of a polynomial p(x) is the highest power of x that appears in it. For instance:

- **7x - 5** has degree 1
- **3y^2 + y - 8** has degree 2
- **x^3 - 2x^2 + 4x + 1** has degree 3

Expressions like 1/(x - 3) or root(x + 5) are **not** polynomials because the variable appears in the denominator or under a root.

### Classifying Polynomials by Degree

| Degree | Name | Standard Form | Examples |
|--------|------|---------------|----------|
| 1 | Linear | ax + b | 5x + 1, -3x + 7 |
| 2 | Quadratic | ax^2 + bx + c | 3x^2 - 2x + 4, -v^2 + 6 |
| 3 | Cubic | ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d | x^3 + 2x - 7, 4x^3 |

The term **quadratic** traces its origin to the Latin word for "square."

In this chapter, the central theme is the deep connection between a polynomial's **zeroes** and its **coefficients**, along with the geometric picture of what zeroes look like on a graph.

## Zeroes of a Polynomial

When you substitute a number k into a polynomial p(x), the result p(k) is called the **value of p(x) at x = k**.

If **p(k) = 0**, we say k is a **zero** (or root) of the polynomial.

### Example: Locating the Zeroes

Take p(x) = x^2 - 5x + 6.

- p(2) = 4 - 10 + 6 = **0**
- p(3) = 9 - 15 + 6 = **0**

Both 2 and 3 make the polynomial vanish, so the zeroes are **2 and 3**.

### Zero of a Linear Polynomial

For a linear polynomial p(x) = ax + b, there is exactly one zero:

**k = -b/a = -(Constant term) / (Coefficient of x)**

For instance, the zero of 5x - 15 is -(-15)/5 = 3.

## Geometrical Meaning of Zeroes

### For Linear Polynomials

The graph of y = ax + b is a **straight line**. It crosses the x-axis at precisely one point: (-b/a, 0). The x-coordinate of that crossing is the zero of the polynomial.

**Example:** The graph of y = 3x - 9 crosses the x-axis at (3, 0). So the zero of 3x - 9 is 3.

### For Quadratic Polynomials

The graph of y = ax^2 + bx + c is a **parabola**:

- When **a > 0**, the parabola opens **upwards** (shaped like a bowl)
- When **a < 0**, the parabola opens **downwards** (shaped like an arch)

> **Key idea:** The zeroes of a quadratic polynomial are the x-coordinates where the parabola meets the x-axis.

Three situations arise:

**Case 1: Two distinct zeroes** -- The parabola cuts the x-axis at two separate points. The polynomial has two different zeroes.

**Case 2: One repeated zero** -- The parabola just grazes the x-axis at one point. The polynomial has a single zero counted twice.

**Case 3: No real zeroes** -- The parabola floats entirely above or below the x-axis. The polynomial has no real zeroes.

### For Cubic Polynomials

A cubic polynomial's graph can cross the x-axis at **up to 3 points**.

**Example:** For y = x^3 - 9x:

- At x = -3: y = -27 + 27 = 0
- At x = 0: y = 0
- At x = 3: y = 27 - 27 = 0

The zeroes are -3, 0, and 3.

> **Key idea:** A polynomial of degree n can have **at most n zeroes**. Linear: at most 1. Quadratic: at most 2. Cubic: at most 3. Degree n: at most n.

## Relationship Between Zeroes and Coefficients of a Quadratic Polynomial

This is the cornerstone topic of the chapter and a favourite in board examinations.

If **alpha** and **beta** are the zeroes of the quadratic polynomial **ax^2 + bx + c** (a is not zero), then:

- **Sum of zeroes** = alpha + beta = **-b/a** = -(Coefficient of x) / (Coefficient of x^2)
- **Product of zeroes** = alpha x beta = **c/a** = (Constant term) / (Coefficient of x^2)

> **Key idea:** These two compact formulas connect the zeroes directly to the coefficients. They are essential for both board exams and competitive preparation.

### Where Do These Formulas Come From?

If alpha and beta are zeroes of ax^2 + bx + c, then we can write:

ax^2 + bx + c = a(x - alpha)(x - beta) = a[x^2 - (alpha + beta)x + alpha x beta]

Matching the coefficients on both sides:
- b = -a(alpha + beta), giving alpha + beta = -b/a
- c = a(alpha x beta), giving alpha x beta = c/a

## Worked Examples on Zeroes and Coefficients

### Example 1: Find zeroes and verify the relationship

**Polynomial:** x^2 + 9x + 14

**Step 1:** Factorise by splitting the middle term.
- x^2 + 9x + 14 = x^2 + 7x + 2x + 14 = x(x + 7) + 2(x + 7) = (x + 2)(x + 7)

**Step 2:** Find zeroes.
- x + 2 = 0 gives x = -2
- x + 7 = 0 gives x = -7
- Zeroes are **-2 and -7**

**Step 3:** Verify.
- Sum of zeroes = -2 + (-7) = -9 = -(9)/1 = -b/a. Verified!
- Product of zeroes = (-2)(-7) = 14 = 14/1 = c/a. Verified!

### Example 2: Find zeroes of x^2 - 5

**Step 1:** Use the identity a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b).
- x^2 - 5 = (x - root 5)(x + root 5)

**Step 2:** Zeroes are **root 5** and **-root 5**.

**Step 3:** Verify.
- Sum = root 5 + (-root 5) = 0 = -(0)/1 = -b/a. Verified!
- Product = (root 5)(-root 5) = -5 = -5/1 = c/a. Verified!

### Example 3: Find zeroes of 3x^2 - 12x + 9

**Step 1:** Factorise.
- 3x^2 - 12x + 9 = 3x^2 - 9x - 3x + 9 = 3x(x - 3) - 3(x - 3) = (3x - 3)(x - 3) = 3(x - 1)(x - 3)

**Step 2:** Zeroes are **1 and 3**.

**Step 3:** Verify.
- Sum = 1 + 3 = 4 = -(-12)/3 = 12/3 = 4. Verified!
- Product = 1 x 3 = 3 = 9/3 = 3. Verified!

## Finding a Quadratic Polynomial from Given Zeroes

Knowing the sum and product of zeroes lets you reconstruct the polynomial.

### Formula

If S = sum of zeroes and P = product of zeroes, the quadratic polynomial is:

**x^2 - Sx + P**

(or any non-zero scalar multiple: k(x^2 - Sx + P))

### Example: Sum = 5, Product = -6

The polynomial is x^2 - 5x + (-6) = **x^2 - 5x - 6**.

Check: x^2 - 5x - 6 = (x - 6)(x + 1). Zeroes are 6 and -1. Sum = 5, product = -6. Correct!

### More Examples

| Sum of zeroes | Product of zeroes | Quadratic polynomial |
|--------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| 3/4 | -2 | x^2 - (3/4)x - 2 or 4x^2 - 3x - 8 |
| root 3 | 1/5 | x^2 - root(3)x + 1/5 or 5x^2 - 5root(3)x + 1 |
| 0 | root 7 | x^2 + root 7 |
| -2 | 3 | x^2 + 2x + 3 |

## Relationship Between Zeroes and Coefficients of a Cubic Polynomial

If **alpha, beta, and gamma** are the zeroes of the cubic polynomial **ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d**, then:

- **Sum of zeroes:** alpha + beta + gamma = **-b/a**
- **Sum of products taken two at a time:** alpha x beta + beta x gamma + gamma x alpha = **c/a**
- **Product of all three zeroes:** alpha x beta x gamma = **-d/a**

> **Key idea:** Three relationships tie the three zeroes to the four coefficients. The signs follow an alternating pattern: -b/a, +c/a, -d/a.

### Worked Example

**Verify** that 2, -3, and 1/2 are zeroes of p(x) = 2x^3 - x^2 - 13x + 6.

**Step 1:** Check each value.
- p(2) = 2(8) - (4) - 13(2) + 6 = 16 - 4 - 26 + 6 = -8 (not zero!)

Let us try 2, -3, and 1/2 for p(x) = 2x^3 + x^2 - 13x + 6 instead.
- p(2) = 16 + 4 - 26 + 6 = 0
- p(-3) = 2(-27) + 9 + 39 + 6 = -54 + 54 = 0
- p(1/2) = 2(1/8) + 1/4 - 13/2 + 6 = 1/4 + 1/4 - 13/2 + 6 = 1/2 - 13/2 + 6 = -6 + 6 = 0

All three give zero, confirming they are zeroes.

**Step 2:** Verify (a = 2, b = 1, c = -13, d = 6).
- Sum = 2 + (-3) + 1/2 = -1/2 = -(1)/2 = -b/a. Verified!
- Sum of products in pairs = 2(-3) + (-3)(1/2) + (1/2)(2) = -6 - 3/2 + 1 = -13/2 = c/a. Verified!
- Product = 2 x (-3) x (1/2) = -3 = -(6)/2 = -d/a. Verified!

## Important Tips for Board Exams

1. **Always verify** the relationship between zeroes and coefficients after finding them -- this earns full marks.

2. To factorise quadratic polynomials, master the **splitting the middle term** technique:
   - For ax^2 + bx + c, find two numbers with product = a x c and sum = b
   - Rewrite bx using these two numbers
   - Group and factor

3. To build a quadratic polynomial from known zeroes alpha and beta:
   - p(x) = x^2 - (alpha + beta)x + (alpha x beta)

4. Maximum zeroes rule:
   - Linear (degree 1): at most **1** zero
   - Quadratic (degree 2): at most **2** zeroes
   - Cubic (degree 3): at most **3** zeroes
   - Degree n: at most **n** zeroes

## Chapter Summary

1. Polynomials of degrees 1, 2, and 3 are called **linear**, **quadratic**, and **cubic** respectively.

2. A quadratic polynomial in x with real coefficients has the form **ax^2 + bx + c**, where a, b, c are real and a is not zero.

3. The **zeroes** of a polynomial p(x) correspond to the x-coordinates where the graph of y = p(x) crosses the x-axis.

4. A quadratic polynomial has **at most 2 zeroes**; a cubic has **at most 3**.

5. If alpha and beta are zeroes of ax^2 + bx + c:
   - alpha + beta = -b/a
   - alpha x beta = c/a

6. If alpha, beta, and gamma are zeroes of ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d:
   - alpha + beta + gamma = -b/a
   - alpha.beta + beta.gamma + gamma.alpha = c/a
   - alpha.beta.gamma = -d/a
