## When Is Identity Politics Healthy and When Is It Harmful?

**Student:** The chapter says identity politics can be both good and bad. How do I tell the difference?

**Mentor:** This is one of the most important questions in modern democracy. Let me give you some tests.

**Test 1: Does it include or exclude?**

**Healthy identity politics** opens doors for the historically excluded.
- Dalit movement: opens education, employment, dignity for Dalits
- Women's movement: opens public life, equal rights for women
- LGBT movement: opens equal citizenship for sexual minorities
- Tribal rights: opens land, culture, education for Adivasis

**Harmful identity politics** closes doors for others.
- "Our community first" excluding other communities
- Demonising minorities for political gain
- Saying one religion is "real Indian" and others are not
- Caste-based violence against other castes

The same identity (say, Hindu, or Muslim, or Dalit) can be politicised in inclusive or exclusive ways. The TONE matters as much as the labels.

**Test 2: Does it empower the powerless or entrench the powerful?**

**Healthy identity politics** lifts up those who have been pushed down.

**Harmful identity politics** protects those who already have power against others getting it.

When the BSP (Bahujan Samaj Party) speaks for Dalits — historically the most oppressed group — that is empowering the powerless. Healthy.

When upper-caste people complain about reservations taking "their" seats — that is the powerful resisting equality. Less healthy (though sometimes contains legitimate concerns about implementation).

When Tamil identity politics challenges Hindi imposition — that is regional identity protecting itself from majoritarianism. Healthy.

When Hindi-nationalist politics demands Hindi as "national language" — that is majority pushing dominance. Less healthy.

**Test 3: Does it focus on policy or identity alone?**

**Healthy identity politics** combines identity with policy.
- "Vote for us because we'll deliver education, jobs, healthcare for our community"

**Harmful identity politics** is just about identity, with no real policy.
- "Vote for us because we are your community" (with no actual plan)
- "Don't vote for them because they belong to the other community"

The first invites accountability — did they deliver? The second is just tribal mobilisation.

**Test 4: Does it use violence?**

**Healthy identity politics** is democratic — uses elections, peaceful protest, advocacy, legal channels.

**Harmful identity politics** uses violence — riots, lynching, intimidation, mob rule.

Any identity politics that turns violent is unhealthy, no matter how legitimate its underlying grievance.

**Test 5: Does it allow for change?**

**Healthy identity politics** allows for inter-community alliances, individual exceptions, evolution.

**Harmful identity politics** is rigid — "you must vote with your community"; "anyone who votes differently is a traitor."

**Examples from Indian politics:**

(1) **Mandal Commission and OBC politics (1990 onwards)**: OBCs are historically backward (about 50% of Indian population). Reserving government jobs and educational seats for them was identity politics — but EMPOWERING. Healthy.

But: when politicians use OBC mobilisation just to win elections without delivering real improvements, less healthy.

(2) **Dalit politics**: B.R. Ambedkar built Dalit identity politics to fight caste oppression. Kanshi Ram and Mayawati continued. The BSP rose to power in UP. This was empowering. Healthy.

But: when Dalit politics is reduced to caste arithmetic without policy substance, less healthy.

(3) **Tamil regional identity**: protecting Tamil language and culture against Hindi imposition has kept Tamil Nadu's culture vibrant. The Dravidian parties (DMK, AIADMK) have delivered education, social welfare, women's empowerment in Tamil Nadu. Healthy.

But: extreme anti-Hindi politics that demonises northerners or other communities can become unhealthy.

(4) **Hindu nationalism (BJP and related)**: Hindu identity has been a major political force, especially since the 1980s. Defenders say it represents legitimate Hindu interests historically ignored.

Critics argue that when it demonises Muslims, fuels riots, attacks pluralism, it crosses into communalism.

Different observers will judge differently. The TESTS above can help you form your own view.

(5) **Women's movement**: long history of fighting patriarchy. Brought voting rights, education, anti-violence laws, reservation. Healthy.

But: some critics argue women's movement has sometimes ignored women in disadvantaged communities (Dalit women, Muslim women) — focused on urban elite women. Internal correction.

(6) **LGBT politics**: brought decriminalisation of homosexuality (Supreme Court, 2018), some recognition of trans rights. Empowering. Healthy.

**The general principle:**

Identity politics is HEALTHY when it:
- Includes more people
- Empowers the marginalised
- Has policy substance
- Uses democratic means
- Allows flexibility and change

Identity politics is UNHEALTHY when it:
- Excludes people
- Defends existing privilege
- Has only identity, no policy
- Uses violence
- Demands tribal loyalty

These tests are not perfect. Reasonable people will disagree on borderline cases. But the tests give you a framework to think rather than just react emotionally.

When you grow up and engage with Indian politics, you'll constantly encounter identity-based mobilisation — by religion, caste, region, gender, language. Apply these tests. Vote for the healthier kind. Avoid the unhealthy kind.

This is the daily work of being a thoughtful citizen in a diverse democracy.

## Why Has the Caste System Lasted So Long?

**Student:** The caste system has been criticised for centuries. Buddha, Bhakti saints, Ambedkar — all opposed it. Why has it lasted?

**Mentor:** This is one of the most stubborn social systems in human history. Several reasons explain its persistence.

**1. It's woven into multiple systems at once.**

Caste isn't just a religious idea. It's:
- A religious idea (varna-jati hierarchy)
- An economic system (occupational specialisation)
- A marriage system (endogamy)
- A residential pattern (separate localities)
- A purity-pollution system (food, contact, water)
- A power structure (who controls land, decisions)
- A psychological system (internalised by all participants)

Changing one part doesn't change the whole. You can criticise the religious justification, but the economic structures, marriage patterns, and residential patterns persist.

**2. Inheritance keeps it alive.**

Caste is INHERITED. You are born into your caste. Even if you reject it personally, others continue to see you through it.

If you marry within your caste, your children inherit the same caste. If you marry outside, you face social pressure, family conflict, sometimes violence.

This makes change slow. Even committed anti-caste activists transmit caste to their children unless they (and their children) consistently break the patterns.

**3. Reservations institutionalise caste.**

This is a difficult truth.

To compensate for historical injustice, India's Constitution provides reservations for SCs, STs, and OBCs. This is justified — historical injustice creates ongoing disadvantage; affirmative action attempts to compensate.

But: reservations require everyone to declare their caste on government forms. To benefit from reservations, you must be officially recognised as SC, ST, or OBC. So the state, in trying to help marginalised castes, also REINFORCES the caste classification system.

This is a real dilemma. Many argue reservations are still necessary because actual social caste discrimination persists. Others argue that reservations entrench the very system they were meant to eliminate.

There is no easy answer. The current consensus: reservations should continue until caste discrimination genuinely ends, which is far from achieved.

**4. Caste functions as social capital.**

In a country with weak institutions, caste networks provide:
- Job referrals ("our caste man owns that factory")
- Marriage opportunities
- Business partnerships and trust
- Community support
- Political connections
- Real estate access

For many Indians, abandoning caste means abandoning these networks. The costs are real.

**5. Politicians benefit from caste.**

Caste-based voting is a feature of Indian elections. Politicians know their caste base. They cultivate it. They give tickets to specific castes in specific constituencies. They mobilise based on caste loyalty.

This creates a political class with interest in MAINTAINING caste salience. Politicians who try to transcend caste (Aam Aadmi Party in some places) sometimes succeed but often struggle.

**6. Religious and cultural rituals sustain it.**

Many Indian religious and cultural practices have caste dimensions:
- Wedding rituals (different for different castes)
- Funeral rites
- Some festivals
- Some traditional clothing/jewellery
- Some musical and artistic traditions

To reject caste fully means rejecting some cultural inheritance. This is hard for many people.

**7. Caste in urban India.**

Some have argued caste is fading in urban India. Partly true:
- Workplaces and schools are less caste-segregated
- Public transport, restaurants, shops are open to all
- Friends and colleagues across castes are normal

But: marriages remain mostly within caste. Real estate often segregated. Many professional networks have caste underpinnings.

So urban caste has become more SUBTLE rather than disappeared.

**8. The 'creamy layer' problem.**

Within historically backward castes (especially OBCs), some have become economically and socially advanced over the last 70+ years (the "creamy layer"). These elites benefit most from reservations, while the truly poor within the caste benefit less.

The Supreme Court has restricted reservations for the creamy layer in some contexts. But the issue shows that even reservation policy doesn't always reach those most in need.

**9. Caste violence.**

Violence keeps caste salient. Atrocities against Dalits — the murder of Dalits for trying to access water, education, or higher castes' privileges — happen even now. The 2024 RSS chief's comments about caste tensions show the issue is far from resolved. Each atrocity reminds Dalits (and others) that caste still matters.

**10. Globalisation has been mixed.**

Globalisation has reduced some caste barriers (multinational companies hire by merit, more or less) but has also:
- Created new inequalities (top IT jobs require English and good schools — both correlated with caste)
- Generated new social anxieties as old patterns disrupt

**WHAT IS CHANGING:**

(1) **Education**: more Dalits, tribals, OBCs in colleges. Several Dalit professionals, IAS officers, professors, politicians.

(2) **Reservation outcomes**: significant Dalit political representation. Dalit Chief Ministers (Mayawati), Presidents (K.R. Narayanan, Ram Nath Kovind, Droupadi Murmu).

(3) **Anti-discrimination laws**: SC/ST Atrocities Act provides strong penalties.

(4) **Inter-caste marriages**: slowly increasing, especially among urban educated.

(5) **Dalit assertiveness**: Dalits less willing to accept humiliation; more willing to challenge legally and politically.

(6) **Media coverage**: caste violence gets more attention now than 50 years ago.

(7) **Dalit literature, cinema, art**: Dalit voices reaching wider audiences.

(8) **International awareness**: caste discrimination recognised globally as a human rights issue.

**WHAT NEEDS TO CHANGE:**

(1) **Enforce existing laws** — atrocities act, anti-discrimination laws.

(2) **Quality education for all** — especially in rural areas where caste is starkest.

(3) **Land reform** — caste and land ownership are linked.

(4) **Inter-caste marriage support** — currently socially punished.

(5) **End caste segregation** in housing, neighbourhoods, schools.

(6) **Cultural change** — every Indian family has to examine its own caste practices.

(7) **Continue affirmative action** — reservations remain necessary until equality is real.

(8) **Politicians must transcend caste politics** — easier said than done.

**THE LONG VIEW:**

The caste system has weakened significantly in 70+ years. But it has not disappeared. It may take another generation or two of conscious effort to truly transcend it.

This is the work of every Indian — especially the young. When you choose friends, marriage partners, business associates, employees — caste should not matter. When you encounter caste discrimination, oppose it. When you have privilege, share it.

The Constitution gave the legal framework. Society has to do the rest. Every generation has to keep working at it.

Buddha, Bhakti saints, Ambedkar started this work. They did their part. Your generation has to continue.

That is the unfinished business of Indian democracy.

## What Does "Real" Secularism Mean in India?

**Student:** I keep hearing about Indian secularism. What does it actually mean in practice?

**Mentor:** Good question. Secularism is one of the most discussed and debated concepts in Indian politics. Let me clarify.

**What Secularism Doesn't Mean.**

Some misconceptions:

(1) **Secularism doesn't mean anti-religion.** Indian secularism doesn't say religion is bad or shouldn't exist. People are free to be deeply religious.

(2) **Secularism doesn't mean atheism.** Most Indians are religious. Secularism is about HOW religion relates to the state, not about whether people should be religious.

(3) **Secularism doesn't mean equal treatment regardless of religion's content.** The state can intervene in religion in some cases — banning practices that violate fundamental rights (like child marriage, sati, untouchability).

(4) **Secularism doesn't mean ignoring religion entirely.** Religious holidays are state holidays. Religious places are protected. Religious symbols can appear in public.

**What Secularism Does Mean.**

(1) **No state religion.** India doesn't have an official religion. Hindus are 80% of population but Hinduism is not the state religion. Compare to UK (Anglicanism), Israel (Judaism), Iran (Islam), Vatican (Catholicism), Bhutan (Buddhism).

(2) **Equal treatment of all religions by the state.** The state should treat Hindus, Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, atheists equally.

(3) **Religious freedom (Articles 25-28).** Every Indian can practise, profess, propagate any religion (or none).

(4) **No discrimination on religious grounds.** In jobs, education, services — religion cannot be the basis for denying someone.

(5) **The state can interfere to reform religion.** Indian secularism allows the state to ban harmful religious practices — temple entry for Dalits, prohibiting sati, banning triple talaq, etc.

(6) **Religious bodies don't make state law.** Religious leaders advise; they don't directly legislate.

**Indian Secularism vs Western Secularism.**

Indian secularism is **different** from Western models:

**French secularism (laïcité)**:
- Strict separation
- Religion confined to private sphere
- No religious symbols in public schools
- State actively pushes religion out

**American secularism**:
- Separation of church and state
- State doesn't establish or favour any religion
- But religious life is vibrant and individual

**Indian secularism ("principled distance")**:
- Equal respect for all religions, not strict separation
- State can engage with religion (including reform)
- Religious symbols welcome in public life
- All major religious festivals celebrated as state holidays
- Religious personal laws (Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Parsi) coexist

So Indian secularism is more accommodative than French, more state-involved than American.

**The Indian Model in Practice.**

(1) **Sarva Dharma Sambhava** — "all religions equal" — is the popular phrase capturing the spirit.

(2) **Common civil law for most matters** — but **personal laws** (marriage, divorce, inheritance) differ for Hindus, Muslims, Christians, Parsis. This is an ongoing debate (Uniform Civil Code).

(3) **State manages major temples in some places** (Tirupati, Sabarimala, Jagannath Puri, etc.) — though this is contested.

(4) **State funds some religious institutions** — Haj subsidy (recently ended), Kumbh Mela arrangements, repairs to ancient religious monuments.

(5) **State can ban practices** — sati was banned (1829), child marriage restricted, untouchability outlawed, animal sacrifice restricted in some places.

(6) **Religious education in private schools is allowed** — but in government schools, religion-specific teaching is restricted.

(7) **Religious freedom includes conversion** — though several states have passed laws restricting "forced conversion" or "conversion by allurement."

**Tensions and Debates.**

(1) **Uniform Civil Code (UCC)**: Should there be one common code for all Indians (Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Parsi)? Supporters say YES — gender equality requires it. Opponents say it threatens religious freedom. Currently, the debate continues.

(2) **State involvement in temples but not mosques/churches**: Critics argue this isn't equal treatment. Defenders say Hindu temples are managed because they are public institutions historically; mosques and churches are private religious bodies.

(3) **Religious instruction**: Should government schools teach about religions? If so, which? How?

(4) **Religious symbols in public**: Sometimes contested — e.g., wearing hijab in schools (Karnataka, 2022); cow protection laws.

(5) **Communal politics**: Politicians sometimes use religion for votes. Critics say this violates secularism. Supporters say it's normal democracy.

(6) **Minority rights and the majority**: Some say minorities get "special treatment" through subsidies, separate personal laws, etc. Others say minorities need protection against majoritarian dominance.

**Examples of Indian Secularism in Action.**

(1) **Festivals**: India celebrates Holi, Diwali, Eid, Christmas, Guru Nanak Jayanti, Buddha Purnima, Easter, etc. — all as state holidays. Compare to many countries that recognise only one religion's holidays.

(2) **Courts protect minorities**: Supreme Court has struck down laws that discriminated against Muslims (triple talaq invalidated 2017), Christians (some conversion law issues), and others. Same for protecting Hindu minorities in some states.

(3) **Religious institutions function freely**: Mosques in temple towns, temples in Muslim-majority areas, churches in tribal regions. Most operate freely most of the time.

(4) **State engages with religion for reform**: Temple entry for Dalits, Sabarimala women's entry case (Supreme Court 2018, then reservations), addressing harmful religious practices.

(5) **Religious leaders engage with state**: Religious bodies advise government on policy affecting their communities.

**Threats to Indian Secularism.**

Critics point to several concerning trends:

(1) **Communal politics intensifying**: vote-bank politics around religious identity has become more aggressive.

(2) **Religious violence**: lynchings, mob attacks targeting religious minorities (especially Muslims in some places).

(3) **Hate speech**: religious leaders giving violent or exclusionary speeches.

(4) **Anti-conversion laws**: several states have passed laws that critics say restrict religious freedom (especially of Christians, Muslims).

(5) **Discriminatory citizenship**: Citizenship Amendment Act, 2019 — critics say it discriminates by religion.

(6) **Reduced criticism of religion**: chilling effect on rationalist voices, religious reformers, atheists.

These trends concern those who value Indian secularism.

**Defenders of Indian Secularism's Health.**

Others argue Indian secularism is robust:

(1) **Most Indians remain religiously tolerant in daily life**.

(2) **Constitutional framework is intact** — Supreme Court continues to strike down clearly discriminatory measures.

(3) **Pluralism remains visible** — temples, mosques, churches, gurudwaras side by side.

(4) **Religious minorities continue to thrive** in many fields — business, politics, arts, science.

(5) **Election results don't always reward communal politics** — many voters consider other issues.

**The Citizen's Role.**

Whatever the trends, individual Indians can:

(1) **Respect all religions** — including those they don't follow.

(2) **Reject hate speech** — call it out when seen, in family, on social media, in public.

(3) **Vote based on policy, not religious identity** — choose candidates who deliver, not those who divide.

(4) **Protect religious minorities** — if you're in the majority, use that strength.

(5) **Engage with people of other religions** — friendship, work, learning across faiths.

(6) **Speak up against riots and violence** — even when politically inconvenient.

(7) **Read the Constitution and understand secularism** — know what India committed to.

(8) **Defend institutions** — courts, Election Commission, media — that protect secularism.

**The Big Picture.**

Indian secularism is a unique creation — not imported from anywhere, but designed for India's specific challenges. It tries to balance:
- Religious freedom for everyone
- Equal treatment by the state
- Protection of minorities
- Possibility of state-led reform
- Coexistence of multiple religious traditions

This balance is hard. It is contested. It is sometimes threatened.

But for 75+ years, it has held. India remains one of the few countries in the world with such elaborate religious diversity AND a functioning democracy.

When you grow up and engage with Indian politics, secularism will be one of the contested ideas you encounter. Form your view thoughtfully. Defend the version that protects everyone — including those whose religion you don't share, including atheists, including the irreligious.

That is what makes a country truly free. That is what India committed to in 1950. Each generation has to renew that commitment.

The alternative — religious majoritarianism — has been tried in many countries. The results have been bad. India chose a different path. Help keep India on it.
