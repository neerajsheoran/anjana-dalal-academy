## Social Differences Are Not the Problem

Every society has differences. Indian society has many — of language, region, religion, caste, gender, class, ethnicity, profession.

The instinct of some political thinkers (and many ordinary people) is to wish these differences would disappear: "Why can't we all just be Indians, without these labels?"

This sounds nice. But it is **wrong** in important ways.

**Social diversity is normal.** All large societies have it. It is not going away.

**Suppressing identities causes resentment.** When you tell women "don't make this a gender issue" or Dalits "don't make this a caste issue," you are asking them to ignore real disadvantages they face.

**Political expression of differences can strengthen democracy.** When historically marginalised groups organise politically — women, Dalits, religious minorities — they fight for fairness. Their voices become part of public debate. The democracy becomes more inclusive.

**The danger is not difference itself — it's how it's expressed.**

This chapter looks at three social divisions in India — **gender, religion, and caste** — and how they shape politics. We will see when their political expression is healthy (helping the marginalised) and when it is harmful (creating violent divisions).

## Part 1 — Gender and Politics

Let us begin with gender.

### Why Gender Is Political

Some say "gender is a personal matter — nothing to do with politics."

This is wrong. Politics is about power. Who has power, who doesn't, who decides, who is decided about. Gender is one of the deepest power divisions in every society — including India.

**The personal IS political.**

When boys are encouraged to study and girls to learn cooking — that's political (it affects what they become).

When women do most household work and men get paid for similar work outside — that's political (it shapes income, status, autonomy).

When men dominate panchayats, parliaments, courts, boardrooms — that's political (decisions are made by mostly half the population).

When violence against women is treated as "private matter" — that's political (the state's choice to not intervene is itself political).

So gender is everywhere in politics — whether or not we name it.

### Public-Private Division

Most societies, including India, traditionally divide work by gender:

**Women's work** (usually unpaid):
- Cooking
- Cleaning
- Childcare
- Looking after elderly
- Fetching water
- Collecting firewood
- Working in family farms (often without recognition)

**Men's work** (usually paid):
- Outside employment
- Public roles
- Political activity
- Business

This is called **sexual division of labour**. Even when women DO work outside (and many do — over 75% of agricultural workers in India are women), their work is often:
- Underpaid
- Unrecognised
- Treated as supplementary

The result: women's role in **public life and politics is minimal** in most societies.

### Patriarchy

This system where men dominate is called **patriarchy** (literally "rule by the father").

Patriarchy is not just about individual men being mean. It is a **system** built into:
- Family structures
- Property inheritance
- Religious authorities
- Educational practices
- Workplace cultures
- Political institutions

Even women internalise patriarchy and pass it to their daughters.

### The Women's Movement

Around the world, women have organised against patriarchy:

(1) **Voting rights movement** (suffragettes in UK and USA, late 19th-early 20th centuries)
(2) **Equal pay movement** (mid-20th century onwards)
(3) **Equal opportunity in education and employment**
(4) **Property rights for women**
(5) **Protection against violence** (rape laws, domestic violence laws)
(6) **Reproductive rights** (abortion, contraception)
(7) **Equal representation in politics**

The result over 200 years: women got the vote (last in Switzerland in 1971!), got into universities, got into professions, got into politics.

### Women in Indian Politics

India has a complicated record:

**Achievements**:
- Constitutional equality from 1950
- Several women Prime Ministers, Presidents, Chief Ministers (Indira Gandhi, Sushma Swaraj, Mamata Banerjee, Mayawati, Jayalalithaa, Pratibha Patil, Droupadi Murmu, etc.)
- Strong women in many fields (Sonia Gandhi, Sushma Swaraj, Nirmala Sitharaman, Smriti Irani, etc.)
- 33% reservation in panchayats (many states 50%) — over 1 million elected women representatives
- **Women's Reservation Bill** finally passed in 2023 — will reserve 1/3 of Lok Sabha and state assembly seats for women (will come into effect after delimitation following 2026 census)

**Continuing Problems**:
- Female labour force participation has actually DROPPED in recent decades (from about 30% to about 20%)
- Gender wage gap remains
- Domestic violence remains widespread (about 30%+ of women report having experienced it)
- Sex-selective abortion has skewed sex ratios (about 940 girls per 1000 boys at birth)
- Women in Lok Sabha: about 15% (low by global standards)
- Women in higher judiciary: less than 12%
- Honour killings, dowry deaths, sexual violence continue

**Where India Stands Globally**:

In the **Global Gender Gap Index 2023** (World Economic Forum), India ranks **127 out of 146 countries**. We are behind countries like Bangladesh and Nepal. This is one of India's significant weaknesses.

### What Will Change Things

Several factors are slowly improving the situation:

(1) **Education** — more girls in schools and universities now than ever before
(2) **Employment** — though uneven, many sectors hire women (especially IT, services)
(3) **Laws** — strong laws against dowry, domestic violence, workplace harassment, sex-selection (though enforcement varies)
(4) **Awareness** — campaigns like #MeToo, Beti Bachao Beti Padhao, Mahila Samman Yojana
(5) **Political representation** — Women's Reservation Bill will help once implemented
(6) **Role models** — visible successful women in every field
(7) **Family dynamics** — slowly changing in urban families; harder in some rural areas

But India will only be a full democracy when women are full equal citizens — not just legally, but in actual practice.

## Part 2 — Religion and Politics

The relationship between religion and politics is one of the most consequential issues in any society.

### Why It's Complicated

Religion involves people's deepest beliefs about meaning, morality, community. Politics involves the distribution of power and resources. When religion enters politics:

**It can be positive**:
- Religious leaders inspiring social reform (Gandhi, Martin Luther King Jr.)
- Religious values motivating help for the poor
- Religious community organising for justice
- Religious teachings inspiring civic virtue

**It can be negative**:
- Religious extremism leading to violence
- Religious majorities oppressing minorities
- Religion used to justify discrimination
- Religious conflict tearing societies apart

The challenge: how to allow religion's positive role while preventing its negative effects.

### India's Constitutional Framework — Secularism

India's Constitution makes India a **secular** state. This means:

(1) **No state religion**. India has no official religion (unlike Pakistan — Islam; UK — Anglicanism; Israel — Judaism; Vatican — Catholicism).

(2) **Equal respect for all religions**. The state treats Hindus, Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Jains, Buddhists, Parsis, and atheists as equal citizens.

(3) **Religious freedom**. Citizens can practise, profess, and propagate any religion (or none).

(4) **State doesn't run religion**. Generally, government doesn't tell religions what to teach or how to practise (with some exceptions for safety or rights).

(5) **Religion doesn't run state**. Religious bodies don't make state laws (though there are some exceptions like personal laws).

Indian secularism is sometimes called **"principled distance"** — distinct from French secularism (laïcité, which is strictly separation) or American secularism (more privatised religion).

### Communalism — The Danger

**Communalism** is the political use of religious identity to divide society.

When communalism takes hold:
- People are seen primarily through religious identity
- "Us vs Them" thinking dominates
- Religious symbols and resentments are politicised
- Politicians appeal to religious votes
- Tensions can erupt into violence (riots, lynchings, pogroms)

India has had many communal incidents:
- Partition violence (1947-48): the worst — 1-2 million killed
- Anti-Sikh riots (1984): after Indira Gandhi's assassination
- Bombay riots and bomb blasts (1992-93): after Babri Masjid demolition
- Gujarat riots (2002): after Godhra train burning
- Muzaffarnagar riots (2013)
- Delhi riots (2020)
- Frequent smaller incidents

In each, ordinary people of different faiths who had lived together peacefully suddenly turned on each other. Communal politics had poisoned the well.

### Why Communalism Is Dangerous

(1) **It treats religion as the primary identity.** But people have many identities — gender, region, profession, family, age, hobbies. Reducing them to religion is dehumanising.

(2) **It assumes religions are monolithic.** But every religion has internal diversity. There is no single "Hindu" or "Muslim" or "Christian" view on most political issues.

(3) **It produces violence.** When one community sees another as enemy, violence follows.

(4) **It corrupts religion.** Religion becomes about political power rather than spiritual quest. Religious leaders become political brokers.

(5) **It weakens the country.** A divided country cannot focus on development, education, science, prosperity.

### Why Secularism Matters

Indian secularism has been vital for several reasons:

(1) **Religious diversity demands it.** India has 80% Hindus, 14% Muslims, plus Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, tribal religions, atheists. State preference for any one would marginalise others.

(2) **Partition's lesson.** The horrors of 1947-48 showed where religious politics leads. The Constitution makers chose secularism to prevent recurrence.

(3) **Democratic equality.** All citizens are equal regardless of religion. The state should not favour one over another.

(4) **Stability.** Most diverse countries that abandoned secularism have suffered (Lebanon's civil war, Yugoslavia's wars, Northern Ireland's troubles).

(5) **India's own tradition.** India has been religiously diverse for thousands of years. Plurality is natural to Indian civilisation.

### Communalism vs Healthy Religious Politics

Not all religion in politics is communal. There can be legitimate religious politics:

**Healthy religious politics**:
- Religious communities can express their views on social issues
- Religious bodies can advocate for policies (against poverty, for peace, for the environment)
- Religious leaders can speak on moral issues
- Religious traditions can inspire civic engagement
- People can vote based on their religious values

**Unhealthy religious politics (communalism)**:
- Religion becomes the SOLE basis for political mobilisation
- "Vote for our community" rather than "vote on policies"
- Demonising other religions
- Religious symbols politicised
- Religion linked to nationalism in exclusive ways

The line between healthy and unhealthy is sometimes blurry. But the basic test: **does religious politics include or exclude? Does it lift up the marginalised or attack other communities?**

### Indian Politics and Religion Today

In recent years, several issues have been politically charged:

(1) **Ram Mandir-Babri Masjid dispute** (settled by Supreme Court in 2019, Ram Mandir consecrated 2024)

(2) **Triple talaq** — practice in Muslim divorce; banned by Supreme Court (2017) and Parliament (2019)

(3) **Uniform Civil Code** — debate about common personal laws

(4) **Cow protection** — laws restricting cow slaughter; some violence around it

(5) **Citizenship Amendment Act (2019)** and proposed NRC — contested politically

(6) **Religious conversion laws** — several states have passed restrictions

(7) **Hijab debate** — Karnataka case (2022)

(8) **Article 370 revocation (2019)** — J&K's special status

Each of these has religious dimensions and political controversy. Whether they represent healthy religious politics or veering toward communalism is itself debated.

### The Challenge for Citizens

Every Indian — Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Sikh, atheist — has to navigate this terrain.

(1) **Don't reduce people to their religion.** A Muslim is not just a Muslim — also a citizen, professional, family member, individual.

(2) **Don't believe stereotypes.** Most Hindus are not extremist. Most Muslims are not extremist. Most Christians are not converting people. Etc.

(3) **Don't fall for "they hate us" narratives.** These are usually politically motivated.

(4) **Respect religious freedom.** Even if you disagree with someone's religion, they have the right to practise it.

(5) **Distinguish private and public.** Religion is mostly personal. Politics affects everyone. Don't mix them carelessly.

(6) **Vote on issues, not identity.** Ask candidates about policies, not just which religion they belong to.

(7) **Protect minorities.** If you are part of the majority (whichever religion), use that strength to protect minorities. This is moral, but also wise — the majority benefits from a peaceful pluralistic society.

(8) **Refuse violence.** Whatever the provocation, violence is not the answer.

These principles, lived by enough Indians, keep secularism alive. They are the daily work of citizenship in a diverse democracy.

## Part 3 — Caste and Politics

The third major social division in India is **caste**.

### What Is Caste?

Caste is a **hereditary social hierarchy** unique to Indian society. It is:
- **Birth-based** (you are born into a caste; you can't easily change it)
- **Hierarchical** (some castes traditionally seen as higher, others lower)
- **Occupation-linked** (each caste traditionally associated with certain work)
- **Endogamous** (people traditionally marry within their caste)
- **Ritualistic** (involves rules about food, contact, purity)

The traditional structure had four main varnas:
- **Brahmins** (priests, scholars)
- **Kshatriyas** (warriors, rulers)
- **Vaishyas** (traders, farmers)
- **Shudras** (workers, servants)

And outside this — the **"untouchables"** (now called **Dalits**, meaning "broken people," or Scheduled Castes legally). These were the most oppressed group.

Below or alongside these — **tribal communities** (Adivasis, now Scheduled Tribes).

In addition, **Other Backward Classes (OBCs)** — various caste groups historically less privileged but not "untouchable."

### The Cost of the Caste System

For thousands of years, caste produced terrible injustice:
- Dalits forced into menial work (cleaning, leather work, scavenging)
- Forbidden to enter temples
- Forbidden to use public wells
- Children sat on floor in schools; received water from above
- Faced violence, exclusion, humiliation
- Couldn't own land
- Couldn't access education

This wasn't just historical. Some of these continued into the 20th century. Some continue (in modified form) today.

### Anti-Caste Movements

Many Indians fought against caste injustice:

(1) **Buddha (6th century BCE)** — opposed caste discrimination
(2) **Bhakti saints** (Kabir, Ravidas, Nanak, Mirabai, Tulsidas, others) — denounced caste in their poetry
(3) **Sikh gurus** — explicitly anti-caste; built langars where all eat together
(4) **Jyotirao Phule (1827-1890)** — major Maharashtra reformer; built schools for Dalits and girls
(5) **Periyar (E.V. Ramasamy, 1879-1973)** — Tamil rationalist; "Self-Respect Movement"
(6) **Narayana Guru (1856-1928)** — Kerala saint-reformer
(7) **Mahatma Gandhi** — called untouchables "Harijans" (children of God); fought against untouchability
(8) **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar (1891-1956)** — the towering figure of modern Dalit politics; chief architect of Constitution; later converted to Buddhism with hundreds of thousands of followers

### Constitutional Provisions

The Constitution makes several efforts to dismantle caste:

(1) **Abolition of untouchability (Article 17)** — practising it is a criminal offence
(2) **Equality before law (Article 14)** — no discrimination
(3) **No discrimination by caste, religion, race, sex, place of birth (Article 15)**
(4) **Equal opportunity in government employment (Article 16)**
(5) **Reservations** — for SCs (15%), STs (7.5%), OBCs (27%, since 1990s) in education and government employment; reserved seats in legislatures for SC/ST
(6) **Protection of SC/ST Atrocities Act** — strong penalties for crimes against Dalits and tribals

### Has Caste Faded?

Yes and no.

**Yes** — in several ways:
- Untouchability is illegal and much less practiced openly
- Education has reached many Dalits and tribals
- Reservations have brought Dalits into government jobs, universities, politics
- Dalit Chief Ministers, Presidents, Speakers, judges, professionals exist
- Caste mobility has increased (especially in cities)
- Inter-caste marriages are more common (still rare in many places)
- Urban professional life is less caste-driven

**No** — in other ways:
- Caste violence still occurs (atrocities against Dalits, especially in rural India)
- Caste matters in marriage choices for most Indians
- Caste affects political voting (parties target caste vote banks)
- Caste discrimination in housing, social interactions persists
- Honour killings (for inter-caste love) occur
- Manual scavenging (illegal but ongoing) is done largely by Dalits

So caste is **transforming** but **not gone**.

### Caste in Politics

Caste shapes Indian politics in many ways:

**Positive effects** (when caste politics protects the marginalised):
(1) **Dalit, tribal, OBC political mobilisation** has given voice to historically excluded groups
(2) **Reservations have created leadership** among these groups
(3) **Parties focused on these communities** (BSP, RJD, SP, Dravidian parties, etc.) have brought issues to mainstream debate
(4) **Awareness of caste injustice** has spread

**Negative effects** (when caste politics divides society):
(1) **Caste vote banks** — parties cynically appeal to caste rather than principles
(2) **Caste-based violence** sometimes increases during elections
(3) **Caste loyalty** can override merit or policy considerations
(4) **Identity politics** can prevent broader coalitions
(5) **Manipulation** — politicians manipulate caste anxieties for power

### Three Conclusions About Caste

From the chapter (and political science scholarship):

(1) **Caste is not eliminating itself.** Modernisation, urbanisation, market economy have NOT erased caste. Caste persists.

(2) **No country has been able to use caste as the only basis of political mobilisation.** When parties try this, they often face limits — voters have many concerns, not just caste.

(3) **Caste politics doesn't always mean discrimination.** Caste-based mobilisation by historically marginalised groups (Dalit politics, OBC politics) has often been DEMOCRATISING — bringing more groups into the political process.

So caste in politics is complex. It can entrench injustice; it can also challenge injustice. It depends on who is mobilising whom and for what purpose.

## The Bigger Lesson

Social differences exist in every society. India has more than most. **The question is not whether to have them — they exist. The question is how politics handles them.**

Three approaches:

(1) **Suppression**: pretend differences don't exist; force everyone to be the same. This fails — differences erupt as resentment later.

(2) **Exploitation**: politicians use differences to win votes by demonising other groups. This succeeds short-term but damages society long-term.

(3) **Accommodation and empowerment**: recognise differences, ensure no one is discriminated against, empower historically marginalised groups, prevent any group from dominating. This is the constitutional vision.

The third approach is the right one. It is also the hardest. It requires:
- **Tolerance** of difference
- **Wisdom** to distinguish legitimate identity politics from divisive identity politics
- **Vigilance** against communalism, casteism, sexism
- **Empowerment** of those who have been excluded
- **Trust** that diversity makes a country stronger

This is the work of every Indian citizen. Not just politicians — every voter, every neighbour, every family.

When you grow up and engage with Indian politics, you will face these issues constantly. Make your choices wisely. Reject communalism, casteism, and gender domination. Embrace empowerment, dignity, equality.

The country you inherit is one of the most diverse in human history. Your job is to keep it diverse AND keep it united. Both, together. That is the Indian challenge — and the Indian opportunity.

## Key Concepts to Remember

- **Social diversity is normal**; the question is how politics handles it.
- **Gender politics**: patriarchy, sexual division of labour, women's movement, women's reservation.
- **Public-private division**: women's domestic work, men's public work — both political.
- **India's gender gap is large** — ranks 127/146 in Global Gender Gap Index 2023.
- **Religion and politics**: India is **secular** — equal respect for all religions, no state religion.
- **Communalism**: dangerous when religion becomes basis for political mobilisation and exclusion.
- **Healthy religious politics** vs **communalism**: tone of inclusion or exclusion matters.
- **Caste**: birth-based hierarchy unique to South Asia.
- **Anti-caste tradition**: Buddha, Bhakti saints, Phule, Periyar, Ambedkar, Gandhi.
- **Constitutional provisions**: abolition of untouchability, reservations, equal protection.
- **Caste in politics**: complex — can be democratising (Dalit empowerment) or dividing (vote banks).
- The right approach: **accommodation and empowerment** of historically marginalised — not suppression of diversity, not exploitation of differences.
