## A Common Complaint

Many Indians say something like:
- "All parties are the same"
- "Politicians are all corrupt"
- "We don't need parties — they just divide us"
- "We should have a system without parties"

Yet, political parties exist in **every functioning democracy** in the world today. Why? Are people wrong to be cynical, or are parties really necessary?

The answer: **parties are essential for modern democracy** — but specific parties can be deeply flawed. The trick is to understand WHY parties matter and WHEN they go wrong.

## What Is a Political Party?

A **political party** is a group of people who:

1. **Come together to contest elections** and hold political power
2. **Agree on some policies and programmes**
3. **Promote what they believe is the common good** (sometimes; not always)

Parties have three main components:

(1) **Leaders** — who lead the party, become candidates, run governments
(2) **Active members** — who organise the party, mobilise voters, fight elections
(3) **Supporters/voters** — who back the party in elections

A political party is **partisan** — it stands for one position. But this is its strength, not weakness — different parties together give voters a range of choices.

## Why Do We Need Political Parties?

Several reasons. None of them obvious.

### 1. To Make Sense of Many Candidates

In an election, hundreds or thousands of candidates may contest. How can voters know each one personally?

Parties simplify this. You know roughly what BJP stands for, what Congress stands for, what AAP stands for. You can vote based on party even without knowing the candidate.

### 2. To Pool Resources and Coordinate

Running an election requires money, volunteers, organisation. One person can't do it alone. A party provides the network.

### 3. To Form Governments

A government requires majority support in the legislature. If everyone were independent, forming a stable government would be nearly impossible.

Parties create disciplined groups that can govern.

### 4. To Develop Policies

Governing requires thinking through complex issues — economy, foreign policy, social welfare. Individual politicians don't have time. Parties have think tanks, policy researchers, expert committees that develop positions.

### 5. To Hold Government Accountable

If parties are in opposition, they monitor the government, raise issues, criticise wrong decisions. This is essential for democracy.

### 6. To Connect Voters to Government

Parties are how voters get involved with politics. They mobilise voters, run campaigns, explain issues, encourage civic engagement.

### 7. To Aggregate Interests

A country has many different interests — farmers, workers, businesspeople, women, minorities. Parties bring these interests together into broader programmes that can govern.

### 8. To Provide Alternatives

In a democracy, voters can throw out a bad government. But they need an alternative. Parties provide the "next option" — without which throwing out government would lead to chaos.

So parties serve many functions. They are imperfect, but they are necessary.

## What Happens Without Parties?

Imagine a democracy with no parties:

- Hundreds of candidates with no clear identity
- Voters confused, unable to make informed choices
- Government unstable — different legislators voting different ways
- No consistent policy direction
- No alternative when current government fails
- Rich and powerful individuals dominate (they don't need parties)
- Smaller groups (workers, minorities, women) have no voice

Without parties, democracy might function for a moment, but it would not work in practice.

**Every modern democracy has parties.** This isn't accident.

## How Many Parties Is Best?

Different democracies have different party systems.

### 1. One-Party System

**Only one party** controls government. Other parties either don't exist or aren't allowed to win.

Examples:
- **China** — Communist Party rules; other parties exist but are subordinate
- **Soviet Union** (historical) — Communist Party only
- **North Korea** — Workers' Party
- **Cuba** — Communist Party

This is **not democracy** — citizens have no real choice. It is **authoritarianism**.

### 2. Two-Party System

**Two main parties** compete. Others exist but rarely win.

Examples:
- **USA** — Democrats and Republicans
- **UK** — Conservatives and Labour (though Liberal Democrats and others present)
- **Australia** — Labor and Coalition (LP-NP)

Advantages:
- Clear choice for voters
- Stable governments (usually)
- Easy accountability

Disadvantages:
- Smaller groups (minorities, ideological factions) have less voice
- Decisions tend toward middle ground (good or bad)
- Can become very polarised

### 3. Multi-Party System

**Many parties** compete; usually multiple parties win seats; coalitions are common.

Examples:
- **India** — many national and regional parties
- **Germany** — multiple parties; coalitions common
- **Israel** — many small parties; complex coalitions
- **Italy** — many parties, frequent government changes
- **France** — multi-party

Advantages:
- More choice for voters
- Diverse interests represented
- More inclusive

Disadvantages:
- Coalition instability
- Slower decisions
- Smaller parties can have outsized influence

### Which Is Best?

There's no single best. **It depends on the country.**

(1) **Small, homogeneous countries** — two-party may work (UK, Australia).

(2) **Large, diverse countries** — multi-party may be needed (India, Germany).

(3) **Authoritarian countries** — one-party persists by force.

India has chosen multi-party — necessary given its huge size and diversity. No single party can fully represent all of India's diversity.

## India's Party System

India is a vibrant **multi-party democracy** with both national and regional parties.

### National Parties

The Election Commission grants "national party" status to parties meeting certain criteria (substantial vote share in 4+ states, etc.). Major national parties include:

(1) **Indian National Congress (INC)** — founded 1885; the party that led India's freedom movement; ruled India for most of the period 1947-1989; ideology: secularism, social welfare, democratic socialism. Currently in opposition at the centre.

(2) **Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP)** — founded 1980 (predecessor Jana Sangh founded 1951); ideology: Hindutva (Hindu nationalism), economic liberalism; currently ruling India under PM Narendra Modi (since 2014).

(3) **Bahujan Samaj Party (BSP)** — founded 1984 by Kanshi Ram; primarily Dalit-focused; was strong in UP; weaker recently.

(4) **Communist Party of India (CPI) and CPI (Marxist)** — established 1920 and 1964 respectively; left-wing; strong historically in Kerala, West Bengal, Tripura; weaker now.

(5) **Aam Aadmi Party (AAP)** — founded 2012 by Arvind Kejriwal after India Against Corruption movement; ruled Delhi; ruled Punjab; gained national party status; ideology: anti-corruption, secularism, welfarism.

(6) **National People's Party (NPP)** — strong in Meghalaya and Northeast.

### Regional Parties

Many parties dominate specific states:

- **DMK** and **AIADMK** (Tamil Nadu) — Dravidian parties
- **Trinamool Congress (TMC)** (West Bengal) — Mamata Banerjee
- **Biju Janata Dal (BJD)** (Odisha) — Naveen Patnaik (long-running CM, lost 2024)
- **Telugu Desam Party (TDP)** and **YSR Congress Party (YSRCP)** (Andhra Pradesh)
- **Bharat Rashtra Samithi (BRS)** (Telangana) — formed Telangana state
- **Shiv Sena (Uddhav Thackeray)** and **Shiv Sena (Shinde)** and **NCP (Sharad Pawar)** and **NCP (Ajit Pawar)** (Maharashtra) — recently split parties
- **Janata Dal (United)** (Bihar) — Nitish Kumar
- **Rashtriya Janata Dal (RJD)** (Bihar) — Yadav family
- **Samajwadi Party (SP)** (UP) — Yadav family
- **Akali Dal** (Punjab) — Sikh-oriented
- **National Conference** and **Peoples Democratic Party (PDP)** (Jammu & Kashmir)
- **Mizo National Front (MNF)** (Mizoram)
- **And many more** in different states

Regional parties exist because:
- States are large; they have distinct identities
- Linguistic and cultural identities seek political voice
- Regional issues often need regional parties to champion them
- National parties don't always understand local concerns

In Indian politics, regional parties are crucial. Many central governments since 1989 have depended on regional party support.

### Number of Parties

India has registered over 2,500 political parties (with Election Commission). Most are small or inactive. Active parties number perhaps 50-100. Major parties number 20-30.

This makes Indian politics complex but also rich.

### Coalitions in Indian Politics

Since 1989, **coalition governments** have been common at the Centre. Major coalitions:

(1) **National Front** (1989-1990) — V.P. Singh-led
(2) **United Front** (1996-1998) — multiple parties
(3) **NDA** (National Democratic Alliance) — BJP-led
   - First: 1998-2004 (Atal Bihari Vajpayee PM)
   - Second: 2014-2019 (Modi PM, BJP won alone but kept NDA)
   - Third: 2019-2024 (BJP won alone)
   - Fourth: 2024-present (BJP didn't get majority alone; needs allies)
(4) **UPA** (United Progressive Alliance) — Congress-led
   - First: 2004-2009 (Manmohan Singh PM)
   - Second: 2009-2014 (Manmohan Singh PM)

Coalition era has been a feature of Indian democracy for 35+ years.

## Challenges Faced by Political Parties

Despite their necessity, parties face serious challenges.

### 1. Dynastic Politics

Many Indian parties are dominated by families:
- **Congress**: Nehru-Gandhi family (Indira → Rajiv → Sonia → Rahul)
- **DMK**: Karunanidhi family (Karunanidhi → Stalin → Udhayanidhi)
- **TMC**: Banerjee family
- **NCP**: Pawar family
- **RJD**: Yadav family
- **SP**: Yadav family
- **Akali Dal**: Badal family
- **Many regional parties**: family-run

Dynastic politics has several problems:
- Top positions go to family, regardless of merit
- Outsiders blocked from leadership
- Reduces internal democracy
- Concentrates power in few hands

Defenders argue:
- Family loyalty provides stability
- Voters can still reject dynasties (they sometimes do)
- Some non-dynastic parties (BJP, AAP) have also risen

### 2. Lack of Internal Democracy

Most Indian parties don't have real internal democracy:
- Top leaders are not elected by members
- Candidates for elections selected by leaders, not chosen democratically
- Party policies set by top, not bottom up
- Dissent often punished

Compare to Western democracies where:
- Party leaders are usually elected by members or delegates
- Local committees have real say in candidate selection
- Internal debates are public

### 3. Money in Politics

Indian elections involve enormous money:
- Candidates spend crores on campaigns
- Some allegedly buy votes
- Corporate donations are large but often hidden
- Black money in politics is a major issue

This makes political competition unequal — only the wealthy can fully compete. Also creates corruption (politicians need to repay donors).

**Electoral Bonds** scheme (struck down by Supreme Court in 2024) was meant to clean up donations but became controversial due to lack of transparency.

### 4. Criminal Backgrounds

Many Indian politicians have criminal cases:
- According to Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR), about 40% of Lok Sabha MPs in 2019 had criminal cases against them
- Some are serious (murder, kidnapping, attempted murder)
- Some are minor or politically motivated

Why?
- Criminals can intimidate voters and rivals
- Criminals have money
- Criminals can mobilise local supporters
- Voters in some places prefer "strong men"

Supreme Court has tried to reform — disqualifying convicted MPs, faster trials for politicians — but progress is slow.

### 5. Identity Politics

Discussed in Chapter 3. Parties often mobilise based on religion, caste, language, region. Sometimes empowering; sometimes dividing.

### 6. Anti-Defection Issues

The **Anti-Defection Law (1985)** prevents legislators from switching parties (with some exceptions). This was meant to prevent corruption and "horse trading."

But: it also reduces legislators' freedom to vote on issues. They must follow the party whip, even if they disagree.

Result: legislators are accountable to party leaders, not voters.

### 7. Lack of Transparency

Most Indian parties don't publish:
- Detailed accounts
- Internal election results
- Policy-making processes
- Internal debates

Some progress with RTI applicable to parties' public functions, but parties have resisted full transparency.

## Reforms Needed

Indian parties need several reforms:

(1) **Internal democracy** — leaders elected by members, candidates chosen democratically
(2) **Transparent finance** — full disclosure of donations and spending
(3) **Decriminalisation** — bar serious criminals from contesting
(4) **Limit dynasties** — promote merit-based leadership
(5) **Issue-based politics** — beyond identity mobilisation
(6) **Stronger anti-corruption mechanisms**
(7) **More citizen engagement** — make parties responsive
(8) **Reduce role of money** — public funding of elections, lower spending limits

These reforms have been discussed for decades. Progress is slow because parties themselves must reform — and they often don't want to.

## What Citizens Can Do

Despite the problems, citizens have power:

(1) **Vote thoughtfully** — based on policies and performance, not just identity or money
(2) **Reject candidates with serious criminal cases**
(3) **Support cleaner parties** — vote for those reforming themselves
(4) **Engage with parties** — join, criticise, push for reforms
(5) **Don't accept bribes** — vote your conscience
(6) **Speak up about money in politics**
(7) **Reward parties that perform** — re-elect those who deliver
(8) **Punish those who fail** — vote them out

Indian voters have repeatedly punished bad governments — they threw out Indira Gandhi in 1977 after the Emergency, threw out Congress in 1989, threw out BJP in 2004, brought BJP back in 2014, kept Modi in 2019, reduced his majority in 2024. Voters CAN make a difference.

## Conclusion — Parties Are Necessary, But Need Reform

Political parties are essential to modern democracy. Without them:
- Elections wouldn't work
- Governments couldn't be formed
- Policies couldn't be developed
- Citizens couldn't make informed choices

But specific parties are deeply flawed. Indian parties especially struggle with dynasties, criminalisation, lack of internal democracy, and money in politics.

The challenge: keep the institution (parties) while reforming the practice. Demand better parties through your vote, your engagement, your voice.

When you turn 18, you'll vote. The question is whether you'll vote thoughtfully — choosing candidates and parties based on what they ACTUALLY do, not what they merely promise; based on their record, not their slogans; based on policies, not just identity.

That kind of voting, multiplied across the country, gradually transforms parties. Indian parties are not what they should be — but they have improved over the decades, and they can improve more.

The work continues. Be part of it.

## Key Concepts to Remember

- **Political party**: group competing for power, with agreed policies and programmes.
- Three components: **leaders, active members, supporters/voters**.
- Why parties matter: simplify choice, pool resources, form governments, develop policies, hold government accountable, connect voters, aggregate interests, provide alternatives.
- **Party systems**: one-party (authoritarian), two-party (USA, UK), multi-party (India, Germany).
- Multi-party suits India's diversity.
- **National parties**: Congress, BJP, AAP, CPI, CPI(M), BSP, NPP.
- **Regional parties**: DMK, AIADMK, TMC, BJD, TDP, YSRCP, BRS, Shiv Sena, NCP, JDU, RJD, SP, Akali Dal, and many more.
- **Coalition era** since 1989 — NDA, UPA, etc.
- **Challenges**: dynastic politics, lack of internal democracy, money in politics, criminalisation, identity politics, anti-defection issues, lack of transparency.
- **Reforms needed**: internal democracy, transparent finance, decriminalisation, issue-based politics, citizen engagement.
- **Citizen's role**: vote thoughtfully, reject criminals, push for reforms.
