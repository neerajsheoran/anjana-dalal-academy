## A Common Confusion

If someone asks: "What do we call the Indian government — Union, Central, or Federal?" — the answer is **all three**.

- The Constitution uses the phrase **"Union of India"** — emphasising unity
- The phrase **"Central Government"** is common in everyday usage
- The technical political science term is **"Federal government"** — describing the system

These three names point to the same institution, viewed from different angles. The Constitution prefers "Union" to signal that India's federation is permanent — states cannot leave (unlike, say, the USSR, which broke apart).

## What Is Federalism?

**Federalism** is a system of government in which **power is divided between a central authority and various constituent units** of the country, and this division is **protected by the constitution**.

Two essential features:

1. **Two or more levels of government** — central + state (and often local).
2. **Each level has its own jurisdiction** — guaranteed by the constitution. Neither level can casually take powers from the other.

Most large modern democracies are federal:
- **USA** — 50 states
- **Germany** — 16 states (Länder)
- **Australia** — 6 states + territories
- **Canada** — 10 provinces + territories
- **Switzerland** — 26 cantons
- **Mexico** — 31 states
- **Brazil** — 26 states
- **India** — 28 states + 8 union territories

Smaller or culturally more uniform countries are often **unitary** (one strong central government):
- France
- Japan
- UK (though devolved to Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland)
- Israel

The key distinction: in a unitary system, the central government can take back powers from sub-units; in a federal system, it cannot (without constitutional amendment).

## Two Routes to Federalism

Federations arise in two ways.

### 1. "Coming Together" Federations

Independent states **come together** to form a larger federation. Examples:
- **USA** — 13 colonies came together (1776 onwards)
- **Switzerland** — cantons came together
- **Australia** — colonies came together (1901)

In these, states are usually equally powerful and the federal centre has limited powers (those given by states).

### 2. "Holding Together" Federations

A large country **devolves power** from a strong centre to constituent units. Examples:
- **India** — single country at independence, divided into states
- **Spain** — Catalonia, Basque region, etc., have autonomy
- **Belgium** — became federal in 1993

In these, the centre is usually stronger and the federation is designed to manage diversity.

**India is a "holding together" federation** — designed to hold together a vast, diverse country.

## The Indian Federation

India's federalism is described in the Constitution, especially in the **Seventh Schedule** (which lists subjects).

### Two-Tier Structure (1950)

Originally, India had a two-tier system:
- **Union (Central) Government**
- **State Governments**

### Three-Tier Structure (Post-1992)

The **73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments (1992)** added a third tier:
- **Local Government** — Panchayati Raj (rural) and Municipalities (urban)

This is now a constitutional level of government.

### Division of Subjects — Seventh Schedule

The Constitution divides subjects into three lists:

#### Union List (97 subjects)

Only the Centre can make laws on these:
- Defence
- Foreign affairs
- Atomic energy
- Currency
- Inter-state trade
- Communications
- Railways
- Banking, insurance
- Census
- National highways
- Citizenship

These are subjects of **national importance**.

#### State List (66 subjects)

Only the State can make laws on these:
- Police
- Public order
- Public health and sanitation
- Agriculture
- Irrigation (within state)
- Trade and commerce within state
- Local government
- Land revenue
- Forests (some)
- Fisheries

These are subjects of **state interest**.

#### Concurrent List (47 subjects)

Both Centre and State can make laws:
- Education
- Forests (mostly)
- Criminal law
- Marriage and divorce
- Trade unions
- Bankruptcy
- Adoption and succession

If both legislate on the same matter, **central law prevails**.

#### Residuary Subjects

Anything not in any list — **goes to the Centre**. This is unlike the USA (where residuary goes to states). It makes India a more **centralised** federation.

Examples of residuary subjects: cyber laws (added later), space, biotechnology.

## Key Features of Indian Federalism

### 1. Written Constitution

Powers of Union and States are written, not just convention.

### 2. Dual Government

Both Union and State governments exist independently, deriving authority from the Constitution.

### 3. Independent Judiciary

The Supreme Court interprets the Constitution and can settle disputes between Union and State.

### 4. Constitutional Amendments

Some amendments need approval of both Parliament AND a majority of states (e.g., changes to representation in Parliament).

### 5. Single Citizenship

Unlike USA (where you are a citizen of both your state and the nation), in India there is **one Indian citizenship**.

### 6. Integrated Judiciary

Unlike USA (separate state and federal courts), India has **one integrated judiciary** with the Supreme Court at the top.

### 7. Strong Centre

India's federation is **more centralised** than USA or Germany:
- Residuary powers with Centre
- Governors are appointed by Centre
- Some emergency provisions allow Centre to take over states
- President's Rule can dismiss state governments (Article 356)
- All India Services (IAS, IPS) connect Centre and States
- Money is mostly raised by Centre and shared with states

This is sometimes called **"quasi-federal"** — a federal structure with a strong centre.

### 8. Unitary in Spirit, Federal in Form?

Some scholars argue India is more unitary than federal. Reasons:
- Centre can change state boundaries
- Centre appoints governors who can recommend President's Rule
- Centre controls most revenue
- Constitution gives Centre clear superiority in many matters

Others argue India is genuinely federal because:
- Constitutional protection of state powers
- Strong state identities (linguistic, cultural)
- States make many real decisions
- Centre cannot easily abolish states

The truth: India is a **federation with strong central features** — pragmatic given India's size, diversity, and history of fragmentation.

## How Indian Federalism Has Worked

### Linguistic States (1956 onwards)

After independence, there were debates about how states should be drawn. The **States Reorganisation Commission (1955)** recommended drawing state boundaries on **linguistic lines**.

The **States Reorganisation Act (1956)** redrew the map:
- Each major language community got its own state (Tamil Nadu for Tamils, Andhra for Telugus, Karnataka for Kannadigas, etc.)
- This was controversial at the time (Nehru initially opposed)
- But it has worked — linguistic states have given each major community a political home

Today, India has **28 states and 8 union territories**, mostly drawn on linguistic lines.

### Language Policy

India has:
- **22 official languages** in the Eighth Schedule
- **No national language** (constitutionally)
- **Hindi and English** as official languages of the Union (Hindi was supposed to replace English by 1965, but Tamil resistance led to English continuing)
- **Three-language formula** in schools — Hindi-speakers learn a regional language; non-Hindi speakers learn Hindi
- **States can use any official language** for their internal affairs

This is one of the most accommodating language policies in the world.

### Centre-State Relations

These have gone through phases:

**Phase 1: 1947-1989 — Congress-dominated era.** Most states had Congress governments. Centre-state relations were relatively smooth (within one party).

**Phase 2: 1989 onwards — Coalition era.** No single party had majority. Many states had non-Congress governments. Coalitions at centre. Federalism became more genuine. States had more bargaining power.

**Phase 3: 2014 onwards — BJP-led era.** BJP gained majority. Some states with non-BJP governments complain of central agencies (CBI, ED, IT) being used disproportionately. GST has reduced state fiscal autonomy. Debates about centralisation are ongoing.

### Article 356 — President's Rule

The Centre can dismiss a state government if there's "failure of constitutional machinery." This power has been used:
- About **100+ times** since independence
- Sometimes legitimately (during genuine breakdown)
- Sometimes for political reasons (when central party didn't like state government)

The **Bommai case (1994)** restricted misuse — the Supreme Court ruled that President's Rule is subject to judicial review and requires the floor test in the legislature.

This restriction has reduced the misuse of Article 356.

### Sharing of Resources

Two major questions:

(1) **Who collects taxes?**
- Income tax, customs, GST — collected by Centre
- Stamp duty, alcohol excise — collected by States

(2) **How is money shared?**
- **Finance Commission** (constitutional body, set up every 5 years) decides how central tax revenues are shared between Centre and States. Latest is 15th Finance Commission.

States typically argue they should get a bigger share. The Centre argues it needs revenue for national schemes. This is an ongoing federal negotiation.

### GST — Major Change in 2017

The **Goods and Services Tax (GST)** introduced in 2017 was a major change:
- Replaced many state-level taxes (VAT, etc.) and central taxes (service tax, etc.)
- One national tax with shared revenue
- **GST Council** decides rates — has Centre + all state finance ministers
- Each state has voting power roughly proportional to size

This was a major federal reform — it required all states to agree to give up some tax autonomy. The compromise: states got a guaranteed minimum revenue growth for 5 years.

GST has helped Indian businesses (one tax instead of many state taxes) but reduced state fiscal autonomy. The debate continues.

## Decentralisation — The Third Tier

The **73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments (1992)** were among the most important democratic reforms.

### 73rd Amendment — Panchayati Raj

Created a **three-tier rural local government**:
- **Gram Panchayat** (village level)
- **Panchayat Samiti** (block level — middle of the tier)
- **Zila Parishad** (district level)

Features:
- **Elections every 5 years** (constitutionally mandatory now)
- **Reservation for SC/ST** based on population
- **One-third reservation for women** (many states have raised this to 50%)
- **State Finance Commission** to allocate funds
- **State Election Commission** to conduct elections

### 74th Amendment — Urban Local Bodies

Similar structure for cities:
- **Municipal Corporation** (large cities)
- **Municipal Council** (medium towns)
- **Nagar Panchayat** (small towns)

Same features — elections, reservations, finance commission.

### Why This Matters

These amendments brought democracy to the village and neighbourhood level. They:
- Empowered local communities
- Gave women a real political role (over 1 million elected women representatives — more than entire western Europe)
- Made local government accountable
- Strengthened federalism

But implementation has been uneven:
- Some states (Kerala, West Bengal) have strong panchayat traditions
- Others have weak local government with limited powers
- Funds are often inadequate
- State governments sometimes resist transferring real powers

The work of completing decentralisation continues.

## Areas of Federal Tension

Indian federalism has tensions in several areas:

### 1. Centre vs Opposition-Ruled States

States ruled by opposition parties often complain:
- Central agencies (CBI, ED, IT) used against state leaders
- Central funds delayed or denied
- Governors (appointed by Centre) interfering in state matters
- Central laws encroaching on state subjects

This is partly normal politics, partly genuine federal concern.

### 2. North vs South

Southern states (Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka, Andhra, Telangana) sometimes complain:
- They contribute more taxes than they receive in central allocations
- Hindi imposition is resisted
- Some central policies favour Hindi-speaking states

This is the "Northern India vs Southern India" dimension of Indian federalism.

### 3. River Water Disputes

Rivers cross state boundaries. Disputes are common:
- Cauvery (Tamil Nadu vs Karnataka)
- Krishna (multiple states)
- Yamuna (Delhi, Haryana, UP)
- Mahanadi (Odisha vs Chhattisgarh)

These are settled by tribunals, sometimes ineffectively. Politics often makes resolution harder.

### 4. Border Disputes

Some states have border disputes:
- Assam-Mizoram
- Karnataka-Maharashtra (Belgaum)
- Punjab-Haryana (Chandigarh)

These are mostly low-intensity but occasionally flare up.

### 5. Special Status Debates

The revocation of **Article 370** (Jammu & Kashmir's special status) in **2019** was a major federal event. J&K was downgraded from a state to a Union Territory.

Critics argued it weakened federalism. Supporters argued it brought J&K into mainstream Indian federation.

Some other states (Nagaland, Mizoram) have special provisions. Sixth Schedule areas in Northeast have unique autonomy.

### 6. Fiscal Federalism

Ongoing debate:
- Should states get more tax-sharing?
- Should the Centre run more national schemes (currently many)?
- How much fiscal autonomy do states have post-GST?

## The Strengths of Indian Federalism

Despite tensions, Indian federalism has worked remarkably:

1. **India has stayed united** — no successful separatist movements have produced new countries (unlike many post-colonial countries that broke apart)
2. **Linguistic states** have accommodated diversity
3. **State governments deliver many services** — schools, hospitals, roads, agriculture
4. **Regional parties** flourish — adding voice
5. **Local democracy** through panchayats reaches villages
6. **Coalition era** strengthened federalism
7. **Supreme Court** has protected federal balance
8. **Cultural diversity** preserved alongside political unity

For one of the world's largest, most diverse countries, this is a major achievement.

## Conclusion

Indian federalism is unique — neither pure federal like USA, nor unitary like France. It is designed for India's specific challenges: huge size, immense diversity, complex history.

The Constitution makers crafted a federation strong enough to hold together but flexible enough to accommodate differences. It has evolved over 75+ years, mostly successfully.

The work continues. Local democracy needs strengthening. Centre-State relations need constant tending. New issues (data localisation, cyber federalism, climate response) require federal coordination.

When you grow up and engage with politics, you'll see federalism in action — in news, in debates, in your own state's relationship with Delhi. Care about it. Understand it. Defend it when needed.

India's federal balance is one of its most precious institutional achievements. Like the Constitution itself, it must be renewed by every generation.

## Key Concepts to Remember

- **Federalism**: power-sharing between central authority and constituent units, protected by constitution.
- **"Coming together" vs "holding together"** federations — India is the latter.
- **Three lists** in Seventh Schedule: Union, State, Concurrent. **Residuary** powers to Centre.
- India is **quasi-federal** — federal structure with strong central features.
- **Linguistic states** since 1956 — accommodating language diversity.
- **22 official languages** in Eighth Schedule; **no national language**; Hindi + English at Union level.
- **Three-language formula** in schools.
- **Article 356** (President's Rule) — used 100+ times; Bommai case (1994) restricted misuse.
- **GST (2017)** — major federal reform; GST Council with all state FMs.
- **73rd and 74th Amendments (1992)** — three-tier government, local democracy.
- **Reservations in panchayats** — 1/3 to 1/2 for women; based on population for SC/ST.
- Tensions: Centre vs opposition-ruled states, north vs south, river disputes, GST, special status.
- Indian federalism has kept the country united despite enormous diversity.
