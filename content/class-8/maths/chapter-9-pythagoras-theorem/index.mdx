## 2.1 Doubling a Square

In the **Baudhyana Shulba-Sutra** (around 800 BCE), the renowned Indian scholar Baudhyana posed a deceptively simple challenge:

**Given a square, how do you construct another square with exactly double its area?**

Your instinct might be to double the side length. But if the original square has side a, doubling the side gives side 2a and area (2a)² = 4a². That is **four times** the original, not two!

Baudhyana's brilliant solution (Verse 1.9):

> **Key idea:** "The square constructed on the diagonal of a given square has exactly double the area of that given square."

Simply draw a new square on the **diagonal** of the original. Its area is precisely **twice** the original!

![A square with a tilted square drawn on its diagonal, showing the diagonal square has double the area](doubling-square-diagonal.png)

**Why does this hold?** Sketch horizontal and vertical lines through the tilted square. You will see the original square splits into 2 identical right triangles, while the diagonal square splits into 4 of those same triangles. Since 4 is twice 2, the area doubles.

We can continue building a chain of growing squares:
- Starting square: 2 triangles
- Diagonal square: 4 triangles (double the area)
- Next diagonal square: 8 triangles (four times the original)

## 2.2 Halving a Square

To build a square with **half** the area of a larger square, reverse the process: connect the **midpoints** of the four sides of the larger square to form a tilted inner square.

This inner square covers exactly **half** the area of the outer one. Drawing horizontal and vertical lines through the figure confirms that the inner square occupies exactly half the total space.

## 2.3 Hypotenuse of an Isosceles Right Triangle

In a **right triangle**, the longest side -- the one facing the right angle -- is called the **hypotenuse**.

Consider an isosceles right triangle where both shorter sides measure 1 unit. What length is its hypotenuse?

Two such triangles fit together to form a unit square. The square erected on the hypotenuse has area = 2 x the original unit square = **2 sq. units**.

If the hypotenuse is c, then c² = 2, giving **c = sqrt(2)**.

### Pinning Down sqrt(2)

We can zero in on its value step by step:
- 1² = 1 and 2² = 4, so **1 < sqrt(2) < 2**
- 1.4² = 1.96 and 1.5² = 2.25, so **1.4 < sqrt(2) < 1.5**
- 1.41² = 1.9881 and 1.42² = 2.0164, so **1.41 < sqrt(2) < 1.42**
- 1.414² = 1.999396 and 1.415² = 2.002225, so **1.414 < sqrt(2) < 1.415**

sqrt(2) = 1.41421356... The digits never end and never repeat!

### Why sqrt(2) Cannot Be a Fraction

Can sqrt(2) be expressed as some fraction m/n in lowest terms? **No.**

If 2 = m²/n², then m² = 2n². Now count how many times the prime 2 divides each side. In m², the factor 2 appears an even number of times. In 2n², it appears an odd number of times. An even number can never equal an odd number -- contradiction. So sqrt(2) is not a fraction.

> **Key idea:** sqrt(2) is an **irrational number** -- it has no exact fractional form. Its decimal expansion is infinite and non-repeating. This stunning fact was proved rigorously by **Euclid** (around 300 BCE).

### General Rule for Isosceles Right Triangles

When both equal sides have length **a** and the hypotenuse is **c**:

**c² = 2a²** or equivalently **c = a x sqrt(2)**

**Example:** If a = 9, then c² = 2 x 81 = 162. Since 12² = 144 and 13² = 169, the hypotenuse lies between 12 and 13 units.

**Example:** If hypotenuse c = sqrt(50), then c² = 50, so 2a² = 50, giving a² = 25, and **a = 5**.

## 2.4 Combining Two Different Squares

We know how to merge two identical squares into one. But what about two squares of **different** sizes?

Baudhyana's Shulba-Sutra (Verse 1.12) provides an extraordinary answer:

> **Key idea:** "The area produced by the diagonal equals the combined areas produced by the two perpendicular sides."

To merge two differently-sized squares:
1. Form a **right-angled triangle** whose two perpendicular sides match the sides of the two given squares.
2. The square built on the **hypotenuse** of this triangle has area equal to the sum of the two original square areas.

---

## 2.5 The Baudhayana-Pythagoras Theorem

This insight crystallises into the celebrated theorem:

**In a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.**

With legs **a** and **b** and hypotenuse **c**:

**a² + b² = c²**

![Right triangle with legs a and b, hypotenuse c, and squares drawn on each side showing a-squared plus b-squared equals c-squared](pythagoras-theorem-visual-proof.png)

This result is known as the **Baudhayana-Pythagoras Theorem**. Baudhyana recorded it in his Shulba-Sutra around 800 BCE -- predating the Greek mathematician Pythagoras (c. 570-495 BCE) by roughly three centuries.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** A right triangle has legs 6 and 8. Find the hypotenuse.
- c² = 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100
- c = sqrt(100) = **10**

**Example 2:** A right triangle has legs 9 and 12. Find the hypotenuse.
- c² = 9² + 12² = 81 + 144 = 225
- c = sqrt(225) = **15**

**Example 3:** A right triangle has hypotenuse 13 and one leg 5. Find the other leg.
- 5² + b² = 13²
- 25 + b² = 169
- b² = 144
- b = **12**

**Example 4:** A right triangle has legs 2 and 3. Find the hypotenuse.
- c² = 2² + 3² = 4 + 9 = 13
- c = sqrt(13) (approximately 3.606)

## Pythagorean Triples

A **Pythagorean triple** is a set of three whole numbers (a, b, c) satisfying a² + b² = c².

Well-known Pythagorean triples:
- **(3, 4, 5):** 9 + 16 = 25
- **(5, 12, 13):** 25 + 144 = 169
- **(8, 15, 17):** 64 + 225 = 289
- **(7, 24, 25):** 49 + 576 = 625

If (a, b, c) is a triple, then **(ka, kb, kc)** is also a triple for any positive number k. From (3, 4, 5) we get (6, 8, 10), (9, 12, 15), (12, 16, 20), and infinitely more.

> **Key idea:** Pythagorean triples give right triangles with all whole-number sides. Lists of such triples appear on Babylonian clay tablets from around 1800 BCE, showing that this knowledge is truly ancient.

---

## The Converse of the Theorem

The **converse** holds equally well: if three sides of a triangle satisfy a² + b² = c², then the triangle must contain a right angle (opposite the longest side c).

**Example:** Does a triangle with sides 9, 12, 15 form a right triangle?
- 9² + 12² = 81 + 144 = 225 = 15²
- **Yes**, it is a right triangle.

**Example:** Does a triangle with sides 4, 6, 8 form a right triangle?
- 4² + 6² = 16 + 36 = 52, but 8² = 64
- 52 does not equal 64, so **no**, it is not a right triangle.

## Applications

### Finding Distances

**Example:** A bamboo pole of length 13 m leans against a building. Its base rests 5 m from the building. How high does the pole reach?
- 5² + h² = 13²
- 25 + h² = 169
- h² = 144
- h = **12 m**

**Example:** From a crossroad, Aarav cycles 9 km south while Bharat cycles 12 km west. How far apart are they?
- d² = 9² + 12² = 81 + 144 = 225
- d = **15 km**

## Summary

- The **Baudhayana-Pythagoras Theorem** states: In a right triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c, **a² + b² = c²**.
- Baudhyana stated this in the **Shulba-Sutra** (c. 800 BCE), one of the earliest recorded versions.
- The diagonal of a square with side a has length **a x sqrt(2)**, and the square on the diagonal has **double** the area.
- **sqrt(2) is irrational** -- it cannot be written as a fraction and has an infinite, non-repeating decimal.
- **Pythagorean triples** such as (3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13) are whole-number solutions to the theorem.
- The **converse** also holds: if a² + b² = c², the triangle is right-angled.
- The theorem is used in measuring distances, heights, and verifying right angles in construction.
