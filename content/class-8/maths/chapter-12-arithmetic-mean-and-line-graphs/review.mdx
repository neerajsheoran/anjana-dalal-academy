## Key Concepts

- The **mean** (arithmetic average) = sum of all values / number of values
- The mean is the **balance point** where total gap below = total gap above
- Adding a value above the mean **raises** it; adding below **lowers** it; adding exactly the mean keeps it unchanged
- Adding a constant k to every value shifts the mean by **k**
- Multiplying every value by k scales the mean by **k**
- The **median** is the middle value in sorted data; for even count, average the two central values
- The median is more **resistant to outliers** than the mean
- With **frequency tables**: Mean = sum of (value x frequency) / total frequency
- The mean need not match any actual data value
- **Line graphs** connect plotted data points to show trends over time
- A rising segment signals growth; a falling segment signals decline; steepness shows rate of change
- When comparing two data sets, check both the **average** and the **spread** (consistency)
- Two groups can share the same average yet differ in reliability

## Important Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Mean (average) | Sum of all values divided by the count of values |
| Median | The middle value when data is sorted; average of two middle values if count is even |
| Frequency | How many times a particular value appears in the data |
| Line graph | A graph connecting data points with straight lines to show trends |
| Outlier | A data value that stands out as unusually high or low |
| Central tendency | A single value that represents the centre of a data set (mean, median) |

## Remember These

- Mean = balance point: the sum of distances below equals the sum of distances above
- If one data value was recorded incorrectly, fix the total by adjusting by the error amount, then recalculate the mean
- To find an unknown value when the mean is given: Unknown = (Mean x Count) - Sum of known values
- Line graphs are best for showing **change over time** -- bar graphs are better for comparing categories
- Same average does not mean same performance -- always consider the **spread** of data
