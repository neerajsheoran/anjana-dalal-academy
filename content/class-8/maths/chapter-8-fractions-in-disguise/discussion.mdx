## Understanding Percentages

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says percentages are "fractions in disguise." So a percentage is just a fraction with 100 in the bottom?

**Mother:** Exactly, beta. Saying 30 percent is the same as saying 30 out of 100, which equals the fraction 3/10. The percentage is simply a friendlier costume for the fraction, making comparisons much easier.

**Child:** Why does 100 work so well as the standard denominator?

**Mother:** Because it is large enough to capture fine differences -- you can easily tell apart 67 percent from 68 percent -- but small enough that our brains can grasp the meaning instantly. And since we count in base 10, percentages convert to decimals effortlessly. 30 percent is just 0.30. That double usefulness makes percentages the universal language of comparison.

**Child:** Kautilya's Arthashastra from 2300 years ago already used "per hundred" for interest rates. India has been thinking in percentages since ancient times!

**Mother:** And today the concept is everywhere -- board exam scores, GST rates, cricket strike rates, nutrient labels on Maggi packets. Understanding percentages is a life skill, not just a maths skill.

## Profit, Loss, and Percentage

**Child:** Maa, why is profit percentage always calculated on the cost price? Why not the selling price?

**Mother:** Because the cost price is your starting investment -- the money you actually put in. If you buy a painting for Rs 4500 and sell it for Rs 5175, your profit is Rs 675. Measured against your investment of 4500, that is 15 percent. If you calculated on the selling price of 5175, you would get about 13 percent, which understates your actual return.

**Child:** So if I buy something for 1500 rupees and want a 25 percent profit, I sell for 1500 times 1.25, which is 1875 rupees.

**Mother:** Right. The formula SP equals CP times (1 plus profit percent over 100) is really the distributive property in disguise -- multiplying by (1 plus something) expands into the original amount plus the profit.

**Child:** That links right back to Chapter 6 on distributivity!

**Mother:** Everything in mathematics connects. When your Chachi runs her boutique in Jaipur, she calculates markup percentages on every item. When she offers a Navratri sale, she knows exactly how much she can discount and still make profit. That is all this chapter in real life.

## Simple Interest

**Child:** The simple interest formula SI equals PRT over 100 -- is that just proportional reasoning from Chapter 7?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Interest is directly proportional to the principal, to the rate, and to the time. Double any one while keeping the others fixed, and the interest doubles. It is a three-way direct proportion.

**Child:** So if Deepak deposits 8000 rupees at 6 percent for 4 years, the interest is 8000 times 6 times 4 divided by 100, which is 1920 rupees. Total amount is 9920 rupees.

**Mother:** With simple interest, each year gives the same interest -- 480 rupees per year in this case. That constancy is why it is called "simple." Real banks often use compound interest, where you earn interest on previous interest too. You will learn that later.

**Child:** Is there a quick mental shortcut for estimating interest?

**Mother:** Yes. Use 10 percent as your anchor. For 6 percent on 8000: 10 percent is 800, so 1 percent is 80, and 6 percent is 480 per year. Over 4 years, that is 1920. Quick and reliable.

## Discounts and GST

**Child:** When a shop says "Flat 20 percent off," the discount is on the marked price. But the shopkeeper might still be making profit, right?

**Mother:** Almost always. Shopkeepers mark up prices before announcing discounts. If a kurta costs the shopkeeper 700 rupees and she marks it at 1200 rupees, then a 20 percent discount brings the selling price to 960 rupees. Her profit is 260 rupees, which is about 37 percent on cost price -- a healthy margin even after the "sale."

**Child:** So the percentage discount advertised is not the same as the shopkeeper's loss. It is calculated on different bases.

**Mother:** Exactly. This is why understanding the **base** of any percentage is critical. When you see "70 percent off!" during an end-of-season sale, always ask: 70 percent of what? The original marked price might have been inflated to begin with.

**Child:** And GST is charged on top of the selling price? So a laptop at 45,000 rupees with 18 percent GST becomes 53,100 rupees.

**Mother:** GST -- Goods and Services Tax -- replaced a tangle of older indirect taxes in India in 2017. Knowing how it adds to prices is essential financial literacy. The bill amount equals selling price times (1 plus GST percent over 100).

## Comparing Percentages Thoughtfully

**Child:** Maa, the chapter warns that 20 percent of 200 grams can be more than 30 percent of 120 grams. That seems strange at first.

**Mother:** Let us check. 20 percent of 200 is 40 grams. 30 percent of 120 is 36 grams. So even though 20 percent sounds smaller than 30 percent, the actual amount depends on the total quantity you started with.

**Child:** So percentages are relative, not absolute. You cannot compare them without knowing what wholes they refer to.

**Mother:** This trips up people all the time, even adults. A news headline might say "Village A saw a 25 percent rise in rainfall" and "Village B saw a 10 percent rise." But if Village A normally gets 200 mm and Village B normally gets 1000 mm, the actual increases are 50 mm versus 100 mm -- Village B got far more extra rain despite the lower percentage.

**Child:** Cow's milk is about 87 percent water. So a 500 mL packet has about 435 mL of water?

**Mother:** Exactly. And the Earth's surface is 71 percent water -- which is why our planet looks blue from space. Percentages help you see past the surface of everyday facts, beta. They are truly fractions in disguise.
