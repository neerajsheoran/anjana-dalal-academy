## 5.1 Is This a Multiple Of?

### Expressing Numbers as Sums of Consecutive Numbers

Many numbers can be written as the sum of consecutive numbers:

- 9 = 4 + 5
- 15 = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5
- 18 = 5 + 6 + 7
- 21 = 10 + 11 = 6 + 7 + 8 = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6

Some interesting questions to explore:
- Can every natural number be expressed as a sum of consecutive numbers?
- Which numbers can be written in multiple ways?
- Every **odd number** can be written as a sum of two consecutive numbers (for instance, 11 = 5 + 6, since any two consecutive numbers differ by 1 and their sum is always odd).

---

### Parity: The Even-Odd Nature of Numbers

Pick any 4 consecutive numbers, say 5, 6, 7, 8. Place "+" and "-" signs between them in every possible way. There are **8 combinations**:

- 5 + 6 + 7 + 8 = 26
- 5 + 6 + 7 - 8 = 10
- 5 + 6 - 7 + 8 = 12
- 5 + 6 - 7 - 8 = -4
- 5 - 6 + 7 + 8 = 14
- 5 - 6 + 7 - 8 = -2
- 5 - 6 - 7 + 8 = 0
- 5 - 6 - 7 - 8 = -16

Observe carefully: **every result is an even number!**

> **Key idea:** Regardless of which 4 consecutive numbers you pick, and regardless of which signs you choose, the result is always **even**. This occurs because switching a "+" to a "-" (or vice versa) always changes the value by an even amount (twice that number). So all 8 expressions share the same **parity** (all even or all odd).

### The Algebraic Reason

**Why does this work?** The parity of a + b and a - b is always identical. Here is why:
- (a + b) - (a - b) = 2b, which is always even

Since their difference is even, both expressions must have the same parity. This reasoning extends to any number of terms: all expressions a +/- b +/- c +/- d share the same parity.

---

### Making It Even

Without computing, you can determine if an expression is always even or odd using parity rules:

- **even + even = even**
- **odd + odd = even**
- **even + odd = odd**
- **even x anything = even**

**Examples of always-even expressions:**
- **6m + 4n** — both terms are even, so the sum is even
- **2p + 2q** — equals 2(p + q), always has factor 2
- **17k - 9k** — equals 8k, which is always even

**Example that is NOT always even:**
- **n² + 3** — if n = 4, then n² + 3 = 19 (odd); if n = 5, then n² + 3 = 28 (even)

---

### Adding Evens to Get Multiples of 4

When do two even numbers add up to give a multiple of 4?

- **Multiples of 4 + Multiples of 4 = Multiple of 4**
  - 4p + 4q = 4(p + q). Example: 8 + 20 = 28
- **Non-multiples of 4 (but even) + Non-multiples of 4 (but even) = Multiple of 4**
  - (4p + 2) + (4q + 2) = 4(p + q + 1). Example: 10 + 14 = 24
- **Multiple of 4 + Non-multiple of 4 (but even) = NOT a multiple of 4**
  - 4p + (4q + 2) = 4(p + q) + 2. Example: 12 + 10 = 22

> **Key idea:** Two even numbers sum to a multiple of 4 only when both are multiples of 4, or both are even but NOT multiples of 4. Mixing one of each type never gives a multiple of 4.

### Always, Sometimes, or Never True

For statements about divisibility, determine whether each is **always true**, **sometimes true**, or **never true**:

**1. If 6 divides two numbers, it divides their sum.** — **Always true.**
- If the two numbers are 6a and 6b, then 6a + 6b = 6(a + b), which is divisible by 6.
- In general: if a divides M and a divides N, then a divides M + N and a divides M - N.

**2. If a number is divisible by 10, then 10 divides any two numbers that add up to it.** — **Sometimes true.**
- 60 = 30 + 30 (both divisible by 10? No, 30 is divisible by 10? Yes.)
- But 60 = 37 + 23 (neither 37 nor 23 is divisible by 10)

**3. If a number is divisible by 5, all its multiples are divisible by 5.** — **Always true.**
- If the number is 5j, then any multiple is 5j x m = 5(jm), which has 5 as a factor.

**4. If a number is divisible by 18, it is divisible by all factors of 18.** — **Always true.**
- In general: if A is divisible by k, then A is divisible by every factor of k.

**5. If a number is divisible by 5, it is divisible by any multiple of 5.** — **Sometimes true.**
- 30 (= 5 x 6) is divisible by 15 (= 5 x 3) since 3 divides 6.
- But 30 is NOT divisible by 20 (= 5 x 4) since 4 does not divide 6.

---

### What Remains?

Numbers that leave a **remainder of 4** when divided by 7 can be written as **7k + 4** (where k = 0, 1, 2, 3, ...).

For k = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4: the numbers are 4, 11, 18, 25, 32, ...

These can also be written as **7k - 3** (where k = 1, 2, 3, ...) since being "4 more" than a multiple of 7 is the same as being "3 less" than the next multiple of 7.

> **Key idea:** The remainder when dividing by n sorts all numbers into n groups. Numbers in the same group behave identically under many arithmetic operations.

## 5.2 Quick Divisibility Checks

### Divisibility by 10, 5, and 2

Any number can be expanded: for example, 6293 = 6 x 1000 + 2 x 100 + 9 x 10 + 3 x 1.

Since 1000, 100, and 10 are all multiples of 10, only the **units digit** determines divisibility by 10.

- **Divisible by 10:** units digit is **0**
- **Divisible by 5:** units digit is **0 or 5**
- **Divisible by 2:** units digit is **0, 2, 4, 6, or 8** (even)

### Divisibility by 4

Since 100, 1000, 10000, ... are all divisible by 4, only the **last two digits** matter. A number is divisible by 4 when the number formed by its last two digits is divisible by 4.

### Divisibility by 8

Since 1000, 10000, ... are all divisible by 8, only the **last three digits** matter. A number is divisible by 8 when the number formed by its last three digits is divisible by 8.

---

### Divisibility by 9: The Digit Sum Method

Notice these facts:
- 10 = 9 + 1
- 100 = 99 + 1
- 1000 = 999 + 1
- 10000 = 9999 + 1

Every place value is **1 more than a multiple of 9**.

So for a number like 853:
- 853 = 8 x (99 + 1) + 5 x (9 + 1) + 3 x 1
- = (8 x 99 + 5 x 9) + (8 + 5 + 3)
- = (a multiple of 9) + (sum of digits)

The remainder when 853 is divided by 9 equals the remainder when **8 + 5 + 3 = 16** is divided by 9, which equals the remainder when **1 + 6 = 7** is divided by 9. So 853 leaves a remainder of **7** when divided by 9.

> **Key idea:** A number is divisible by 9 **if and only if** the sum of its digits is divisible by 9. You can repeatedly add digits until you get a single digit — this is the remainder when divided by 9 (with 9 itself giving remainder 0).

**Example:** Is 4518 divisible by 9?
- Sum of digits: 4 + 5 + 1 + 8 = 18
- Sum again: 1 + 8 = 9
- Since we get 9, the number IS divisible by 9.

---

### Divisibility by 3

The same logic applies to 3! Since 9 is a multiple of 3, and each place value leaves remainder 1 when divided by 3:

**A number is divisible by 3 if and only if the sum of its digits is divisible by 3.**

**Example:** Is 8631 divisible by 3?
- Sum of digits: 8 + 6 + 3 + 1 = 18
- 18 is divisible by 3, so yes, 8631 is divisible by 3.

---

### Divisibility by 11

For divisibility by 11, we use the **alternating sum** of digits (alternately add and subtract starting from the right).

Notice:
- 10 = 11 - 1 (remainder -1)
- 100 = 99 + 1 (remainder +1)
- 1000 = 1001 - 1 (remainder -1)

The remainders alternate: +1, -1, +1, -1, ...

So for a number like 59917:
- Alternating sum = 7 - 1 + 9 - 9 + 5 = 11
- 11 is divisible by 11, so 59917 is divisible by 11.

> **Key idea:** A number is divisible by 11 when the **alternating sum** of its digits (starting from the units place: add, subtract, add, subtract, ...) is divisible by 11 (or equals 0).

---

## Divisibility Rules at a Glance

| Divisor | Rule |
|---|---|
| **2** | Last digit is even (0, 2, 4, 6, 8) |
| **3** | Sum of digits is divisible by 3 |
| **4** | Last two digits form a number divisible by 4 |
| **5** | Last digit is 0 or 5 |
| **6** | Divisible by both 2 and 3 |
| **8** | Last three digits form a number divisible by 8 |
| **9** | Sum of digits is divisible by 9 |
| **10** | Last digit is 0 |
| **11** | Alternating sum of digits is divisible by 11 |

## Summary

- The **parity** (even/odd nature) of expressions like a +/- b +/- c +/- d depends only on the parities of a, b, c, d — not on the choice of + or - signs.
- **Divisibility properties** can be proved algebraically: if a divides M and N, then a divides M + N and M - N.
- If A is divisible by k, then A is divisible by all **factors** of k, and all **multiples** of A are divisible by k.
- Numbers with the same remainder when divided by n can be expressed as **nk + r**.
- **Divisibility tests** for 2, 4, 5, 8, 10 depend on the last few digits; tests for 3 and 9 use the digit sum; and the test for 11 uses the alternating digit sum.
- These rules work because of how place values relate to the divisor (each place value being a multiple of the divisor, or 1 more, or alternating between +1 and -1).
