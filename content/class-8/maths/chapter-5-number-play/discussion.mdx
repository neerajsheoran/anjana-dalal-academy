## Even-Odd Reasoning: A Powerful Shortcut

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says that if you take any 4 consecutive numbers and put plus or minus signs between them, the answer is always even. How can that be true for EVERY possible combination?

**Mother:** It comes down to parity, beta — whether a number is even or odd. The key insight is this: when you switch a plus sign to a minus sign (or the other way around), the result changes by twice that number. Since twice anything is always even, every switch preserves parity. All eight combinations must therefore be either all even or all odd.

**Child:** So the parity of a + b is always the same as a - b, because their difference is 2b, which is even?

**Mother:** Exactly. And this works for any number of terms, not just two. This kind of reasoning is a powerful shortcut in competitive maths. Many Olympiad problems can be cracked just by checking parity.

**Child:** Like if someone asks whether 5 +/- 8 +/- 13 +/- 2 can equal 7. I check: 5 + 8 + 13 + 2 = 28, which is even. Since all combinations share the same parity, they are all even. And 7 is odd — so it is impossible!

**Mother:** Brilliant reasoning! You did not have to test all 8 combinations. You just needed one quick addition and a parity check. That is the beauty of mathematical thinking — it saves enormous effort.

## Understanding Divisibility Rules

**Child:** Maa, there are rules for divisibility by 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 11 — so many! How do I organise them in my head?

**Mother:** Group them by the underlying logic, beta. The first group — rules for 2, 4, 5, 8, and 10 — all depend on the last few digits. That is because higher place values (100, 1000, etc.) are all divisible by these numbers, so only the trailing digits matter.

**Child:** So for 2 and 5, just the last digit matters. For 4, the last two digits. For 8, the last three digits.

**Mother:** The second group — rules for 3 and 9 — use the digit sum. This works because every power of 10 is exactly 1 more than a multiple of 9. So 10 = 9 + 1, 100 = 99 + 1, 1000 = 999 + 1. When you divide by 9, only the sum of the digits survives.

**Child:** And since 9 is itself a multiple of 3, the same digit sum trick works for 3 as well!

**Mother:** The third group — the rule for 11 — uses the alternating digit sum. This is because the remainders of powers of 10 when divided by 11 alternate between +1 and -1. So 10 gives -1, 100 gives +1, 1000 gives -1, and so on.

**Child:** For 59917: alternating from the right gives 7 - 1 + 9 - 9 + 5 = 11. Since 11 is divisible by 11, the number is divisible by 11. That is elegant!

**Mother:** In everyday life, these rules are more useful than you might think. When Maa checks a bill at the grocery store — say ₹891 — I can instantly tell it is divisible by 9 because 8 + 9 + 1 = 18, and 1 + 8 = 9. Quick mental checks like these help catch billing errors.

## Thinking About Divisibility Properties

**Child:** Maa, the chapter asks whether certain statements are always, sometimes, or never true. That needs really careful thinking.

**Mother:** This is training you to reason like a mathematician, beta. Take the statement "If 6 divides two numbers, then 6 divides their sum." If the numbers are 6a and 6b, their sum is 6(a + b). Since 6 is a factor, the statement is always true.

**Child:** But "if a number is divisible by 10, then 10 divides any two parts that add up to it" — that is only sometimes true. 60 = 30 + 30 works, but 60 = 37 + 23 does not.

**Mother:** Exactly. The key distinction is between "always" and "sometimes." For "always true," there must be no exceptions at all — even a single counterexample downgrades it to "sometimes true."

**Child:** And "if a number is divisible by 18, it is divisible by all factors of 18" — that is always true because the factors of 18 (1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18) are all built into 18's structure.

**Mother:** Right. If 18 divides a number, then so do 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, and 18 — every factor of 18. This principle extends generally: if k divides A, then every factor of k also divides A. Keep this rule in your toolkit — it appears in many exam problems.

## Remainders and Patterns

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says numbers leaving remainder 4 when divided by 7 can be written as 7k + 4. This feels like algebra meeting number theory.

**Mother:** It is! This is called modular arithmetic, and it is one of the most powerful ideas in modern mathematics. When you sort numbers by their remainder, you create groups that behave predictably under addition and multiplication.

**Child:** So the "remainder 4" group when dividing by 7 includes 4, 11, 18, 25, 32, ... They form an arithmetic sequence with common difference 7.

**Mother:** And if you add two numbers from the "remainder 4" group — say 11 and 18 — you get 29. Dividing 29 by 7 gives remainder 1. So the sum of two "remainder 4" numbers is always a "remainder 1" number. The remainders have their own consistent arithmetic!

**Child:** That is like a little number system within the bigger one! Is this what is used in computer security?

**Mother:** Exactly, beta! Modern encryption — the kind that protects your Paytm transactions and Aadhaar data — is based on modular arithmetic with very large prime numbers. The concepts in this chapter are literally the foundation of digital security for billions of people.

## When Evens Add Up to Multiples of 4

**Child:** Maa, the section about when two even numbers add to a multiple of 4 has a neat pattern. Both must be multiples of 4, or both must be even non-multiples of 4.

**Mother:** Think of even numbers as coming in two flavours. Type A: multiples of 4, like 4, 8, 12, 16, 20. Type B: even numbers that are not multiples of 4, like 2, 6, 10, 14, 18. Type A can be written as 4p, Type B as 4p + 2.

**Child:** If I add two Type A numbers: 4p + 4q = 4(p + q), which is a multiple of 4. And two Type B numbers: (4p + 2) + (4q + 2) = 4(p + q + 1), which is also a multiple of 4!

**Mother:** But mix them — 4p + (4q + 2) = 4(p + q) + 2 — and you get a number that is even but NOT a multiple of 4. This is a perfect example of how algebraic reasoning proves something that would be exhausting to verify by testing numbers one by one.

**Child:** This feels connected to the parity discussion — it is like going deeper, from "even or odd" to "multiple of 4 or not."

**Mother:** You have spotted something profound! This idea of refining classifications — first by 2, then by 4, then by 8 — is exactly how mathematicians think. Each level of refinement reveals more structure. What starts as a simple even-odd question in Class 8 connects to ideas that researchers use in advanced number theory. Mathematics is one long, beautiful chain.
