## The Distributive Property

**Child:** Maa, the chapter title sounds like a riddle -- "We Distribute, Yet Things Multiply." What does distributing have to do with multiplication?

**Mother:** Think about it like this, beta. Suppose you want to buy 8 packets of biscuits at Rs 99 each. Instead of multiplying 8 by 99, you can think of 99 as 100 minus 1. So 8 times 99 becomes 8 times 100 minus 8 times 1, which is 800 minus 8, giving 792. You split -- or distributed -- the multiplication across simpler pieces.

**Child:** Oh! So a times (b plus c) equals ab plus ac. We break one factor into parts, multiply each part, and add the results.

**Mother:** Exactly. And this is not some modern textbook trick. Brahmagupta wrote about this technique back in 628 CE. He called it "khanda-gunanam" -- multiplication by segments. Shopkeepers across India have been doing this kind of mental arithmetic for centuries when making quick calculations at their counters.

**Child:** The rectangle picture helps too -- if you have a rows and (b plus c) columns of tiles, you can split the floor into two smaller rectangles with areas ab and ac.

**Mother:** That geometric view is how Euclid understood it in ancient Greece. Whether you see it as tiles on a floor or numbers in your head, the underlying truth is the same.

## Important Identities

**Child:** Maa, the three big identities -- (a plus b) squared, (a minus b) squared, and (a plus b)(a minus b) -- seem to come up everywhere. Why are they so special?

**Mother:** Because they turn difficult calculations into simple ones, and they appear in exams from Class 8 all the way through JEE. Once you truly understand them, they become your best friends in algebra.

**Child:** Let me try one. To find 72 squared, I write it as (70 plus 2) squared. That gives 4900 plus 280 plus 4, which is 5184. Much faster than long multiplication!

**Mother:** Beautiful. Now try 97 squared using the difference identity.

**Child:** (100 minus 3) squared equals 10000 minus 600 plus 9, which is 9409. And for 47 times 53, that is (50 minus 3)(50 plus 3), so I get 2500 minus 9 equals 2491.

**Mother:** Wonderful work. Bhaskaracharya's Lilavati, written in 1150 CE, is full of such elegant uses of these identities, presented as beautiful Sanskrit verses. Our mathematical heritage runs deep.

## Fast Multiplication Tricks

**Child:** The method for multiplying by 11 is so satisfying! For 5263 times 11, I just add neighbouring digits: the answer is 57893. But why does it work?

**Mother:** When you multiply by 11, you are really multiplying by 10 plus 1. So each digit in the result comes from adding two adjacent digits of the original number. The tens-place copy and the ones-place copy overlap, and their sum gives the new digits.

**Child:** And multiplying by 99 is just multiplying by 100 and subtracting. So 43 times 99 equals 4300 minus 43, which is 4257.

**Mother:** These shortcuts are priceless during timed exams. Even a few seconds saved per calculation adds up over an entire paper. My grandmother used to calculate fabric prices at her sari shop using exactly these methods -- she never needed a calculator!

**Child:** Multiplying by 101 is fun too. 47 times 101 equals 4700 plus 47, which is 4747. The two-digit number just writes itself twice!

**Mother:** For two-digit numbers, yes -- the result is the original number repeated. That happens because 101 places one copy in the thousands-hundreds position and another in the tens-ones position.

## Difference of Squares Pattern

**Child:** The pattern n times (n plus 2) equals (n plus 1) squared minus 1 is really handy. So 14 times 16 equals 15 squared minus 1, which is 224.

**Mother:** That follows directly from (a minus b)(a plus b) equals a squared minus b squared, with a equals 15 and b equals 1. You can push further: 13 times 17 equals 15 squared minus 4, which is 221.

**Child:** And the identity (a plus b) squared plus (a minus b) squared equals 2(a squared plus b squared) -- when would I use that?

**Mother:** Suppose someone asks you to find 63 squared plus 57 squared. Since 63 equals 60 plus 3 and 57 equals 60 minus 3, this equals 2 times (3600 plus 9), which is 7218. That is much quicker than squaring both numbers by hand.

**Child:** These identities feel like secret weapons for calculations.

**Mother:** They are more than shortcuts -- they reveal how numbers are structured. Understanding why (a plus b) squared is NOT the same as a squared plus b squared is a crucial insight. That extra 2ab term is what many students forget, and it costs marks in exams.

## Working with Algebraic Expressions

**Child:** When I expand (a plus b)(a squared plus 2ab plus b squared), I have to multiply every term in the first bracket by every term in the second. That gives six products to manage.

**Mother:** Stay organised. Write out all six products in a line, then look for like terms to combine. In this case, you end up with a cubed plus 3a squared b plus 3ab squared plus b cubed -- the cube of a sum.

**Child:** So (a plus b) cubed has coefficients 1, 3, 3, 1. Those are the numbers from the third row of what we call Pascal's Triangle!

**Mother:** In India, this triangle was known to Pingala and later to Varahamihira and Halayudha -- many centuries before Pascal. The coefficients of (a plus b) raised to any power n always come from the nth row of this triangle.

**Child:** Should I memorise the cube identity for exams, or derive it each time?

**Mother:** Memorise it for speed, but know how to derive it for safety. If your mind goes blank under pressure, you can always multiply (a plus b) by (a plus b) squared step by step. When you understand the process, the formula stays with you naturally.
