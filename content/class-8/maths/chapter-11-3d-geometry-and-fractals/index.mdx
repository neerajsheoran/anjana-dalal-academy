## 4.1 Fractals

A **fractal** is a geometric figure where the overall shape keeps repeating itself at progressively tinier scales. This remarkable property is known as **self-similarity** -- zoom into any part, and you discover a miniature replica of the whole.

### Fractals All Around Us

Nature is full of fractal-like structures. Consider a **cauliflower**: break off a small floret, and it looks almost identical to the entire head. Break that floret further, and you find yet another miniature cauliflower!

Other natural examples of self-similarity include:
- **Rivers and their tributaries** -- a main river branches into smaller streams, which branch into even smaller ones
- **Broccoli** -- each small bunch mirrors the shape of the whole vegetable
- **Snowflakes** -- intricate branching patterns repeat at smaller scales
- **Blood vessels** -- arteries divide into smaller arterioles, which divide into capillaries

---

### Sierpinski Carpet

The Polish mathematician **Sierpinski** introduced a striking fractal constructed as follows:
1. **Step 0:** Begin with a filled square.
2. **Step 1:** Split it into 9 identical smaller squares (a 3x3 grid) and punch out the one in the centre.
3. **Step 2:** Apply the same operation to each of the 8 surviving squares.
4. Carry on repeating this forever...

![Steps 0 to 3 of the Sierpinski Carpet fractal showing progressive removal of central squares](sierpinski-carpet-steps.png)

**Tracking the numbers:**
- Surviving squares at step n: **R(n) = 8^n**
- R(0) = 1, R(1) = 8, R(2) = 64, R(3) = 512, ...
- Holes at step n: H(n) = H(n-1) + R(n-1)
- H(0) = 0, H(1) = 1, H(2) = 9, H(3) = 73, ...

---

### Sierpinski Triangle (Gasket)

A related Sierpinski fractal begins with an **equilateral triangle**:
1. Connect the midpoints of the three sides to form 4 smaller equilateral triangles.
2. Delete the central upside-down triangle.
3. Repeat on each of the 3 surviving triangles.

After n steps, the count of remaining triangles is **3^n**, and each one occupies (1/4)^n of the original area.

---

### Koch Snowflake

![Steps 0 to 3 of the Koch Snowflake fractal showing the boundary growing more intricate at each step](koch-snowflake-steps.png)

Invented by the Swedish mathematician **Von Koch** in 1904:
1. Begin with an equilateral triangle.
2. Split every side into 3 equal segments.
3. On the middle segment, build an outward-pointing equilateral triangle and erase the segment that served as its base.
4. Every original side now has a protruding bump. Repeat on every new side.

After n steps:
- Total number of sides = **3 x 4^n**
- The boundary length grows without limit at each step, yet the total enclosed area never exceeds a certain finite value!

> **Key idea:** Fractals showcase **exponential growth** in intricacy at every iteration. Whether it is sides, holes, or surviving pieces, the counts expand as powers of a fixed number.

---

### Fractals in Art and Architecture

Fractal-inspired motifs have appeared in creative works for centuries:
- **Gopurams of South Indian temples** -- The towering gateway structures of temples like those at Madurai feature a large central form surrounded by progressively smaller sculptural elements that echo the overall shape.
- **African textile patterns** -- Traditional Nigerian Fulani wedding blankets display diamond shapes nested inside diamond shapes at multiple scales.
- **M.C. Escher** -- The Dutch artist's print "Smaller and Smaller" depicts identical lizard shapes cascading inward at ever-diminishing sizes.

## 4.2 Visualising Solids

### Profiles of Solids

When we observe a 3D object from various directions, distinct 2D **profiles** (silhouettes) emerge.

**Illustrations:**
- A **cube** shows a square outline when viewed head-on, but a hexagonal outline when viewed corner-first
- A **cylinder** appears as a rectangle from the side and a circle from above
- A **cone** looks like a triangle from the side and a circle from below

**Puzzles to ponder:** Can you identify a solid that produces:
- A rectangular silhouette from one direction and a circular one from another? (A cylinder!)
- A circular silhouette from one direction and a triangular one from another? (A cone!)
- A trapezoidal silhouette from one direction and a circular one from another? (A frustum -- like a steel bucket!)

---

### Faces, Edges, and Vertices

Solids bounded by flat surfaces possess three key features:
- **Faces:** The flat polygonal surfaces that form the boundary
- **Edges:** The line segments where two faces share a boundary
- **Vertices:** The corner points where several edges converge

| Solid | Faces | Edges | Vertices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cube/Cuboid | 6 | 12 | 8 |
| Triangular prism | 5 | 9 | 6 |
| Triangular pyramid (tetrahedron) | 4 | 6 | 4 |
| Square pyramid | 5 | 8 | 5 |
| Pentagonal prism | 7 | 15 | 10 |
| Octahedron | 8 | 12 | 6 |

### General Formulas

For a **prism** whose base is an n-sided polygon:
- Faces = n + 2
- Edges = 3n
- Vertices = 2n

For a **pyramid** whose base is an n-sided polygon:
- Faces = n + 1
- Edges = 2n
- Vertices = n + 1

> **Key idea:** Every convex polyhedron (a solid with flat faces) obeys **Euler's formula**: **V - E + F = 2**, where V = vertices, E = edges, F = faces. Try verifying this with any solid from the table above!

---

### Nets of Solids

A **net** is a flat arrangement of connected shapes that, when folded, produces a solid. Think of it as "peeling open" a 3D object and pressing it flat.

![All 11 possible nets of a cube shown as flat arrangements of 6 connected squares](nets-of-a-cube.png)

**Net of a cube:** The well-known cross pattern of 6 squares is one possibility. A cube has **11 distinct nets** in total.

**Net of a cylinder:** Two circles (for the top and bottom lids) alongside a rectangle (whose width matches the circle's circumference).

**Net of a cone:** One circle (the base) paired with a sector cut from a larger circle (the curved surface).

**Net of a regular tetrahedron:** 4 equilateral triangles arranged in a specific layout. It has only **2 distinct nets**.

**Net of an octahedron:** 8 equilateral triangles. An octahedron has **11 distinct nets**.

**Net of a dodecahedron** (12 pentagonal faces): An astonishing **43,380 different nets**!

**Can a sphere have a net?** Try gift-wrapping a cricket ball with flat paper -- you will inevitably get creases or gaps! A sphere has no flat net.

---

### Shortest Paths on a Cube

An elegant application of nets is determining the **shortest route** between two points on the surface of a cuboid.

**Technique:** Unfold the cuboid into a flat net. On this flat layout, the shortest path between two points is simply a straight line. When you refold the net, that straight line traces the shortest surface path.

**Illustration:** Imagine an ant sitting at the centre of one face of a cuboid. A grain of sugar sits at the centre of the opposite face.
- Draw the cuboid's net.
- Mark the ant's position and the sugar's position on the flat net.
- The straight line connecting them on the net reveals the shortest crawling path.

Sometimes multiple unfoldings must be tested to identify which one yields the truly shortest distance.

## Summary

- **Fractals** are self-similar shapes where the same motif recurs at ever-smaller scales.
- Well-known fractals include the **Sierpinski Carpet**, **Sierpinski Triangle**, and **Koch Snowflake**.
- Fractal-like patterns occur naturally (cauliflower, rivers, blood vessels) and in art (Indian temple gopurams, African textiles).
- 3D solids are characterised by **faces** (flat surfaces), **edges** (boundary lines), and **vertices** (corner points).
- **Euler's formula:** V - E + F = 2 for any convex polyhedron.
- A **net** is a flat layout that folds into a solid. Different solids admit different numbers of possible nets.
- **Shortest surface paths** on a cuboid can be found by unfolding it flat and drawing a straight line.
