## Think of a Number Tricks

**Child:** Maa, the number trick in the chapter always gives the answer 3, no matter what number I start with. How does the original number disappear?

**Mother:** Let us walk through it step by step, beta. You start with some number n. Triple it to get 3n. Add 9 to get 3n plus 9. Divide by 3 to get n plus 3. Now subtract the original n, and you are left with just 3. The n gets cancelled out completely.

**Child:** So the trick is designed so that the unknown disappears! If I wanted the final answer to be 10 instead, I would change the "add 9" step to "add 30," because 30 divided by 3 is 10.

**Mother:** Exactly. You can design your own tricks by starting from the desired answer and working backwards. This reverse-engineering skill is exactly what software developers use -- they know the output they want and figure out the steps to reach it.

**Child:** The birthday trick is fancier -- it packs the month and day into a single number using 100M plus D plus 205. You subtract 205 to decode it.

**Mother:** The clever part is that multiplying the month by 100 pushes it into the hundreds position, while the day stays in the units and tens positions. It is place value working hand-in-hand with algebra. Both concepts from earlier chapters join forces here.

## Number Pyramids

**Child:** Maa, in a number pyramid, each number is the sum of the two below it. If the base is p, q, r, the top becomes p plus 2q plus r. Why does q appear twice?

**Mother:** Because q feeds into both entries of the second row. The second row has p plus q on the left and q plus r on the right. When you add those two to get the summit, q shows up in both terms. So it contributes twice.

**Child:** For a 4-row pyramid with base p, q, r, s, the summit is p plus 3q plus 3r plus s. The coefficients 1, 3, 3, 1 come from Pascal's Triangle!

**Mother:** Yes! Pascal's Triangle -- which Indian mathematicians called Meru Prastara, described centuries ago by Pingala, Varahamihira, and Halayudha -- appears in binomial expansions, probability, and now in number pyramids too.

**Child:** The chapter also mentions that if you fill the base with Virahanka-Fibonacci numbers, all the numbers in the pyramid are also Virahanka-Fibonacci numbers. That is incredible!

**Mother:** Mathematics is full of such hidden connections. The more you explore, the more these surprises reveal themselves. For exams, make sure you can solve pyramids both forward -- base to summit -- and backward -- given the summit, find a missing base value.

## Calendar and Grid Puzzles

**Child:** Maa, the calendar trick is neat. A 2 by 2 block starting at d contains d, d plus 1, d plus 7, d plus 8. The sum is 4d plus 16. So if someone tells me the sum, I calculate d as (sum minus 16) divided by 4.

**Mother:** This is setting up a linear equation and solving it -- the bread and butter of algebra. The calendar's weekly structure gives you the relationship (each row is 7 apart), and algebra converts that structure into a formula.

**Child:** What about a 3 by 3 block?

**Mother:** In a 3 by 3 block starting at d, the nine dates are d, d plus 1, d plus 2, d plus 7, d plus 8, d plus 9, d plus 14, d plus 15, d plus 16. Their sum is 9d plus 72. So d equals (sum minus 72) divided by 9.

**Child:** And the shape puzzles where a star plus a star plus a star equals 36 means the star is 12?

**Mother:** Then you plug star equals 12 into the next equation. If diamond plus diamond plus star equals 28, you get 2 diamonds plus 12 equals 28, so diamond equals 8. This is the substitution method -- the very technique engineers and scientists use every day.

## Divisibility Tricks Explained

**Child:** Maa, if I reverse a 2-digit number and subtract, the result is always a multiple of 9. The chapter shows it as 10a plus b minus 10b minus a equals 9 times (a minus b). That is so elegant!

**Mother:** The magic is in writing the number using place value. Once you express a 2-digit number as 10a plus b, algebra handles the rest automatically.

**Child:** For 3-digit numbers, the difference between a number and its reverse is 99 times (a minus c). The middle digit b completely cancels out!

**Mother:** And the sum of a 2-digit number and its reverse is 11 times (a plus b). These are not magic -- they are algebra applied to our base-10 number system.

**Child:** Maa, can I invent my own trick?

**Mother:** Try this: pick any 3-digit number and write it twice to form a 6-digit number. Like 318 becomes 318318. This 6-digit number is always divisible by 7, 11, and 13.

**Child:** Because the 6-digit number equals the 3-digit number times 1001! And 1001 is 7 times 11 times 13!

**Mother:** Beautiful reasoning, beta. That is the essence of this chapter -- algebra peels away the mystery and reveals the clear logic underneath.

## Solving Equations

**Child:** Maa, the age puzzle in the chapter uses one equation with one unknown. Sunita is 4 times her daughter's age, and 6 years ago she was 7 times her daughter's age. How do we set it up?

**Mother:** Let the daughter's current age be d. Then Sunita's age is 4d. Six years ago, they were d minus 6 and 4d minus 6. The condition says 4d minus 6 equals 7 times (d minus 6). Expanding: 4d minus 6 equals 7d minus 42. Rearranging: 36 equals 3d, so d equals 12. The daughter is 12 and Sunita is 48.

**Child:** Let me verify: 6 years ago they were 6 and 42. And 42 is indeed 7 times 6. It works!

**Mother:** Always substitute your answer back into the original problem to check. For the two-number problem -- sum is 56 and difference is 18 -- adding the equations gives 2x equals 74, so x equals 37. Then y equals 19. Check: 37 plus 19 is 56, and 37 minus 19 is 18.

**Child:** These skills will be needed when equations get harder in class 9 and 10, right?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Quadratic equations, simultaneous equations, polynomials -- they all build on the linear equation-solving you master here. Get this foundation solid, and the rest will follow naturally.
