## 5.1 The Balancing Act

### Mean as the Centre

The **mean** (also called the arithmetic mean or average) of a collection of numbers equals the total of all values divided by how many values there are.

**Illustration:** The mean of 5 and 11 is (5 + 11)/2 = **8**.

When you mark numbers on a number line, the mean sits at the **balance point** -- the spot where the combined distances on the left exactly match the combined distances on the right.

**Illustration:** Consider the data set 7, 9, 10, 18:
- Mean = (7 + 9 + 10 + 18)/4 = 44/4 = **11**
- Distances below: (11-7) + (11-9) + (11-10) = 4 + 2 + 1 = 7
- Distances above: (18-11) = 7
- Both sides sum to 7 -- perfectly balanced!

> **Key idea:** The mean is the one and only point where the total gap below equals the total gap above. No other value has this balance property. This is why the mean is called a measure of **central tendency**.

---

### How the Mean Shifts

**Adding a value above the current mean:** The mean **rises** to restore balance.

**Adding a value below the current mean:** The mean **drops**.

**Adding a value exactly equal to the mean:** The mean **remains unchanged**.

**Removing a value above the current mean:** The mean **drops**.

**Removing a value below the current mean:** The mean **rises**.

---

### Keeping the Mean Steady

Is it possible to insert or delete values without altering the mean?

**Absolutely!** Insert two values -- one above and one below the mean -- at equal distances from the mean, and the balance stays perfect.

You can even insert three values: two below the mean and one above, provided their total distances from the mean cancel out.

---

### Scaling and Shifting the Mean

**What happens when you add a fixed number to every data value?**

If each value goes up by k, the mean also goes up by k.

**Algebraic justification:** If the original mean is m = (x1 + x2 + ... + xn)/n, then after adding k to each:

New mean = ((x1+k) + (x2+k) + ... + (xn+k))/n = m + k

**What happens when you multiply every data value by a fixed number?**

If each value is scaled by k, the mean is scaled by k too.

**Justification:** New mean = (kx1 + kx2 + ... + kxn)/n = k(x1 + x2 + ... + xn)/n = km

> **Key idea:** The mean reacts proportionally to uniform changes in the data. A constant addition shifts the mean by that constant. A constant multiplier scales the mean by that multiplier.

## 5.2 Median

The **median** is the middle entry once the data is sorted from smallest to largest. When the count of values is even, the median is the average of the two central entries.

### How the Median Shifts

- Inserting a value **above** the median pushes it up (or leaves it unchanged).
- Inserting a value **below** the median pushes it down (or leaves it unchanged).
- The median is far less disturbed by extreme outliers compared to the mean.

## Finding the Unknown

### Problem 1: A Missing Value

A sports teacher records the heights (in cm) of 8 students and finds the mean height is 143.5 cm. Seven recorded heights are 140, 148, 135, 150, 138, 145, and 142. The eighth height, h, is unknown.

Sum of all heights = Mean x Count of students
- (140 + 148 + 135 + 150 + 138 + 145 + 142) + h = 143.5 x 8 = 1148
- 998 + h = 1148
- h = **150 cm**

### Problem 2: A Wrongly Noted Value

A shopkeeper calculates her average daily sale as Rs 2,400 over 12 days. Later she realises one day's sale was noted as Rs 200 more than the actual amount.

- Incorrect total = 2400 x 12 = Rs 28,800
- Correct total = 28,800 - 200 = Rs 28,600
- Correct average = 28,600/12 = **Rs 2,383.33** (approx.)

## Mean and Median with Frequencies

When certain values repeat, a **frequency table** streamlines the calculation.

**Illustration:** Number of books read by students in a month:

| Books Read | Frequency |
|---|---|
| 2 | 5 |
| 3 | 8 |
| 4 | 12 |
| 5 | 5 |

**Mean = (2x5 + 3x8 + 4x12 + 5x5) / (5 + 8 + 12 + 5)**
= (10 + 24 + 48 + 25) / 30
= 107/30 = **3.57** (approx.)

The mean need not coincide with any actual data value. Here, 3.57 falls between 3 and 4, even though no student read 3.57 books.

## 5.3 Line Graphs

A **line graph** displays data points connected by straight lines, making it easy to spot how a quantity evolves over time or another variable.

### Interpreting Line Graphs

- The **horizontal axis (x-axis)** typically represents time or ordered categories.
- The **vertical axis (y-axis)** represents the measured quantity.
- Each data pair is plotted as a dot, and adjacent dots are linked by straight lines.
- A **rising segment** signals growth; a **falling segment** signals decline.

### Illustration: Rainfall Across Months

| Month | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rainfall (mm) | 12 | 8 | 15 | 35 | 70 | 140 |

![Line graph showing temperature over a day with time on x-axis and temperature on y-axis, rising to a peak at 3 PM then falling](line-graph-temperature-day.png)

Plot these values with months on the x-axis and rainfall on the y-axis. The graph reveals a gradual rise from January to March, followed by a steep climb from April to June as the monsoon approaches.

### Constructing Line Graphs

**Steps:**
1. Draw and label both axes.
2. Pick a suitable scale for each axis.
3. Plot every data point as a dot.
4. Connect neighbouring dots with straight lines.
5. Add a descriptive title to the graph.

> **Key idea:** Line graphs excel at revealing **trends** -- how a quantity grows, shrinks, or stays flat over time. The steepness of each segment indicates how rapidly the change is occurring.

## 5.4 Interpreting Data

When analysing data and graphs, ask yourself:
- What **trend** does the data display? (growing, shrinking, steady)
- Are there any **outliers** (values that stand out as unusual)?
- What might **explain** the observed pattern?
- Can the trend help us **forecast** future values?

### Comparing Two Data Sets

When comparing two groups, examine both the **average** (mean or median) and the **spread** of the data. Two groups can share the same average yet differ wildly in consistency.

**Illustration:** Two kabaddi players both average 8 raid points per match over a tournament. But Player A scores between 6 and 10 every match, while Player B swings between 2 and 14. Their averages are identical, but Player A is far more dependable.

---

## Summary

- The **mean** is the balance point of a data set -- the total gap below equals the total gap above.
- Adding a constant k to every value shifts the mean by k. Multiplying every value by k scales the mean by k.
- The **median** is the central value in sorted data and resists distortion by extreme outliers more than the mean does.
- **Frequency tables** simplify mean calculations when values repeat: multiply each value by its frequency, sum up, and divide by total frequency.
- **Line graphs** track how a quantity changes over time by connecting plotted points.
- When interpreting data, look for trends, outliers, and plausible explanations.
