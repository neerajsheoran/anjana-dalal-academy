## The Rangoli Puzzle

Imagine Priya is decorating her courtyard for Diwali. She has small square tiles and wants to arrange them into larger square patterns:

- With **1 tile**, she makes a 1x1 square.
- With **4 tiles**, she makes a 2x2 square.
- With **9 tiles**, she makes a 3x3 square.
- With **16 tiles**, she makes a 4x4 square.

But when she has **12 tiles**, can she form a perfect square? She tries arranging them — 3 rows of 4 is a rectangle, not a square. No matter how she arranges 12 tiles, she cannot form a perfect square!

> **Key idea:** Only certain special numbers of tiles can be arranged into a perfect square shape. These numbers — 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, ... — are called **perfect squares**. They are the result of multiplying a whole number by itself.

---

## 1.1 Square Numbers

A **square number** (or **perfect square**) is the product of a number multiplied by itself.

| Side length | Tiles needed |
|---|---|
| 1 | 1 x 1 = **1** |
| 2 | 2 x 2 = **4** |
| 3 | 3 x 3 = **9** |
| 4 | 4 x 4 = **16** |
| 5 | 5 x 5 = **25** |
| 10 | 10 x 10 = **100** |

![Square numbers visualised as dots arranged in square grids for 1, 4, 9, 16, and 25](square-numbers-dot-grids.png)

We write n x n as **n²**, read as "n squared."

For instance:
- 7² = 7 x 7 = 49
- 11² = 11 x 11 = 121

Fractions and decimals can also be squared:
- (2/7)² = (2/7) x (2/7) = 4/49
- (1.5)² = 1.5 x 1.5 = 2.25

> **Key idea:** The squares of natural numbers (1, 4, 9, 16, 25, ...) are called **perfect squares**.

## Patterns and Properties of Perfect Squares

Here are the squares of the first 20 natural numbers:

| n | n² | n | n² |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 11 | 121 |
| 2 | 4 | 12 | 144 |
| 3 | 9 | 13 | 169 |
| 4 | 16 | 14 | 196 |
| 5 | 25 | 15 | 225 |
| 6 | 36 | 16 | 256 |
| 7 | 49 | 17 | 289 |
| 8 | 64 | 18 | 324 |
| 9 | 81 | 19 | 361 |
| 10 | 100 | 20 | 400 |

### Last digit pattern

Observe the last digit of each perfect square carefully. A perfect square can only end in **0, 1, 4, 5, 6, or 9**. No perfect square ever ends in 2, 3, 7, or 8.

- Numbers ending in **1 or 9** produce squares ending in **1**.
- Numbers ending in **2 or 8** produce squares ending in **4**.
- Numbers ending in **3 or 7** produce squares ending in **9**.
- Numbers ending in **4 or 6** produce squares ending in **6**.
- Numbers ending in **5** produce squares ending in **5**.
- Numbers ending in **0** produce squares ending in **0**.

> **Key idea:** If a number ends in 2, 3, 7, or 8, it cannot be a perfect square. However, ending in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, or 9 does not guarantee it is a perfect square — for example, 33 ends in 3 (so it is ruled out), while 35 ends in 5 but is not a perfect square either.

### Trailing zeros

- 10² = 100 (one zero becomes two zeros)
- 100² = 10000 (two zeros become four zeros)
- 300² = 90000 (two zeros become four zeros)

**When you square a number, the count of trailing zeros always doubles.** Therefore, perfect squares can only have an **even** number of trailing zeros.

### Squares preserve even-odd nature

- The square of an **even** number is always **even**.
- The square of an **odd** number is always **odd**.

## The Bond Between Perfect Squares and Odd Numbers

Study the gaps between consecutive squares:

- 4 - 1 = **3**
- 9 - 4 = **5**
- 16 - 9 = **7**
- 25 - 16 = **9**

The gaps form the sequence of consecutive odd numbers! This gives us a stunning pattern:

- 1 = **1**
- 1 + 3 = **4**
- 1 + 3 + 5 = **9**
- 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = **16**
- 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 = **25**
- 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 + 11 = **36**

> **Key idea:** Adding the first n odd numbers always gives n². Every perfect square equals a sum of consecutive odd numbers beginning from 1.

### Testing whether a number is a perfect square

Pick a number like 36 and keep subtracting consecutive odd numbers from 1:

- 36 - 1 = 35
- 35 - 3 = 32
- 32 - 5 = 27
- 27 - 7 = 20
- 20 - 9 = 11
- 11 - 11 = **0**

We hit 0 at the 6th step, so 36 = 6² (a perfect square).

Now try 30:

- 30 - 1 = 29
- 29 - 3 = 26
- 26 - 5 = 21
- 21 - 7 = 14
- 14 - 9 = 5
- 5 - 11 = -6

We went below zero without reaching it, so 30 is **not** a perfect square.

### Computing the next square from a known one

Suppose you know that 27² = 729. To get 28²:
- The 28th odd number is 2(28) - 1 = 55
- So 28² = 729 + 55 = **784**

In general, the **nth odd number** is **2n - 1**.

## Perfect Squares and Triangular Numbers

**Triangular numbers** are: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, ...

A lovely connection links triangular numbers to square numbers:

- 1 + 3 = **4** = 2²
- 3 + 6 = **9** = 3²
- 6 + 10 = **16** = 4²
- 10 + 15 = **25** = 5²

> **Key idea:** Adding two consecutive triangular numbers always produces a perfect square.

## Square Roots

If a square garden has area 81 sq. metres, what is its side length? Since 9 x 9 = 81, the side is **9 m**. We call 9 the **square root** of 81.

In general, if y = x², then x is the square root of y.

The square root symbol is **√**:
- √49 = ±7 (since both 7² = 49 and (-7)² = 49)
- √144 = ±12
- √16 = ±4

> **Key idea:** Every perfect square has two integer square roots — one positive and one negative. Typically, we work with the positive square root.

### Techniques for Finding Square Roots

**Technique 1: Listing squares**

To check if 625 is a perfect square, list squares near it:
- 23² = 529, 24² = 576, 25² = 625

So √625 = 25. This approach works but slows down for large numbers.

**Technique 2: Repeated subtraction of odd numbers**

Subtract 1, 3, 5, 7, ... from the number until you hit 0. Reaching 0 at step n means the square root is n.

For instance, √49: subtract 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13 from 49 to reach 0 at the 7th step. So √49 = 7.

**Technique 3: Prime factorisation**

This is the most reliable method.

**Example:** Is 576 a perfect square?
- 576 = 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 x 3 x 3
- Pair them up: (2 x 2) x (2 x 2) x (2 x 2) x (3 x 3)
- Every factor pairs up, so 576 is a perfect square.
- √576 = 2 x 2 x 2 x 3 = **24**

**Example:** Is 180 a perfect square?
- 180 = 2 x 2 x 3 x 3 x 5
- The factor 5 has no pair.
- So 180 is **not** a perfect square.

> **Key idea:** A number is a perfect square when every prime factor in its factorisation appears an **even** number of times. To get the square root, take one factor from each pair.

### Estimating Square Roots

**Example:** Find √2025.

1. 2025 lies between 1600 (40²) and 2500 (50²), so 40 < √2025 < 50.
2. The last digit is 5, so the square root must end in 5. Possible answer: 45.
3. Verify: 45² = 2025. Correct!

**Example:** Estimate √200.
- 14² = 196 and 15² = 225
- So 14 < √200 < 15
- Since 200 is very close to 196, √200 is approximately 14 (slightly more).

## 1.2 Cubic Numbers

![Unit cubes arranged to form cubes of side 1, 2, and 3 showing 1, 8, and 27 unit cubes respectively](cube-numbers-unit-cubes.png)

A **cube** is a solid shape where every edge has the same length and every corner forms a right angle.

- A cube with edge 2 cm contains **2 x 2 x 2 = 8** unit cubes.
- A cube with edge 3 cm contains **3 x 3 x 3 = 27** unit cubes.
- A cube with edge 4 cm contains **4 x 4 x 4 = 64** unit cubes.

We write n x n x n as **n³**, read as "n cubed."

The numbers 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, ... are called **perfect cubes**.

### Table of Cubes

| n | n³ | n | n³ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 11 | 1331 |
| 2 | 8 | 12 | 1728 |
| 3 | 27 | 13 | 2197 |
| 4 | 64 | 14 | 2744 |
| 5 | 125 | 15 | 3375 |
| 6 | 216 | 16 | 4096 |
| 7 | 343 | 17 | 4913 |
| 8 | 512 | 18 | 5832 |
| 9 | 729 | 19 | 6859 |
| 10 | 1000 | 20 | 8000 |

### Properties of Cubes

- Unlike squares, cubes can end in **any digit** from 0 to 9.
- If a number has trailing zeros, its cube has exactly **three times** as many trailing zeros (e.g., 10³ = 1000 has three zeros, 100³ = 1000000 has six zeros). A cube **cannot** have just two trailing zeros.
- Fractions and decimals can also be cubed:
  - (3/5)³ = (3/5) x (3/5) x (3/5) = 27/125
  - (−4)³ = −4 x −4 x −4 = −64

> **Key idea:** Cubing a negative number always gives a negative result, unlike squaring which always gives a positive result.

## The Hardy-Ramanujan Number: A Legendary Tale

The brilliant Indian mathematician **Srinivasa Ramanujan** was once visited by his colleague G. H. Hardy at Cambridge. Hardy mentioned that his taxi had the number **1729**, calling it "a rather uninteresting number."

Ramanujan responded instantly: "Not at all! It is a remarkable number. It is the smallest number expressible as the sum of two cubes in two distinct ways."

- 1729 = 1³ + 12³ = 1 + 1728
- 1729 = 9³ + 10³ = 729 + 1000

This story made 1729 famous as the **Hardy-Ramanujan Number**. Numbers with this property of being expressible as two different sums of two cubes are known as **taxicab numbers**.

The next two taxicab numbers are **4104** and **13832**.

## Perfect Cubes and Consecutive Odd Numbers

Like squares, cubes also share a deep connection with odd numbers:

- 1 = **1** = 1³
- 3 + 5 = **8** = 2³
- 7 + 9 + 11 = **27** = 3³
- 13 + 15 + 17 + 19 = **64** = 4³
- 21 + 23 + 25 + 27 + 29 = **125** = 5³
- 31 + 33 + 35 + 37 + 39 + 41 = **216** = 6³

Every perfect cube can be expressed as a sum of consecutive odd numbers!

## Cube Roots

If y = x³, then x is the **cube root** of y, written as **∛y**.

- ∛8 = 2 (because 2³ = 8)
- ∛27 = 3 (because 3³ = 27)
- ∛1000 = 10 (because 10³ = 1000)

### Finding Cube Roots Using Prime Factorisation

**Example:** Is 2744 a perfect cube?
- 2744 = 2 x 2 x 2 x 7 x 7 x 7
- Group into triplets: (2 x 2 x 2) x (7 x 7 x 7)
- All factors form complete triplets, so 2744 is a perfect cube.
- ∛2744 = 2 x 7 = **14**

**Example:** Is 360 a perfect cube?
- 360 = 2 x 2 x 2 x 3 x 3 x 5
- The factors 3 (appears twice) and 5 (appears once) do not form complete triplets.
- So 360 is **not** a perfect cube.

> **Key idea:** A number is a perfect cube when every prime factor in its factorisation appears exactly **three times** (or a multiple of three times). To find the cube root, take one factor from each triplet.

### Successive Differences of Cubes

For perfect squares, the second level of successive differences gives a constant value (2). For perfect cubes, the **third level** of successive differences produces a constant:

| Perfect cubes | 1 | 8 | 27 | 64 | 125 | 216 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Level 1 | 7 | 19 | 37 | 61 | 91 | |
| Level 2 | 12 | 18 | 24 | 30 | | |
| Level 3 | 6 | 6 | 6 | | | |

At the third level, every difference equals **6**.

## 1.3 Historical Roots

- The earliest recorded tables of squares and cubes were inscribed on **Babylonian** clay tablets around **1700 BCE**.
- In ancient **Sanskrit** mathematical texts, the word **varga** denoted both the square shape and the square of a number. The word **ghana** meant cube. The fourth power was called **varga-varga**.
- **Aryabhata** (499 CE) described varga as "a square figure with four equal sides and the number representing its area."
- The English word **"root"** in mathematics (square root, cube root) traces back to the Sanskrit word **mula**, meaning the root of a plant, a foundation, or an origin. **Varga-mula** meant square root and **ghana-mula** meant cube root.
- This Sanskrit terminology was later translated into **Arabic** (jidhr) and **Latin** (radix), both meaning "root of a plant."

## Summary

- A **square number** is obtained by multiplying a number by itself: n² = n x n.
- Perfect squares can only end in **0, 1, 4, 5, 6, or 9** and have an even number of trailing zeros.
- The **square root** (√) reverses squaring. Every perfect square has two integer square roots (positive and negative).
- A **cube number** is obtained by multiplying a number by itself three times: n³ = n x n x n.
- A number is a **perfect square** when every prime factor appears an even number of times.
- A number is a **perfect cube** when every prime factor appears a multiple of three times.
- The **cube root** (∛) reverses cubing.
- The sum of the first n odd numbers equals n².
- Perfect cubes can also be expressed as sums of consecutive odd numbers.
