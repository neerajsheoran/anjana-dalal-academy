## Map Scales and Real Distances

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says a map scale like 1 to 40,00,000 means 1 cm on the map equals 40 km in real life. How do mapmakers choose the right scale?

**Mother:** It depends on the purpose, beta. A map of all India might use 1 to 40,00,000, but a city map of Jaipur would use something like 1 to 25,000 because you need street-level detail. The Survey of India and ISRO both create maps at different scales for different needs.

**Child:** So if Mumbai and Pune are 3.75 cm apart on a 1 to 40,00,000 map, the real distance is 3.75 times 40, which is 150 km. That matches what Google Maps shows!

**Mother:** Map reading is proportional reasoning at its most practical. Architects use the same principle -- a house plan at 1 to 100 scale means 1 cm on paper equals 100 cm in the actual building. If a living room is 5 cm by 4 cm on the blueprint, the real room is 5 metres by 4 metres.

**Child:** We could map our school playground! If it is 80 metres by 60 metres, at 1 to 100 that would be 80 cm by 60 cm on chart paper.

**Mother:** Exactly. You could mark the cricket pitch, the basketball court, the flag post -- all drawn to the same scale. That is precisely how urban planners design parks and townships.

## Multi-Term Ratios

**Child:** Maa, ratios can have more than two terms. The sambar masala example uses coriander, chillies, chana dal, and pepper in the ratio 6 to 3 to 2 to 1. How do I adjust it if I have fewer ingredients?

**Mother:** Scale everything by the same factor, just like a two-term ratio. If Kamala has only 1 chilli instead of 3, the factor is 1/3. So she needs 2 spoons coriander, 1 chilli, 2/3 spoon chana dal, and 1/3 spoon pepper.

**Child:** The concrete mixture ratio of 1 to 2 to 4 for cement, sand, and aggregate is practical. Engineers must use this when building foundations. With 5 bags of cement, I multiply everything by 5 to get 5 to 10 to 20.

**Mother:** Multi-term ratios show up in science as well. Chemical reactions specify exact ratios of reactants and products. In higher classes, you will learn about stoichiometry, which is nothing but scaling multi-term ratios for chemical equations.

**Child:** For the orange paint mix of yellow to red to white as 3 to 2 to 5, if I have 15 litres of white, I need 9 litres yellow and 6 litres red. Total is 30 litres.

**Mother:** You figured out that 1 part equals 3 litres, then multiplied each component. That is the unitary method applied to multi-term ratios -- find one part, then scale up.

## Dividing a Quantity in a Given Ratio

**Child:** To split 180 degrees among triangle angles in the ratio 2 to 3 to 4, the total parts are 9, and each part is 20 degrees. So the angles are 40, 60, and 80 degrees.

**Mother:** This technique is used everywhere -- splitting business profits, distributing inheritance, allocating water from a canal to different fields. If three partners invest in a shop in the ratio 3 to 4 to 5 and earn a profit of Rs 60,000, their shares are 15,000, 20,000, and 25,000 respectively.

**Child:** The general approach is: each share equals the total multiplied by that term divided by the sum of all terms. So the first partner gets 60,000 times 3 over 12, which is 15,000.

**Mother:** Under Indian partnership law, profits and losses are divided in exactly the ratio of each person's capital contribution. This chapter is not just exam preparation -- it is preparation for real financial decisions.

**Child:** Can ratio terms be decimals or fractions?

**Mother:** Absolutely. The concrete ratio 1 to 2 to 4 could also be written as 0.5 to 1 to 2 if you halve everything. Or you could convert awkward decimals to whole numbers by multiplying through. Either form is valid -- pick whichever is simpler.

## Pie Charts

**Child:** Maa, pie charts turn proportions into slices of a circle. The full circle is 360 degrees, representing the complete total. So 50 percent of something would take up 180 degrees?

**Mother:** Exactly. Each percentage point corresponds to 3.6 degrees. To build a pie chart, express each category as a fraction of the total, multiply by 360, and draw the sector with a protractor.

**Child:** For a school of 50 students where 15 got grade A, the angle is 15 over 50 times 360, which is 108 degrees.

**Mother:** Pie charts appear in government budget speeches, company annual reports, and election coverage. When the Finance Minister presents the Union Budget, pie charts illustrate how tax revenue is split across defence, education, healthcare, and other sectors. Reading them fluently is part of being an informed citizen.

**Child:** What if my angles do not add up to 360?

**Mother:** Then there is a mistake somewhere. The sum must always be exactly 360 degrees. That built-in check is one of the nice features of pie charts -- if your total is 355 or 362, you know to hunt for the error.

## Direct and Inverse Proportion

**Child:** Direct proportion means both quantities grow together, while inverse proportion means one grows as the other shrinks. How do I tell which is which in a word problem?

**Mother:** Ask one simple question: if I double the first quantity, does the second also double, or does it halve? If doubling one doubles the other, that is direct proportion. If doubling one halves the other, it is inverse.

**Child:** More labourers means less time to harvest -- that is inverse. 8 labourers take 12 days, so 24 labourers take 96 divided by 24, which is 4 days. The product -- labourers times days -- stays constant at 96.

**Mother:** And more distance at the same speed means more time -- that is direct. The ratio of distance to time stays constant.

**Child:** So the key formulas are: for direct proportion, x over y equals a constant; for inverse proportion, x times y equals a constant.

**Mother:** Indian Railways uses inverse proportion when optimising schedules -- faster trains on busy routes cut travel time. ISRO relies on direct proportion to calculate fuel requirements -- heavier payloads demand proportionally more fuel. These simple ideas underpin sophisticated engineering.
