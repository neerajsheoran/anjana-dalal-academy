## 7.1 Observing Similarity in Change

Have you noticed what happens when you zoom in on a photo on your phone? Sometimes the image looks perfectly natural, and sometimes it looks oddly stretched or squashed. The secret lies in **how** the dimensions change.

Consider five versions of a peacock photograph:

| Photo | Width (mm) | Height (mm) |
|---|---|---|
| P | 80 | 60 |
| Q | 50 | 30 |
| R | 40 | 30 |
| S | 120 | 90 |
| T | 80 | 80 |

Photos P, R, and S show the peacock looking natural. But Q appears widened and T appears squished. Why?

- Compare P and R: Width of R is half of P (40 = 80/2). Height of R is also half of P (30 = 60/2). **Both dimensions changed by the same factor (1/2)**, so the photo looks natural.
- Compare P and Q: Width dropped by 30 mm, height also dropped by 30 mm. The same **difference**, but width is NOT half of P's width. Since both dimensions did not change by the **same factor**, Q looks distorted.

> **Key idea:** When two quantities change by the **same factor** (multiplicative change), the relationship is **proportional** and shapes look similar. When quantities change by the same difference (additive change), proportionality is NOT guaranteed.

---

## 7.2 Ratios

A **ratio** compares two quantities through division. We write the ratio of width to height of photo P as **80 : 60**.

The numbers 80 and 60 are the **terms** of the ratio.

A ratio a : b means "for every a units of the first quantity, there are b units of the second."

The ratios for our natural-looking photos:
- P: 80 : 60
- R: 40 : 30
- S: 120 : 90

These ratios are all **proportional** because their terms scale by the same factor.

---

## 7.3 Ratios in Their Simplest Form

We reduce a ratio to its **simplest form** by dividing both terms by their **HCF** (Highest Common Factor).

**Photo P:** 80 : 60. HCF of 80 and 60 is 20. Simplest form = **4 : 3**

**Photo R:** 40 : 30. HCF is 10. Simplest form = **4 : 3**

**Photo S:** 120 : 90. HCF is 30. Simplest form = **4 : 3**

**Photo Q:** 50 : 30. HCF is 10. Simplest form = **5 : 3** (different from 4 : 3)

**Photo T:** 80 : 80. HCF is 80. Simplest form = **1 : 1** (different from 4 : 3)

> **Key idea:** Two ratios are **proportional** (or "in proportion") if their simplest forms match. We write this as a : b :: c : d. So 80 : 60 :: 40 : 30 :: 120 : 90, since all reduce to 4 : 3.

## 7.4 Problem Solving with Proportional Reasoning

### Example 1: Checking Proportionality

Are 5 : 8 and 45 : 72 proportional?

- 5 : 8 is already in simplest form.
- HCF of 45 and 72 is 9. So 45 : 72 = 5 : 8 in simplest form.
- **Yes**, they are proportional. 5 : 8 :: 45 : 72.

---

### Example 2: Chai Recipe

Priya makes 4 cups of chai using 6 spoons of sugar. She wants to make 14 cups with the same sweetness. How much sugar does she need?

The ratios must stay proportional:
- 4 : 6 :: 14 : ?
- Factor of change in the first term: 14 / 4 = **3.5**
- Apply the same factor to the second term: 6 x 3.5 = **21**

She needs **21 spoons of sugar** for 14 cups.

---

### Example 3: Bricks for a Boundary Wall

Mohan used 5 bags of cement for a 75-foot boundary wall. Suresh used 3 bags for a 45-foot wall. Is Suresh's wall weaker?

- Mohan's ratio: 75 : 5 = **15 : 1** (simplest form)
- Suresh's ratio: 45 : 3 = **15 : 1** (simplest form)

The ratios are proportional! **Both walls are equally strong.**

---

### Example 4: Growing Up and Changing Ratios

When Ananya was 5 years old, her father was 35. Ratio = 5 : 35 = **1 : 7**.

When Ananya is 15, her father will be 45 (10 years later). Ratio = 15 : 45 = **1 : 3**.

The ratio has **changed**! This happens because ages grow by addition, not multiplication.

> **Key idea:** When we **add** (or subtract) the same number to both terms of a ratio, the ratio generally changes. Proportionality demands change by the same **factor** (multiplication), not the same **difference** (addition).

---

### Example 5: Mango Lassi

Divya makes mango lassi by blending 20 mL mango pulp with 30 mL yoghurt (ratio 20 : 30 = **2 : 3**).

- **Stronger mango flavour:** 25 mL pulp + 25 mL yoghurt (ratio 25 : 25 = **1 : 1**) -- more pulp per unit yoghurt
- **Milder mango flavour:** 15 mL pulp + 35 mL yoghurt (ratio 15 : 35 = **3 : 7**) -- less pulp per unit yoghurt

The flavour is more intense when the pulp-to-yoghurt ratio is **higher**, and milder when it is **lower**.

### Finding Missing Terms

**Example:** Complete ratios proportional to 18 : 27.

**? : 54**
- 54 is 2 times 27. So multiply 18 by 2 = **36**. Answer: **36 : 54**

**8 : ?**
- What factor converts 18 to 8? That is 8/18 = 4/9
- Multiply 27 by 4/9 = **12**. Answer: **8 : 12**

**2 : ?**
- 18 / 9 = 2 (dividing by HCF 9)
- 27 / 9 = 3. Answer: **2 : 3** (simplest form)

## The Rule of Three (Trairasika)

Many real-world problems involve finding a fourth unknown quantity when three are known and the ratios are proportional.

### The Cross-Multiplication Method

If **a : b :: c : d**, then:

**ad = bc** (cross multiplication)

This gives us: **d = (b x c) / a**

### Example: School Lunch

A school kitchen cooks 20 kg of dal for 160 students. On sports day, only 100 students are present. How much dal is needed?

- 160 : 20 :: 100 : x
- Cross multiply: 160 x = 20 x 100 = 2000
- x = 2000 / 160 = **12.5 kg**

### Example: Train Journey

A train covers 120 km in 90 minutes. How far will it travel in 3 hours (180 minutes) at the same speed?

- 90 : 120 :: 180 : x
- Cross multiply: 90x = 120 x 180 = 21600
- x = 21600 / 90 = **240 km**

### Historical Note

In ancient India, **Aryabhata** (499 CE) formalised this technique as the **Rule of Three** (Trairasika). He identified three given quantities:
- **Pramana** (measure) -- the known reference quantity
- **Phala** (fruit) -- the known related quantity
- **Ichchha** (desire) -- the desired reference quantity

The unknown answer, called **Ichchha-phala** (desired fruit), is:

**Ichchha-phala = (Phala x Ichchha) / Pramana**

> **Key idea:** The Rule of Three has powered commerce and science for millennia. If a : b :: c : d, then d = bc/a. This cross-multiplication technique is the essential tool for solving proportion problems.

## Unitary Method

Another strategy: first calculate the value for **one unit**, then scale up.

**Example:** If 7 kg of mangoes costs Rs 490, what is the cost of 11 kg?

1. Cost of 1 kg = 490 / 7 = Rs 70
2. Cost of 11 kg = 70 x 11 = **Rs 770**

This is the **unitary method** -- find the per-unit value first, then multiply.

---

## Summary

- A **ratio** a : b compares two quantities through division.
- Ratios reach their **simplest form** when both terms are divided by their HCF.
- Two ratios are **proportional** when their simplest forms are identical.
- Proportionality requires change by the same **factor** (multiplication), not the same difference (addition/subtraction).
- If a : b :: c : d, then **ad = bc** (cross multiplication), yielding d = bc/a.
- This technique was named **Trairasika** (Rule of Three) by ancient Indian mathematicians such as Aryabhata.
- The **unitary method** finds the value per single unit first, then scales to the target quantity.
