## Understanding Proportionality

**Child:** Maa, the photo resizing example makes so much sense! When I pinch-zoom a picture on my tablet, sometimes the peacock looks fat and sometimes it looks normal. The chapter says it is about factors versus differences.

**Mother:** That is the heart of it. If both width and height are scaled by the same factor -- say both are doubled or both are halved -- the image stays proportional. But if you add or subtract the same number of pixels from each dimension, the shape distorts because the ratio between width and height shifts.

**Child:** So proportionality is about multiplicative change, not additive change. Like the example with Ananya and her father -- when she was 5 and he was 35, the ratio was 1 to 7. Ten years later they are 15 and 45, with ratio 1 to 3. Their ages changed by addition, not multiplication, so the ratio did not stay the same.

**Mother:** This distinction is one of the most fundamental ideas in mathematics. Proportional reasoning touches every part of life -- adjusting dosa batter recipes, reading map distances, calculating medicine dosages based on a child's weight, even mixing paint colours for Rangoli designs.

**Child:** In the chai recipe problem, if 4 cups need 6 spoons of sugar, then 14 cups need 21 spoons -- because the scaling factor is 3.5 for both.

**Mother:** And the quickest check is cross-multiplication. If a over b equals c over d, then a times d must equal b times c.

## Ratios and Simplest Form

**Child:** Why do we bother simplifying ratios to their simplest form, Maa?

**Mother:** Because it makes comparison effortless. The ratios 80 to 60, 40 to 30, and 120 to 90 look completely different, but once you simplify, they are all 4 to 3. Without that step, you would need a lot of mental effort to check whether they are the same proportion.

**Child:** You simplify by dividing both terms by their HCF. For 80 and 60, the HCF is 20, so you get 4 to 3.

**Mother:** Think of the boundary wall problem. Mohan's 75 feet with 5 bags and Suresh's 45 feet with 3 bags both simplify to 15 to 1. Both walls use the same amount of cement per foot, so they are equally strong -- even though the raw numbers look different.

**Child:** That is like normalising -- converting everything to the same scale so comparison is fair.

**Mother:** Precisely. When your grandmother compares prices at two different shops, she does the same thing in her head. "This shop charges Rs 360 for 3 kg, that shop charges Rs 480 for 4 kg -- both are Rs 120 per kg, so the same rate."

## The Rule of Three (Trairasika)

**Child:** Aryabhata called this method Trairasika -- the Rule of Three. You use three known quantities to find a fourth unknown. Why was it such a big deal historically?

**Mother:** Because it solved nearly every practical problem in ancient trade. How much grain for a different-sized army? How much tax for a different area of land? How long will a journey take at a different speed? Aryabhata named the three quantities pramana, phala, and ichchha -- the known measure, the known result, and the desired measure.

**Child:** So if 7 kg of mangoes costs 490 rupees, and I want 11 kg: pramana is 7, phala is 490, ichchha is 11. The ichchha-phala is 490 times 11 divided by 7, which is 770 rupees.

**Mother:** That is exactly cross-multiplication at work. If 7 to 490 is proportional to 11 to x, then 7x equals 490 times 11, giving x equals 770.

**Child:** The unitary method -- finding the price of 1 kg first -- seems easier sometimes. 490 divided by 7 is 70 rupees per kg, so 11 kg costs 770 rupees.

**Mother:** Both approaches give identical answers. The unitary method is often simpler to explain, while cross-multiplication is quicker when the numbers do not divide evenly. Choose whichever feels more natural for the specific problem.

## Flavour Ratios in the Kitchen

**Child:** The mango lassi example is relatable, Maa! Regular lassi has pulp to yoghurt in ratio 2 to 3. Stronger flavour has ratio 1 to 1. How do I compare these systematically?

**Mother:** Convert them to percentages or compare as fractions. The ratio 2 to 3 means 2 parts pulp out of 5 total, which is 40 percent pulp. The ratio 1 to 1 means 1 part out of 2 total, which is 50 percent pulp. So the second version has a stronger mango taste.

**Child:** Higher proportion of pulp means more intense flavour. This connects to percentages too!

**Mother:** Absolutely. Ratios and percentages are twin ways of expressing the same idea. When your naani adjusts her pickle recipe -- a bit more mustard, a touch less salt -- she is tweaking ratios. That intuitive sense of proportion is mathematical thinking, even if she does not write equations.

**Child:** Maa, your rasam is always perfect because you get the tamarind-to-water ratio just right!

**Mother:** Ha! That is proportional reasoning in action, beta. Getting the balance right -- in cooking, in art, in engineering -- is what creates quality.

## When Proportionality Breaks Down

**Child:** The ages example shows that adding the same number to both terms changes the ratio. Are there other situations where proportionality fails?

**Mother:** Many. If doubling the number of cooks does not halve the cooking time because the kitchen is too small, the relationship is not proportional. If doubling the study hours does not double the marks because of fatigue, that is also non-proportional.

**Child:** So real life is messier than neat proportion sums?

**Mother:** Often, yes. But proportional reasoning gives you a starting estimate. Engineers first calculate what the answer would be if things scaled perfectly, then adjust for real-world complications like friction, delays, and human error.

**Child:** For exams, I should first identify whether the problem involves direct proportion, inverse proportion, or neither.

**Mother:** Exactly. Ask yourself: if I double one quantity, does the other also double (direct), get halved (inverse), or do something unpredictable? That one question guides your entire approach.
