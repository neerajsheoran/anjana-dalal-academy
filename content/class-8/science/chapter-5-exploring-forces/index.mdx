## What Exactly Is a Force?

![What exactly is a force — push and pull in everyday actions](what-exactly-is-a-force.png)

Consider the everyday actions you perform without a second thought -- swinging open a classroom door, dragging a heavy suitcase, hoisting a bucket of water from a well, or striking a hockey ball. In every one of these actions, you are either **pushing** or **pulling** an object.

In science, any push or pull acting on an object is called a **force**.

The **SI unit of force** is the **newton** (symbol: **N**), named after the legendary physicist Sir Isaac Newton.

> **Key idea:** A force arises whenever two objects interact. You cannot have a force without at least two objects -- one applying the push or pull and another receiving it.

---

## What Effects Can a Force Produce?

![Effects a force can produce — motion, speed, direction, shape](what-effects-can-a-force-produce.png)

A force is capable of producing several distinct effects:

1. **Setting a stationary object into motion** -- Pushing a stalled bicycle starts it rolling
2. **Altering the speed of a moving object** -- A goalkeeper catching a football brings it from high speed to a complete stop
3. **Changing an object's direction** -- A badminton player smashing a shuttlecock sends it flying in a new direction
4. **Deforming an object** -- Squeezing a rubber ball in your hand changes its shape
5. **Producing a combination of these effects** simultaneously

> **Key idea:** Whenever you observe a change in speed, direction, or shape, you can be certain that a force is responsible. No such change occurs without a force acting.

### What About Objects That Are Perfectly Still?

![What about objects that are perfectly still — balanced forces](what-about-objects-that-are-perfectly-still.png)

A book resting quietly on your study table is not free from forces. Gravity pulls it downward while the table pushes it upward with an equal and opposite force. These forces are perfectly **balanced**, so the book remains motionless. Balanced forces result in no change in motion.

## Classifying Forces

Forces fall into two broad categories depending on whether physical contact between objects is required.

![Diagram showing examples of contact forces (push, pull, friction) and non-contact forces (gravity, magnetic, electrostatic)](classifying-forces.png)

### Contact Forces

These forces operate **only when objects physically touch** each other. The contact may be direct (your hand on a door) or through an intermediary (pulling a cart using a rope).

### Non-Contact Forces

These forces act **across empty space** -- the objects involved do not need to touch at all.

## Contact Forces in Detail

### 1. Muscular Force

![Muscular force — actions powered by muscles](muscular-force.png)

Every physical action your body performs -- walking, climbing stairs, lifting your school bag, chewing food, or even blinking -- relies on the contraction and relaxation of **muscles**. The force generated by muscles is called **muscular force**.

- Animals of all kinds use muscular force: a cheetah sprinting, a fish swimming, an eagle soaring
- For thousands of years, Indian farmers have used bullocks to plough fields and camels to transport goods across Rajasthan's deserts
- Inside your body, muscles power your heartbeat, push food through your digestive tract, and expand and contract your lungs for breathing

### 2. Friction

![Friction — surfaces resisting motion](friction-1.png)

![Friction — second illustration of resistance to sliding](friction-2.png)

Roll a marble across the floor. No matter how hard you flick it, it eventually slows down and stops. Something is steadily draining its motion. That invisible brake is **friction**.

**Friction** is the force that resists the motion (or attempted motion) of one surface sliding over another. It always acts in the **direction opposite** to the object's movement.

**Why does friction exist?**

![Why does friction exist — microscopic surface irregularities](why-does-friction-exist.png)

Even surfaces that look perfectly smooth under your eyes are actually covered with microscopic bumps and grooves. When two surfaces come together, these tiny irregularities interlock, creating resistance against sliding.

**Important properties of friction:**
- Rough surfaces generate **more friction** than smooth surfaces
- The magnitude of friction depends on the **nature of the surfaces** in contact and the force pressing them together
- Friction is a **contact force** because it requires two surfaces to touch
- Even air and water exert friction on objects moving through them -- this is why racing cars, bullet trains, and fighter jets are designed with sleek, **streamlined shapes** to cut through air resistance

> **Key idea:** Friction is a double-edged sword. Without it, you could not walk, write, or grip anything. But in machines, friction wastes energy as heat and causes parts to wear out, which is why engineers use lubricants and ball bearings to reduce it where it is unwanted.

## Non-Contact Forces in Detail

### 1. Magnetic Force

![Magnetic force — attraction and repulsion between poles](magnetic-force-1.png)

![Magnetic force — second illustration of pole interactions](magnetic-force-2.png)

A magnet can attract an iron key lying nearby without touching it. This invisible pull (or push) is the **magnetic force**.

- **Like poles repel**: placing two North poles (or two South poles) near each other pushes them apart
- **Unlike poles attract**: a North pole and a South pole pull towards each other

Thread two ring magnets onto a vertical pencil with their like poles facing each other. The upper magnet hovers in mid-air above the lower one -- a vivid demonstration that magnetic force acts without any contact.

### 2. Electrostatic Force

![Electrostatic force — charged comb attracting paper bits](electrostatic-force.png)

Rub a plastic comb briskly through your dry hair, then hold it near tiny scraps of paper. The paper bits leap towards the comb and cling to it! Rubbing builds up **static electric charges** on the comb's surface, and these charges exert a force on nearby objects.

**Two types of electric charges exist:**
- **Positive** charge
- **Negative** charge

**Rules governing charges:**

![Rules governing charges — like repels, unlike attracts](electrostatic-force-rules-governing-charges.png)

- **Like charges repel** (two positively charged objects push each other away)
- **Unlike charges attract** (a positive and a negative charge pull towards each other)

The force between charged objects is called **electrostatic force**. It operates without any physical contact between the objects.

> **Key idea:** When static charges begin to flow through a conductor in an organised way, they create what we call electric current -- the same current that lights up our homes and powers our devices!

### 3. Gravitational Force

![Gravitational force — Earth pulls every object towards its centre](gravitational-force.png)

Toss a ball straight up into the sky. No matter how powerfully you throw, it always arcs back down. The Earth is constantly **pulling** every object towards its centre. This invisible, ever-present pull is called **gravitational force** (or simply **gravity**).

**Essential features of gravitational force:**
- It is a **non-contact force** -- it acts even across vast distances
- It is always **attractive** -- gravity only pulls, never pushes
- It acts on every object regardless of its material, shape, or colour

**The journey of a ball thrown upward:**

![The journey of a ball thrown upward — going up, peak, coming down](the-journey-of-a-ball-thrown-upward-the-journey-of-a-ball-thrown-upward.png)

1. **Going up**: Speed decreases steadily because gravity opposes the upward motion
2. **At the peak**: The ball pauses for an instant (speed momentarily becomes zero)
3. **Coming down**: Speed increases steadily because gravity accelerates it earthward

## Weight and How It Is Measured

The gravitational pull that Earth exerts on an object is called the object's **weight**. Since weight is a force, its unit is the **newton (N)**.

### Measuring Weight

![Measuring weight using a spring balance](measuring-weight.png)

Weight is measured using a **spring balance**. When you hang an object from the hook:
- The spring stretches under the pull of gravity
- Different objects stretch the spring by different amounts
- The scale reading gives the weight in newtons

### Understanding the Difference Between Weight and Mass

| Property | Mass | Weight |
|----------|------|--------|
| **Definition** | The quantity of matter contained in an object | The gravitational force pulling the object downward |
| **SI Unit** | kilogram (kg) | newton (N) |
| **Does it change with location?** | No -- remains identical everywhere | Yes -- varies depending on local gravity |
| **On the Moon** | Same as on Earth | Roughly one-sixth of Earth weight |

**Weight of a 1 kg object on different celestial bodies:**
- Earth: 10 N
- Moon: 1.6 N
- Mars: 3.8 N
- Venus: 9 N
- Jupiter: 25.4 N

> **Key idea:** Your mass is a fixed property -- it stays the same whether you are in Mumbai, on Mount Everest, or on Mars. But your weight depends entirely on the gravitational pull of wherever you happen to be. A 1 kg bag of rice that weighs 10 N on Earth would weigh just 1.6 N on the Moon!

## Why Do Some Things Float and Others Sink?

Drop a stone and a cork piece into a bucket of water. The stone sinks straight to the bottom while the cork bobs on the surface. If gravity pulls everything downward, why does the cork not sink too?

### The Upward Push: Buoyant Force

![Object in water showing downward gravitational force and upward buoyant force arrows](the-upward-push-buoyant-force.png)

When any object is placed in a liquid, the liquid pushes it **upward**. This upward push is called the **buoyant force** or **upthrust**.

**What decides whether an object floats or sinks?**
- If gravity pulling the object **downward** is stronger than the buoyant force pushing it **upward**, the object **sinks**
- If the two forces are **equal**, the object **floats**

The strength of the buoyant force is related to the **density** of the liquid and the volume of liquid pushed aside by the object (you will explore density in greater detail in a later chapter).

### Archimedes and the Eureka Moment

The ancient Greek thinker **Archimedes** discovered that when an object is placed in a liquid -- whether fully or partially submerged -- the liquid exerts an upward force equal to the weight of the liquid displaced by the object. This insight, known as Archimedes' Principle, explains everything from why ships float to why you feel lighter in a swimming pool.

### A Rock That Floats!

**Pumice** is a volcanic rock formed when frothy, gas-filled lava cools rapidly. The trapped gas bubbles make pumice so lightweight that it actually **floats on water** -- one of the rare natural rocks that can do so!

## Key Takeaways

- A **force** is a push or pull arising from interaction between objects. Its SI unit is the **newton (N)**.
- Force can alter an object's speed, direction of motion, shape, or any combination of these.
- **Contact forces** require physical touch -- muscular force and friction are key examples.
- **Non-contact forces** act across distance without touch -- magnetic force, electrostatic force, and gravitational force.
- **Friction** opposes motion and depends on the roughness of surfaces in contact.
- **Gravitational force** always pulls objects towards Earth and is never repulsive.
- **Weight** is the gravitational force on an object (measured in newtons). **Mass** is the amount of matter (measured in kilograms).
- Mass is constant everywhere; weight changes with local gravitational strength.
- **Buoyant force** (upthrust) is the upward push a liquid exerts on a submerged object. The balance between buoyant force and gravity determines whether an object floats or sinks.
