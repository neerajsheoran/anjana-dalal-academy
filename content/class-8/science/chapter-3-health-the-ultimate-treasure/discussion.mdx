## What Does It Really Mean to Be Healthy?

**Meera:** Arjun, the WHO definition of health is wider than I thought. Not just "no disease" — physical, mental, AND social well-being. Why does mental health get equal billing?

**Arjun:** Because the mind and body are wired together. When you are stressed about an exam, do you sometimes get a headache or a stomachache out of nowhere?

**Meera:** All the time during finals last term.

**Arjun:** That is your mental state pulling your body around. On the other side, when you actually play outside, you feel calmer and lighter. Physical activity releases chemicals in your brain that lift your mood.

**Meera:** Ayurveda talks about this same balance, no? Dinacharya and ritucharya — daily and seasonal routines.

**Arjun:** Yaar, it is wild how those old routines line up with modern research. Waking up early, doing surya namaskar, eating freshly cooked seasonal food, sleeping at a regular time — labs now show these consistent rhythms lower stress hormones and improve sleep quality.

**Meera:** And social health? Just spending time with people?

**Arjun:** Real effect. People who feel lonely or cut off actually fall sick more often — their immune systems get weaker. Festivals like Diwali, Onam, Eid bring families together. That togetherness is not just fun, it genuinely strengthens health.

## Communicable vs Non-Communicable Diseases

**Meera:** Communicable diseases spread through germs — air, water, contact, mosquitoes. Non-communicable ones like diabetes and cancer do not spread. Which is the bigger problem in India today?

**Arjun:** India has a double burden. TB and malaria have not vanished, but non-communicable diseases now cause most deaths. Heart disease, diabetes, cancer have shot up in just one or two generations.

**Meera:** Why so fast?

**Arjun:** Think about what our naani used to eat. Roti from freshly ground atta, seasonal sabzi from the neighbourhood vendor, buttermilk, fruits in season. Now compare with pizza, chips, cold drinks, packaged Maggi. Add hours of screen sitting instead of running in the gali. That is the recipe.

**Meera:** For communicable diseases the chapter lists simple stuff — handwashing, covering coughs, drinking safe water. Do these actually shift the needle?

**Arjun:** COVID basically proved it to the whole world. Twenty seconds of soap and water, plus masks, slashed transmission. We watched it happen.

**Meera:** I still remember the handwashing posters everywhere. Tata 1mg was delivering sanitiser like it was a daily essential.

**Arjun:** Simple does not mean unimportant. Those basic habits saved lakhs of lives in India alone.

## How Vaccines Protect Us

**Meera:** How does a vaccine actually train the immune system? It sounds almost magical.

**Arjun:** It is clever, not magical. Your immune system is like a security guard. A vaccine shows it a harmless look-alike of the real germ — weakened, killed, or just a fragment. The guard studies the look-alike, learns its features, and builds special weapons called antibodies.

**Meera:** And memory cells that remember the criminal for years?

**Arjun:** Exactly. So when the real germ shows up later, the guard recognises it instantly and crushes it before it can do real damage.

**Meera:** Edward Jenner used cowpox to protect against smallpox in the 1790s. But the chapter says India had a tradition called variolation even before that — teekedaars who deliberately gave people a mild smallpox infection to build immunity.

**Arjun:** Yaar, that is the fascinating bit. Healers had noticed that smallpox survivors never caught it again. So they figured out a practical way to induce a mild version. The CONCEPT of training the body's defences was already understood in India.

**Meera:** And during COVID, India produced billions of vaccine doses. Remember the polio drops we got as toddlers? Mission Indradhanush kept extending that coverage.

**Arjun:** Bharat Biotech and ICMR developed Covaxin, an entirely Indian vaccine. India also shipped vaccines to dozens of other countries. ASHA workers and ANMs went door to door. That logistics chain was extraordinary.

## Antibiotics and the Resistance Problem

**Meera:** Antibiotics kill bacteria, not viruses. So why do so many people pop antibiotics for a common cold?

**Arjun:** People self-medicate. They walk into a pharmacy, buy antibiotics without a prescription, hoping to recover faster. But colds are viral. Antibiotics do nothing against them. Worse, the unnecessary use creates a serious problem called antibiotic resistance.

**Meera:** What happens with resistance exactly?

**Arjun:** When bacteria get exposed to antibiotics repeatedly — especially small or incomplete doses — a few naturally tougher bacteria survive. They multiply, and their offspring inherit the same toughness. Over time you end up with bacteria that no antibiotic can touch.

**Meera:** So careless antibiotic use today could mean these drugs do not work when someone really needs them later.

**Arjun:** Exactly. The WHO calls antibiotic resistance one of the biggest threats to human health. India is one of the world's largest consumers, so this is on us. Three rules: only take antibiotics on prescription, take the exact dose, and always finish the full course.

**Meera:** Why is finishing the course so important? Once I feel better I want to stop.

**Arjun:** If you stop early, you have killed the weaker bacteria but the toughest ones survive. With no competition left, they multiply freely — and now you have a resistant population growing inside you.

**Meera:** Yaar. So that "complete the course" sentence on the back of the strip is not pharmacy fluff. It is the difference between a working antibiotic and a useless one.

## Building Healthy Habits for Life

**Meera:** What are the most important things we can actually do right now to stay healthy?

**Arjun:** Five things, and none of them cost money. First, eat the balanced food cooked at home — dal, roti, sabzi, rice, curd, seasonal fruits. Second, play outside for at least an hour every day — cricket, cycling, badminton, anything. Third, sleep eight to nine hours, no negotiation. Fourth, wash hands before meals and after the bathroom. Fifth, do not bottle up your feelings — talk to someone you trust.

**Meera:** The chapter mentions cutting screen time. Is that really a health issue or just adults complaining?

**Arjun:** Actually backed by science. Too much screen time strains your eyes, ruins your posture, disturbs your sleep because blue light interferes with melatonin, and replaces movement with sitting. Studies show kids who get more than two hours a day of recreational screens have higher rates of anxiety and attention problems.

**Meera:** Mummy makes me do surya namaskar every morning. Is yoga actually proven to help?

**Arjun:** International Yoga Day on 21st June is celebrated in over 170 countries. AIIMS research has shown regular yoga lowers blood pressure, reduces cortisol, improves lung capacity, enhances flexibility.

**Meera:** Rishis figured it out thousands of years ago. Modern clinical evidence is just catching up.

**Arjun:** That gift to the world came from here. Worth taking a little pride in.
