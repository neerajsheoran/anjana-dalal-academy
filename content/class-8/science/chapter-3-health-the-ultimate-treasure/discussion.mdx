## What Does It Really Mean to Be Healthy?

**Child:** Maa, the WHO says health is not just about not being sick -- it includes physical, mental, and social well-being. Why does mental health matter so much?

**Mother:** Because your mind and body are deeply linked, beta. When you are constantly stressed about exams, have you noticed you sometimes get headaches or an upset stomach? That is your mental state affecting your body. On the other hand, when you play outside and exercise, you feel happier and calmer. Physical activity releases chemicals in your brain that improve your mood.

**Child:** Ayurveda also talks about this balance, right? The dinacharya and ritucharya concepts -- daily and seasonal routines for wellness?

**Mother:** Yes! Waking up early, doing some stretching or surya namaskar, eating freshly cooked seasonal food, and going to bed at a reasonable hour -- these are not just old-fashioned rules. Modern medical research has confirmed that consistent daily routines reduce stress hormones and improve sleep quality. Our ancestors understood through observation what laboratories now prove with data.

**Child:** And social health means having good relationships? Like spending time with family and friends?

**Mother:** Exactly. Studies have found that people who feel lonely or isolated actually get sick more often -- their immune systems weaken. Think about how festivals like Diwali, Onam, and Eid bring families and communities together. That togetherness is not just fun -- it genuinely strengthens your health. The Indian tradition of living in joint families and visiting neighbours regularly has real health benefits.

## Communicable vs Non-Communicable Diseases

**Child:** Maa, communicable diseases spread through germs -- air, water, contact, or mosquitoes. Non-communicable ones like diabetes and cancer do not spread. Which type is a bigger problem in India today?

**Mother:** India faces a double burden, beta. Communicable diseases like TB and malaria have not disappeared, but non-communicable diseases are now responsible for the majority of deaths. Heart disease, diabetes, and cancer are rising rapidly because our eating habits and activity levels have changed dramatically in just one or two generations.

**Child:** What changed so much?

**Mother:** Think about what Naani used to eat -- roti made from freshly ground atta, seasonal sabzi from the local market, buttermilk, and fresh fruits. Now compare that with the pizza, chips, cold drinks, and packaged noodles that many children eat regularly. Add to that sitting for hours watching screens instead of playing in the gali, and you have a recipe for lifestyle diseases.

**Child:** For communicable diseases, the chapter lists simple prevention methods -- handwashing, covering coughs, drinking safe water. Do these really make a big difference?

**Mother:** During COVID, the whole world saw the power of these basic measures. Just washing hands with soap for 20 seconds and wearing masks reduced disease transmission dramatically. Simple does not mean unimportant -- these habits saved lakhs of lives in India alone.

## How Vaccines Protect Us

**Child:** Maa, how does a vaccine actually train the immune system? It sounds almost like magic.

**Mother:** It is clever science, not magic! Imagine your immune system is like a security guard. A vaccine introduces a harmless look-alike of the actual criminal -- a weakened, killed, or partial version of the germ. The security guard studies this look-alike carefully, learns its features, and creates special weapons (antibodies) designed specifically against it. It also creates memory cells that remember this criminal for years.

**Child:** So when the real germ shows up later, the immune system is already prepared?

**Mother:** Yes! It recognises the invader immediately and destroys it before it can make you seriously ill. That is the beauty of acquired immunity.

**Child:** Edward Jenner used cowpox to protect against smallpox in the 1790s. But the chapter says India already had a tradition of variolation before that -- teekedaars who deliberately gave people mild smallpox to build immunity!

**Mother:** That is one of the fascinating parts of Indian medical history. Traditional healers observed that people who survived smallpox never got it again, and they developed a practical method of inducing mild infections for protection. The concept of training the body's defences was understood in India long before it was formalised in Europe.

**Child:** And during COVID, India produced billions of vaccine doses. The Serum Institute and Bharat Biotech both developed and manufactured vaccines at incredible speed.

**Mother:** ICMR worked with Bharat Biotech to develop Covaxin, an entirely Indian vaccine. India also supplied vaccines to dozens of other countries. It showed the world that Indian science and manufacturing can rise to meet the biggest challenges.

## Antibiotics and the Resistance Problem

**Child:** Antibiotics kill bacteria but not viruses. So why do some people take antibiotics for a common cold, Maa?

**Mother:** That is a very important question. Many people self-medicate -- they go to the chemist and buy antibiotics without a prescription because they think it will make them better faster. But since colds are caused by viruses, antibiotics do nothing against them. Worse, this unnecessary use creates a terrifying problem called antibiotic resistance.

**Child:** What happens with antibiotic resistance?

**Mother:** When bacteria are repeatedly exposed to antibiotics -- especially in small or incomplete doses -- some bacteria survive because they happen to have a slight natural advantage. These survivors multiply, and their offspring inherit the resistance. Over time, we end up with bacteria that no antibiotic can kill.

**Child:** So taking antibiotics carelessly now could mean they will not work when someone truly needs them in the future?

**Mother:** Precisely. The WHO has called antibiotic resistance one of the greatest threats to human health. India, being one of the world's largest consumers of antibiotics, has a particular responsibility. Always remember three rules: take antibiotics only when a doctor prescribes them, take the exact dosage prescribed, and always complete the full course even if you feel better halfway through.

**Child:** Why must you finish the full course?

**Mother:** If you stop early, you may have killed the weaker bacteria but left the strongest ones alive. Those strong survivors then multiply without competition, creating a population of resistant bacteria. Finishing the course ensures every last bacterium is eliminated.

## Building Healthy Habits for Life

**Child:** Maa, what are the most important things I can do right now to stay healthy?

**Mother:** Five things, beta, and none of them cost money. First, eat the balanced food I cook at home -- dal, roti, sabzi, rice, curd, and seasonal fruits. Second, play outside for at least an hour every day -- cricket, cycling, running, whatever you enjoy. Third, sleep eight to nine hours every night without fail. Fourth, wash your hands with soap before every meal and after using the bathroom. And fifth, do not bottle up your feelings -- talk to me, to your friends, to your teacher when something bothers you.

**Child:** The chapter mentions reducing screen time. Is that really a health issue or just something adults say?

**Mother:** It is genuinely backed by science. Too much screen time strains your eyes, ruins your posture, disturbs your sleep because the blue light from screens interferes with melatonin production, and replaces physical activity with sitting. Research shows that children who spend more than two hours a day on recreational screens have higher rates of anxiety and attention problems.

**Child:** You always make me do surya namaskar in the morning. Is yoga actually proven to help?

**Mother:** International Yoga Day is celebrated on 21st June by over 170 countries -- that alone tells you how seriously the world takes it. Research from top institutions like AIIMS has shown that regular yoga practice lowers blood pressure, reduces the stress hormone cortisol, improves breathing capacity, and enhances flexibility. What our rishis practised thousands of years ago, modern science now confirms with clinical evidence. You should feel proud that this gift to the world came from India.
