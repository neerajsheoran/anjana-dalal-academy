## What Makes Up Everything Around Us?

![What makes up everything around us — matter built from tiny particles](what-makes-up-everything-around-us.png)

Pick up a lump of jaggery (gur) from the kitchen. Crush it between your fingers -- it crumbles into smaller bits. Pound those bits in a mortar -- they become a fine brown powder. No matter how much you grind, every tiny grain is still jaggery. It still tastes sweet. Its colour has not changed. All you have done is reduce the **size** of each piece, not turned it into something new. This is a **physical change**.

If you could keep crushing far beyond what any pestle can manage, you would eventually arrive at the tiniest unit of jaggery that still behaves like jaggery. These smallest building blocks are called the **constituent particles** of that substance.

> **Key idea:** All matter -- whether it is a rock, a raindrop, or the air we breathe -- is made up of incredibly tiny particles called constituent particles. These particles are far too small to see with any ordinary microscope.

### The Disappearing Salt Trick

![The disappearing salt trick — particles slip into interparticle spaces](the-disappearing-salt-trick.png)

Drop a spoonful of salt into a glass of warm water and stir. Within moments the salt vanishes. Has it been destroyed? Not at all -- taste the water, and it is salty! When salt dissolves, it breaks into its constituent particles. These particles are so minuscule that they slip into the **tiny gaps between the water particles**. These gaps are called **interparticle spaces**.

### India's Ancient Particle Thinker

Thousands of years ago, the Indian philosopher **Acharya Kanad** reasoned that if you keep splitting matter into smaller and smaller pieces, you must eventually reach a particle that **cannot be divided any further**. He called this tiniest particle a **Parmanu**. His ideas were recorded in the *Vaisheshika Sutras* and represent one of the earliest atomic theories in human history.

## What Determines Whether Something Is Solid, Liquid, or Gas?

![What determines whether something is solid, liquid, or gas — interparticle forces](what-determines-whether-something-is-solid-liquid-or-gas-1.png)

![What determines whether something is solid, liquid, or gas — second view of the factors](what-determines-whether-something-is-solid-liquid-or-gas-2.png)

The particles of any substance pull on each other with invisible forces called **interparticle attractions**. How strong these attractions are depends on two things:
- The **type of substance** (iron particles attract each other far more strongly than water particles do)
- The **distance between particles** -- even a tiny increase in distance weakens these forces dramatically

It is the balance between these attraction forces and the energy the particles carry that decides whether a substance will be a **solid**, a **liquid**, or a **gas**.

## The Solid State

![The solid state — particles tightly packed in fixed positions](the-solid-state.png)

In a solid, particles sit **extremely close together**, and the forces pulling them toward one another are **very powerful**. Each particle is locked in a fixed spot, unable to wander.

**What solids are like:**
- They hold a **definite shape** and a **definite volume**
- Particles can only **jiggle back and forth** (vibrate) in their fixed positions -- like students sitting in assigned seats during an exam
- They resist being squeezed into a smaller space (nearly incompressible)
- They do not flow

**Everyday examples:** A steel spoon, a block of ice, a grain of rock salt, a wooden ruler, a copper coin

### Melting: When a Solid Becomes a Liquid

![Melting — when a solid becomes a liquid](melting-when-a-solid-becomes-a-liquid.png)

Heat a solid and its particles start vibrating harder. At a specific temperature, the vibrations become so energetic that particles begin to break free from their locked positions. The forces holding them weaken, and the solid transforms into a liquid.

This specific temperature is called the **melting point** -- the minimum temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid at normal atmospheric pressure.

| Substance | Melting Point |
|-----------|--------------|
| Ice (frozen water) | 0 degrees C |
| Urea | 133 degrees C |
| Iron | 1538 degrees C |

Substances whose particles cling to each other weakly melt at low temperatures. Those with very strong interparticle forces need much more heat to melt.

## The Liquid State

![The liquid state — particles sliding past one another](the-liquid-state.png)

In a liquid, particles are a bit more spread out than in a solid. The attractions between them are **somewhat weaker** -- strong enough to keep them fairly close, but not strong enough to lock them in place.

**What liquids are like:**
- They have a **definite volume** but take on the **shape of whatever container** they are poured into
- Particles can **slide past one another** within a limited region
- They are almost impossible to compress

Pour 150 mL of milk into a tall glass, a flat bowl, and a round flask -- the milk looks different each time, but the volume stays 150 mL.

### Boiling: When a Liquid Becomes a Gas

![Boiling — when a liquid becomes a gas](boiling-when-a-liquid-becomes-a-gas.png)

![Boiling vs evaporation — surface-only evaporation at any temperature](boiling-when-a-liquid-becomes-a-gas-evaporation.png)

Keep heating a liquid, and the particles move faster and faster. At a certain temperature, they gain enough energy to escape the liquid entirely. Bubbles form throughout the liquid and burst at the surface -- the liquid is boiling.

The **boiling point** is the temperature at which a liquid converts into vapour at atmospheric pressure, with bubbles forming throughout the body of the liquid.

**Evaporation** is a gentler process. It happens at **any temperature** (even well below the boiling point), but it occurs only at the surface and proceeds slowly. A wet school uniform hanging on a clothesline in Delhi dries through evaporation, even though the water on it is nowhere near 100 degrees C.

> **Key idea:** Liquids and gases share two traits -- both can flow and neither holds a fixed shape. Because of this, they are grouped together as **fluids**.

## The Gaseous State

![The gaseous state — particles dart freely with negligible attraction](the-gaseous-state.png)

In a gas, the attractive forces between particles are so weak they are practically **negligible**. Particles have more than enough energy to break free from each other and zoom around **in every direction**.

**What gases are like:**
- No **fixed shape** and no **fixed volume**
- They spread out to **fill every corner** of whatever space they occupy
- Particles dart about randomly at high speeds
- They can be **squeezed** (compressed) easily

### Seeing Gas Particles in Action

![Seeing gas particles in action — agarbatti fragrance spreading](seeing-gas-particles-in-action.png)

Your mother lights an agarbatti during evening prayers in one corner of the living room. Within minutes, the fragrance fills the entire house -- you can smell it in the bedroom, the kitchen, even the balcony. How? The agarbatti releases tiny fragrance particles into the air. These particles collide with the constantly moving air particles and get bumped from place to place, spreading the scent in every direction.

## Spacing Between Particles in the Three States

### Can You Squeeze a Gas?

![Can you squeeze a gas — syringe experiment](can-you-squeeze-a-gas.png)

Grab a plastic syringe (without a needle). Pull the plunger back to fill it with air. Now seal the tip with your finger and push the plunger forward. You can compress the air inside to a noticeably smaller volume. This works because gas particles are spread **far apart** -- there is plenty of empty space to squeeze them into.

Try the same thing with water filling the syringe: the plunger barely moves. Water particles are already sitting close together, leaving almost no room to push them closer.

### The Sugar-Water Experiment

![The sugar-water experiment — dissolved sugar fills interparticle gaps](the-sugar-water-experiment.png)

Here is a neat demonstration of interparticle spaces in liquids:
1. Fill a measuring cylinder to a certain mark with water
2. Add a spoonful of sugar and note the new level -- it rises
3. Stir until the sugar fully dissolves and check the level again -- it drops slightly below the mark you expected
4. This small dip happens because the dissolved sugar particles have settled into the **gaps between the water particles**

### Comparing the Three States at a Glance

| Property | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|----------|-------|--------|-----|
| **Spacing between particles** | Very small | Slightly larger than in solids | Very large |
| **How particles are packed** | Tightly, in fixed positions | Loosely, but still close | Widely scattered |
| **Attraction between particles** | Very strong | Moderately strong | Almost zero |
| **How particles move** | Vibrate in place | Slide around within a limited space | Dart freely in all directions |
| **Shape** | Fixed | Adapts to container | No fixed shape |
| **Volume** | Fixed | Fixed | Expands to fill available space |

> **Key idea:** The spaces between particles in a solid are not filled with air or any other substance -- they are simply **empty gaps** between closely packed particles.

## How Particles Move in Each State

### Movement in Liquids

![Movement in liquids — potassium permanganate spreading in water](movement-in-liquids.png)

Drop a single crystal of **potassium permanganate** (the purple antiseptic crystals available at any medical shop) into a glass of still water. Do not stir. At first, you see deep purple streaks spiralling downward from the crystal. Over the next several minutes, the purple colour spreads evenly until the entire glass is a uniform light pink.

This spreading happens because water particles are in **continuous motion**. They bump into the permanganate particles and gradually carry them throughout the liquid.

**Temperature changes the speed:**
- In **hot water**, the colour spreads rapidly -- within a minute or two
- In **water at room temperature**, it takes several minutes
- In **ice-cold water**, it can take much longer

This confirms that particles **move faster when given more heat energy**.

### Movement in Gases

![Movement in gases — pakora aroma spreading through the chawl](movement-in-gases.png)

When someone fries pakoras in the kitchen of a Mumbai chawl, the delicious aroma reaches neighbours two floors up within minutes. The oil and spice particles released into the air collide with billions of fast-moving air particles, getting bounced and scattered in every direction until the fragrance fills the entire building.

## The Role of Thermal Energy

![The role of thermal energy — heat governs the state of matter](the-role-of-thermal-energy.png)

**Thermal energy** (heat energy) is the hidden engine behind changes of state:

- **In solids:** Particles have low thermal energy. They are held tightly by strong attractions and can only vibrate.
- **At the melting point:** The added thermal energy is used to weaken the attractive forces. Particles break free from their fixed spots, and the solid becomes a liquid.
- **In liquids:** Particles have enough energy to slide around, but not enough to escape completely. They remain within a limited region.
- **In gases:** Particles carry enough thermal energy to completely overpower the forces of attraction. They fly apart and move freely.

### A Familiar Application: How Detergent Cleans Oily Dishes

![A familiar application — how detergent cleans oily dishes](a-familiar-application-how-detergent-cleans-oily-dishes.png)

Ever wondered why plain water cannot wash oil off a steel plate, but a drop of detergent does the job? Here is the particle-level explanation. Each detergent particle has two ends: one end bonds with oil, the other end bonds with water. When you scrub a greasy plate with soapy water, clusters of detergent particles surround each tiny oil droplet -- their oil-loving ends grip the grease, while their water-loving ends face outward into the water. This lifts the oil off the plate and allows it to be washed away.

## Key Takeaways

- All matter is built from **extremely tiny constituent particles** that are invisible to the naked eye.
- These particles are held together by **interparticle forces of attraction**.
- **Solids** have the strongest attractions, the smallest spacing, and a fixed shape and volume. Particles can only vibrate.
- **Liquids** have moderate attractions, slightly more spacing, and a definite volume but no fixed shape. Particles move within a limited space.
- **Gases** have negligible attractions, maximum spacing, and neither fixed shape nor fixed volume. Particles move freely in every direction.
- The **melting point** is the temperature at which a solid transforms into a liquid.
- The **boiling point** is the temperature at which a liquid turns into vapour, with bubble formation throughout.
- **Thermal energy** governs the physical state of matter -- more thermal energy means particles can move more freely.
- At the most fundamental level, the tiny particles that compose all matter are **atoms** and **molecules**.
