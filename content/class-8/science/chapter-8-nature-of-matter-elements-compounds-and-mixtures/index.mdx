## Starting with Mixtures

Open your school tiffin box -- you might see rice, dal, sabzi, and a piece of pickle, all sitting side by side. Each item keeps its own taste, colour, and texture. Together they make a meal, but none of them has chemically changed into something else. This everyday tiffin box is actually a rough model of a **mixture**.

A **mixture** is formed when two or more substances are combined, and each substance **keeps its original properties**. The individual substances inside a mixture are called **components**. Importantly, the components do not undergo any chemical reaction with each other.

### Two Kinds of Mixtures

![Two kinds of mixtures — uniform and non-uniform](two-kinds-of-mixtures.png)

**Non-uniform mixtures:** You can spot the different components with your own eyes (or a magnifying glass). Think of a bowl of **chaat** -- you can clearly see the boiled potatoes, puffed rice, chopped onions, and sev as separate items.

**Uniform mixtures:** The components are so thoroughly blended that every bit of the mixture looks and behaves the same. You cannot pick out individual components, even under a microscope. A glass of **nimbu paani** (lemon water with dissolved sugar and salt) is a good example.

> **Key idea:** In any mixture, every component retains its own characteristics. The components do not react chemically and can usually be separated using physical methods such as filtering, sieving, or evaporation.

### Alloys: Metallic Uniform Mixtures

![Alloys — metallic uniform mixtures like steel, brass, bronze](alloys-metallic-uniform-mixtures.png)

**Alloys** are uniform mixtures where metals (or metals and non-metals) are melted and blended together. Some important alloys:

- **Stainless steel** -- a blend of iron, chromium, nickel, and a trace of carbon (your kitchen utensils are likely made of this)
- **Brass** -- copper mixed with zinc (used in temple bells and decorative items)
- **Bronze** -- copper combined with tin (statues of deities are often cast in bronze)

In ancient Indian texts, alloys were referred to as **Mishraloha** (mixed metals). Works like the *Charaka Samhita* and *Susruta Samhita* describe the use of specific alloys in surgical instruments and medicinal preparations.

## Air: A Mixture We Breathe

![Air — a mixture we breathe with nitrogen, oxygen, argon, and more](air-a-mixture-we-breathe.png)

The air filling this room right now is a **uniform mixture** of several gases:
- **Nitrogen** (roughly 78%) -- the most abundant gas
- **Oxygen** -- vital for breathing and for burning fuels
- **Argon** -- a quiet, unreactive gas
- **Carbon dioxide** -- used by plants for photosynthesis
- **Water vapour** -- its amount varies with humidity

### Proving Carbon Dioxide Exists in Air

![Proving carbon dioxide exists in air — limewater turns milky](proving-carbon-dioxide-exists-in-air.png)

Leave a bowl of clear **lime water** (calcium hydroxide solution) uncovered near an open window for a few hours. It gradually turns **milky white**. This happens because carbon dioxide from the air reacts with the calcium hydroxide to produce tiny solid particles of **calcium carbonate**. The milky appearance confirms that air contains carbon dioxide.

### Dust in the Air

![Dust in the air — pollutants and suspended impurities](dust-in-the-air.png)

Place a piece of dark cloth on a windowsill for a few hours on a windy day. When you check later, you will find a fine layer of tiny particles settled on it. These **dust particles** ride along in the air but are not an essential part of it -- they are considered **pollutants** or **suspended impurities**.

## Mixtures Sorted by Physical State

Components in different physical states can combine to form mixtures:

| Combination | Examples |
|------------|----------|
| Gas + Gas | Air (nitrogen, oxygen, and other gases) |
| Gas + Liquid | Soda water (carbon dioxide dissolved in water) |
| Solid + Gas | Smoke (tiny carbon particles suspended in air) |
| Liquid + Liquid | Vinegar (acetic acid in water), oil floating on water |
| Solid + Liquid | Seawater (salts dissolved in water), muddy river water |
| Solid + Solid | Baking powder, metal alloys like brass |

## What Scientists Mean by "Pure Substance"

In a grocery shop, you might see labels saying "pure ghee" or "pure honey." Here, "pure" means unadulterated -- not mixed with cheaper substitutes. But in **science**, purity has a stricter definition.

A **pure substance** is one that:
- Contains the **same kind of particles** throughout
- **Cannot be separated** into other substances by any physical process (like filtering, boiling, or using a magnet)

By this definition, even the finest "pure ghee" from a shop is a mixture (it contains multiple fats, vitamins, and water traces). Scientific purity means every single particle in the sample is identical.

## Two Types of Pure Substances

Pure substances fall into two categories: **elements** and **compounds**.

### Elements

![Elements — atoms that cannot be broken down further](two-types-of-pure-substances-elemnents.png)

An **element** is a substance that **cannot be broken down** into anything simpler by any chemical or physical method. Elements are the fundamental building blocks from which all matter is constructed.

Every element is made of one kind of particle called an **atom**. The atoms of iron are different from the atoms of oxygen, which are different from the atoms of gold -- each element has its own unique type of atom.

**Common elements grouped by type:**

![Common elements grouped by type — metals, non-metals, metalloids](common-elements-grouped-by-type.png)

- **Metals:** Gold (used in jewellery), iron (used in construction), copper (used in electrical wiring), aluminium (used in aircraft)
- **Non-metals:** Carbon (found in pencil lead and diamonds), sulfur (bright yellow powder), oxygen (the gas we breathe), nitrogen (makes up most of the air)
- **Metalloids:** Silicon (the backbone of computer chips), boron (used in heat-resistant glass) -- these have properties that sit between metals and non-metals

**Noteworthy facts about elements:**

![Noteworthy facts about elements — totals, states at room temperature](noteworthy-facts-about-elements.png)
- Scientists have identified **118 elements** so far
- The vast majority are solids at room temperature
- Only **11 elements** exist as gases at room temperature (all of them are non-metals)
- Just **2 elements** are liquids at room temperature -- mercury (a shiny metal used in old thermometers) and bromine (a reddish-brown non-metal)

> **Key idea:** Atoms of most elements do not like to exist alone. Two or more atoms bond together to form a stable unit called a **molecule**. For instance, two atoms of hydrogen join to form one molecule of hydrogen gas.

### Compounds

![Compounds — atoms of different elements bonded chemically](two-types-of-pure-substances-compounds.png)

A **compound** forms when atoms of two or more different elements **chemically bond** together in a **fixed, definite ratio**. The resulting substance has properties that are **entirely different** from the properties of the elements that created it.

**What makes compounds special:**

![What makes compounds special — fixed ratios, new properties](what-makes-compounds-special.png)

- They always contain their elements in the same fixed ratio
- Their properties bear no resemblance to the individual elements
- You cannot separate them by physical methods (no magnet, no filter, no evaporation will work)
- Breaking them apart requires a **chemical reaction** (like using electricity or strong heat)

**Striking examples:**

![Striking examples — water, salt, sugar formed from very different elements](striking-examples.png)
- **Water** -- built from hydrogen and oxygen (ratio 2:1 by atoms). Hydrogen is a flammable gas, oxygen fans flames, yet water **puts out fires**!
- **Table salt (sodium chloride)** -- formed from sodium (a dangerously reactive soft metal) and chlorine (a toxic yellowish-green gas). Together, they make the harmless white crystals you sprinkle on your food.
- **Sugar (sucrose)** -- composed of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms arranged in a specific structure

### Splitting Water into Its Elements

![Electrolysis of water apparatus showing hydrogen gas collecting at one electrode and oxygen gas at the other](splitting-water-into-its-elements.png)

Pass an electric current through water (with a few drops of dilute sulfuric acid added to help it conduct). Two gases collect at the two electrodes:
- **Hydrogen** at one electrode -- test it with a burning matchstick and it burns with a characteristic "pop" sound
- **Oxygen** at the other -- bring a glowing splint near it and it bursts into bright flame

The volume of hydrogen collected is **double** the volume of oxygen. This confirms water's composition: two parts hydrogen to one part oxygen.

### Decomposing Sugar by Heating

![Decomposing sugar by heating — caramelisation to carbon residue](decomposing-sugar-by-heating.png)

Heat a small amount of sugar in a test tube:
1. It first **melts** and turns golden brown (caramelisation)
2. On further heating, it **blackens** and chars
3. Tiny water droplets appear on the cooler upper walls of the tube
4. A black residue of **charcoal (carbon)** is left behind

This breakdown proves that sugar is a compound of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen.

## Iron and Sulfur: Mixture vs Compound in Action

This classic comparison experiment reveals the core difference between a mixture and a compound using the same two starting materials.

### Sample A -- The Mixture (Iron + Sulfur simply mixed)

![Sample A — the mixture of iron filings and sulfur powder](sample-a-the-mixture-iron-sulfur-simply-mixed.png)

- Iron filings and sulfur powder are stirred together in a dish
- You can see the grey iron and yellow sulfur as distinct particles
- Run a magnet through the mixture: it **picks up the iron** and leaves the sulfur behind
- Add dilute hydrochloric acid: **hydrogen gas** is released (it is colourless, odourless, and pops when lit)

### Sample B -- The Compound (Iron Sulfide, made by heating the mixture)

![Sample B — the iron sulfide compound formed by heating the mixture](sample-b-the-compound-iron-sulfide-made-by-heating-the-mixture.png)

- Heat the iron-sulfur mixture strongly until a dark, solid mass forms
- The resulting substance looks **uniform throughout** -- no grey or yellow patches visible
- A magnet has **no effect** on it (the iron has lost its magnetic property)
- Add dilute hydrochloric acid: **hydrogen sulfide gas** is produced (colourless but smells like rotten eggs -- completely different from the hydrogen gas in Sample A)

> **Key idea:** In a mixture, components keep their individual identities and can be pulled apart by physical methods. In a compound, the elements fuse together chemically to create an entirely new substance whose properties are nothing like those of the original elements.

## Practical Uses of Elements, Compounds, and Mixtures

- **Air** (mixture) -- every living creature depends on it for breathing
- **Water** (compound) -- the basis of all known life
- **Iron and aluminium** (elements) -- the backbone of bridges, buildings, railways, and vehicles across India
- **Stainless steel** (alloy/mixture) -- more durable and rust-resistant than pure iron; used in everything from surgical tools to railway coaches
- **Medicines** -- chemists design compounds that target specific diseases; India is one of the largest producers of generic medicines in the world
- **Fertilisers** -- compounds like urea and ammonium sulfate boost crop yields, supporting Indian agriculture

## Minerals: Nature's Own Substances

![Minerals — nature's own substances with fixed composition](minerals-natures-own-substances.png)

**Minerals** are naturally occurring, solid substances found in the Earth's crust, each with a fixed chemical composition. Most rocks are mixtures of several minerals.

- **Native minerals** are pure elements found freely in nature (for example, gold nuggets, sulfur deposits, and diamond crystals -- which are pure carbon)
- Most minerals, however, are **compounds** made of two or more elements

**Some common minerals:** Quartz (used in watches and electronics), calcite (a key ingredient in cement), mica (the shiny flakes in some rocks), talc (ground into talcum powder)

**Minerals in everyday life:**
- **Cement** is manufactured from a mixture of calcite, quartz, alumina, and iron oxide
- **Talcum powder** comes from the mineral talc, one of the softest minerals known

### Dhokra Art: Ancient Indian Metalwork

![Dhokra art — ancient Indian metalwork using lost-wax casting](dhokra-art-ancient-indian-metalwork.png)

The tribal communities of Bastar (Chhattisgarh) and parts of Odisha and West Bengal practise a stunning metalcraft called **Dhokra**. Artists sculpt figures from beeswax, coat them in layers of clay, melt away the wax, and pour molten brass or bronze into the hollow mould. The result is a beautiful, intricately detailed metal figure. This "lost-wax casting" technique has been passed down for over 4,000 years and is a living example of how understanding alloys and metals can produce breathtaking art.

## What Lies Beyond Matter?

Elements and compounds are the building blocks of **matter** -- anything that has mass and occupies space. But not everything in our world is matter. **Light, sound, heat, electricity, thoughts, and emotions** are crucial parts of our existence, yet none of them has mass or takes up space. They are real, they are important, but they are not made of matter.

## Key Takeaways

- A **mixture** consists of two or more substances combined without chemical reaction. Each component retains its properties and can be separated physically.
- A **pure substance** (in the scientific sense) consists of only one type of particle and cannot be broken apart by physical means.
- **Elements** are the simplest pure substances -- they cannot be decomposed further and serve as the building blocks of everything.
- **Compounds** form when two or more elements bond chemically in a fixed ratio, producing a substance with entirely new properties.
- **Alloys** are uniform mixtures of metals (e.g., stainless steel, brass, bronze).
- **Air** is a uniform mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide, water vapour, and traces of other gases.
- **Minerals** are naturally occurring solid substances with fixed composition, usually compounds but sometimes native elements.
- Not everything is matter: light, heat, sound, electricity, and emotions have no mass and no volume.
