## Curved Mirrors — Beyond the Flat Looking Glass

You have used a flat mirror at home to check your reflection. The image looks just like you — same size, same orientation, standing right behind the glass. But pick up a steel serving bowl from your kitchen. Look at the inside surface, then flip it over and look at the outside. You will notice two very different reflections!

**Spherical mirrors** have reflecting surfaces shaped like a section cut from a hollow ball. Depending on which side does the reflecting, we get two varieties:

### Concave Mirror

When the reflecting surface **curves inward** (like the hollow of a bowl), the mirror is called **concave**. Picture the inside of a satellite dish or a large cooking vessel — that inward curve is the key.

### Convex Mirror

When the reflecting surface **curves outward** (like the back of a shiny steel plate), the mirror is called **convex**. The rounded back of a polished spoon gives you a convex reflection.

> **Key idea:** A flat mirror always shows you an image that matches your size and orientation. Spherical mirrors, however, can produce images that are magnified, reduced, or even flipped upside down — all depending on where the object is placed relative to the mirror.

---

## What Kind of Images Do Curved Mirrors Produce?

### Images from a Concave Mirror

Hold a small toy near a concave mirror:
- **When the toy is very close** — you see an image that is **upright and magnified** (bigger than the toy)
- **When the toy is moved far away** — the image **flips upside down**. At first it appears larger, but as you keep moving the toy farther, the inverted image shrinks progressively

### Images from a Convex Mirror

Now try the same with a convex mirror:
- The image is **always upright** and **always smaller** than the actual object
- Even when you change the distance, the image stays upright — it only gets marginally smaller as the object recedes

### Where Do We Use Concave Mirrors?

- **Torch and vehicle headlamp reflectors** — the inward curve gathers light from a bulb and sends it forward as a strong beam
- **Dentist's inspection mirrors** — a small concave mirror shows teeth in a magnified view, helping the dentist spot tiny cavities
- **Large reflecting telescopes** — astronomers use giant concave mirrors to collect faint starlight
- **Solar cookers and furnaces** — the curved surface focuses sunlight into a hot spot for heating

### Where Do We Use Convex Mirrors?

- **Rear-view mirrors on cars, bikes, and autorickshaws** — they squeeze a wide stretch of road into a small image, giving the driver a broader view. That is why they carry the caution: "Objects in mirror are closer than they appear" — the convex shape makes everything look smaller and farther
- **Blind-corner mirrors** — mounted at sharp turns on hill roads and inside parking garages so people can spot oncoming traffic
- **Security mirrors in jewellery shops and showrooms** — a single convex mirror covers a large area

## The Rules That Govern Reflection

Whenever light strikes a surface and bounces back, we call it **reflection**. Two precise rules describe how this bouncing happens:

### Important Terms

- **Incident ray** — the light beam travelling toward the mirror
- **Reflected ray** — the light beam that bounces away from the mirror
- **Normal** — an imaginary line drawn perpendicular (at 90 degrees) to the mirror surface right where the light lands
- **Angle of incidence (i)** — measured between the incoming ray and the normal
- **Angle of reflection (r)** — measured between the outgoing ray and the normal

![Diagram showing incident ray, reflected ray, normal line, angle of incidence, and angle of reflection on a mirror surface](laws-of-reflection-diagram.png)

### The Two Laws

**First Law:** The angle of incidence always matches the angle of reflection — they are equal in measure.

**Second Law:** The incoming ray, the normal at the point of contact, and the outgoing ray all lie on the same flat surface (the same plane).

> **Key idea:** These two laws hold true for every type of mirror — flat, concave, or convex. No matter the shape of the reflecting surface, each individual light ray obeys these rules at the exact spot where it hits.

## Parallel Light Meeting Different Mirrors

Imagine sending several parallel beams of light toward three different mirrors:

![Comparison of light reflection from plane mirror (parallel rays), concave mirror (converging rays), and convex mirror (diverging rays)](concave-convex-mirror-reflection.png)

- **Flat mirror** — the reflected beams stay **parallel** to each other
- **Concave mirror** — the reflected beams **come together** and meet at a single spot (they converge)
- **Convex mirror** — the reflected beams **fan outward** and spread apart (they diverge)

### Setting Paper Ablaze with a Concave Mirror

Because a concave mirror brings sunlight together into one bright point, that point becomes intensely hot. If you position a sheet of paper at exactly that spot on a sunny afternoon, the concentrated heat can actually set the paper on fire!

This same principle powers **solar concentrators** used across Rajasthan and Gujarat, where large curved reflectors focus sunlight to cook meals, heat water, or even generate electricity.

## Lenses — Letting Light Through

A **lens** is a piece of clear material — typically glass or transparent plastic — with at least one curved surface. Unlike mirrors that bounce light back, lenses let light **pass through** and bend it along the way.

### Convex Lens

![Convex lens (thicker in middle) converging parallel light rays and concave lens (thinner in middle) diverging them](convex-concave-lens-comparison.png)

A lens that bulges out — **thicker at the centre and slimmer at the edges** — is a **convex lens**. The magnifying glass your grandmother uses to read fine print on medicine packets is a convex lens.

Here is a simple experiment: place a single drop of water on a glass table top and look at the text beneath it. The drop acts as a tiny natural convex lens, making the letters appear bigger!

### Concave Lens

A lens that dips inward — **thinner at the centre and thicker at the edges** — is a **concave lens**. It is shaped a bit like two shallow bowls pressed together rim to rim from the outside.

---

## What Kind of Images Do Lenses Produce?

### Convex Lens Images

- **Object held close to the lens** — the image is **upright and magnified**
- **Object placed far from the lens** — the image **flips upside down**. Initially it appears enlarged, then it shrinks as you keep moving the object away

### Concave Lens Images

- The image is **always upright** and **always smaller** than the real object — no matter where you place it

---

## How Lenses Handle Parallel Light

- **Thin flat glass sheet** — light passes straight through without any bending
- **Convex lens** — **brings the beams together** (converges them). For this reason, a convex lens is also called a **converging lens**
- **Concave lens** — **spreads the beams apart** (diverges them). Hence it is also called a **diverging lens**

### Lighting Paper on Fire with a Convex Lens

Just as a concave mirror focuses reflected sunlight, a convex lens focuses sunlight that passes through it. Hold a magnifying glass in the sun and adjust a piece of paper beneath it until you see a tiny, brilliant dot of light — that is the focal point, and it can become hot enough to char the paper.

## Lenses All Around Us

Lenses are part of everyday objects we rarely think twice about:

- **Spectacles** — a convex lens corrects farsightedness, a concave lens corrects nearsightedness
- **Smartphone and DSLR cameras** — multiple tiny lenses work together to capture sharp photographs
- **Telescopes** — gather and focus light from stars and planets millions of kilometres away
- **Microscopes** — reveal bacteria, cells, and crystals invisible to the unaided eye
- **The human eye** — contains a remarkable convex lens that can change its own curvature, letting you read a book held at arm's length and then immediately look up at a distant hilltop

> **Key idea:** Bifocal spectacles have two lens powers built into one frame — the upper portion helps with distance vision and the lower portion assists with reading. You may have seen your grandparents tilt their head to look through the right section!

## Key Takeaways

- **Spherical mirrors** are curved reflecting surfaces cut from a hollow sphere — **concave** (curves inward) or **convex** (curves outward).
- A **concave mirror** can create magnified, reduced, upright, or inverted images depending on the object's position.
- A **convex mirror** always creates an upright, reduced image.
- **Laws of reflection:** The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection; the incident ray, the normal, and the reflected ray share the same plane.
- A **concave mirror converges** parallel light; a **convex mirror diverges** it.
- A **convex lens** (thick in the centre) converges light; a **concave lens** (thick at the edges) diverges light.
- A **convex lens** can produce magnified or reduced, upright or inverted images. A **concave lens** always produces an upright, reduced image.
- Lenses are at work in spectacles, cameras, telescopes, microscopes, and inside our own eyes.
