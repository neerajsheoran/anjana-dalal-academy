## Why Does the Moon Change Shape?

**Child:** Maa, last night the Moon was a full bright circle, but a week ago it was just a thin curve. Is the Moon actually shrinking and growing?

**Mother:** Not at all, beta. The Moon is always a solid sphere. It does not produce any light of its own — it simply reflects sunlight. At any given moment, the Sun lights up one entire half of the Moon. As the Moon travels around the Earth, we see different portions of that sunlit half. That is what creates the phases.

**Child:** So on Purnima we see the full sunlit face, and on Amavasya the sunlit face is turned completely away from us?

**Mother:** Exactly. And between those two extremes, you see crescents, half-moons, and gibbous shapes. The growing phase is called Shukla Paksha, and the shrinking phase is called Krishna Paksha.

**Child:** Some people say the phases happen because Earth's shadow falls on the Moon.

**Mother:** That is a very common misunderstanding. Earth's shadow on the Moon causes a lunar eclipse, and that is quite rare — it happens only a few times a year during specific alignments. The regular monthly phases are simply about our changing viewing angle as the Moon orbits us.

**Child:** You told me a waxing Moon is best seen at sunset and a waning Moon at sunrise. Why?

**Mother:** Think about where the Moon is relative to the Sun at each stage. A young waxing crescent sits just a bit east of the Sun, so after sunset it is still above the horizon for a short while. A waning crescent sits just west of the Sun, so it rises shortly before the Sun does. Ancient Indian astronomers like Varahamihira tracked these patterns so precisely that they could predict eclipses and plan farming seasons centuries ago.

## The Puzzle of Calendar-Making

**Child:** Maa, three things in the sky give us time — the spinning Earth gives us the day, the Moon's cycle gives us the month, and Earth's orbit gives us the year. But they do not fit together neatly, do they?

**Mother:** You have hit upon the biggest headache of every calendar-maker in history! Twelve lunar months add up to only 354 days — that is 11 days short of a solar year. A calendar that follows the Moon alone would drift away from the seasons over time. Imagine celebrating the harvest festival of Makar Sankranti in the middle of the monsoon!

**Child:** The Gregorian calendar fixes this by making months 30 or 31 days, with February at 28 or 29. It ignores the Moon entirely.

**Mother:** Correct — it is purely a solar calendar. Indian luni-solar calendars are cleverer in a way. They use the Moon for marking months but slip in an extra month — Adhika Maasa — every two to three years so that the calendar stays in step with the seasons. That is why Diwali, which falls on the Amavasya of Kartika, and Holi, which falls on the Purnima of Phalguna, land on different Gregorian dates each year.

**Child:** When was the Indian National Calendar introduced?

**Mother:** In 1957. India had dozens of regional calendars — Tamil, Bengali, Marathi, and many more — each with its own starting point and month lengths. The government adopted the Saka calendar, starting on 22 March (the day after the spring equinox), as a common reference. It kept the traditional month names but fixed the day counts so it stays in sync with the Gregorian calendar.

## Sky-Watching in Ancient India

**Child:** Maa, the idea of Uttarayan and Dakshinayan shows that ancient Indians carefully watched where the Sun rose each morning.

**Mother:** Yes, beta. They noticed that the sunrise point shifts slightly northward from December to June — that period is Uttarayan. Then it shifts southward from June to December — that is Dakshinayan. Makar Sankranti celebrates the transition from Dakshinayan to Uttarayan. Different parts of India know this festival by different names — Pongal in Tamil Nadu, Lohri in Punjab, Magh Bihu in Assam.

**Child:** Aryabhata said the Earth rotates on its axis — and he figured that out over 1,500 years ago!

**Mother:** Remarkable, is it not? He also calculated the length of a year as 365 days, 6 hours, 12 minutes, and 30 seconds. The modern accepted value is 365 days, 5 hours, 48 minutes, and 46 seconds. His estimate was astonishingly close, considering the instruments available in the 5th century.

**Child:** Did Indian astronomers predict eclipses too?

**Mother:** They did. Texts like the Surya Siddhanta contain detailed methods for calculating both solar and lunar eclipses. And in the 18th century, Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II built the Jantar Mantar observatories in Jaipur, Delhi, and other cities — massive stone instruments that tracked the positions of stars and planets with extraordinary precision. You can still visit them today.

## India's Eyes in Space

**Child:** Maa, ISRO has sent satellites to study the Moon, Mars, and even the Sun. That is amazing for a country that started its space programme with rockets launched from a fishing village in Kerala!

**Mother:** Thumba, near Thiruvananthapuram — that is where it all began in 1963. Today ISRO operates satellites for communication, weather tracking, navigation, and deep-space science. Mangalyaan reached Mars orbit on its very first attempt and cost about 450 crore rupees — less than the budget of many Hollywood films.

**Child:** Cartosat satellites take detailed photos of the Earth. Are those photos used only for making maps?

**Mother:** Maps are just the beginning. During the Kerala floods of 2018, satellite images helped rescue teams identify stranded villages and plan evacuation routes. INSAT weather satellites feed data to the India Meteorological Department for cyclone tracking and monsoon forecasts. Farmers, fishermen, and disaster managers all depend on these satellites daily.

**Child:** The chapter mentions space debris. Old rocket parts and dead satellites keep circling the Earth and could crash into working satellites.

**Mother:** There are over 30,000 tracked pieces of debris larger than 10 centimetres, racing around at about 28,000 kilometres per hour. At that speed, even a fleck of paint can puncture a satellite's hull. ISRO and agencies like NASA are developing methods to de-orbit old satellites safely and sweep up debris before it causes a chain reaction of collisions.

## Observing the Moon Yourself

**Child:** Maa, can I track the Moon's phases from our balcony?

**Mother:** Absolutely — you need nothing but your eyes and a notebook. Every evening or early morning, jot down the date, the time, and a quick sketch of the Moon's shape and position. Over a full month, you will see the crescent swell into a half-moon, then a gibbous, then a glorious full Moon — and then the whole sequence in reverse.

**Child:** The Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day, right? So if I see it at 6:30 PM tonight, tomorrow it will be around 7:20 PM.

**Mother:** That is right. And on Purnima night, the Moon rises roughly as the Sun sets and stays in the sky until sunrise. On Amavasya, the Moon travels across the sky alongside the Sun, lost in its glare, which is why we cannot see it.

**Child:** This is the same approach we discussed in the very first chapter — observe, record, and look for patterns. Science really does come full circle, Maa!

**Mother:** Beautifully put, beta. The sky is the oldest classroom humanity has ever had. Every civilisation learned from it, and you can join that tradition simply by stepping outside and looking up.
