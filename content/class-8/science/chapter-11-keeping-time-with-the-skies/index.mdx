## The Ever-Changing Face of the Moon

Step outside on a clear evening and look up. Some nights the Moon is a brilliant round disc; other nights it is a thin silver sliver; and on certain nights you cannot spot it at all. Many people assume the Moon only comes out after dark, but you can often catch it hanging in the daytime sky too!

### Why Can We See the Moon at All?

![Why we can see the Moon — sunlight reflected from one half](why-can-we-see-the-moon-at-all.png)

The Moon is a ball of rock — it generates no light of its own. What makes it glow is **reflected sunlight**. At every moment, the Sun lights up exactly one half of the Moon. The other half, turned away from the Sun, stays in darkness.

## The Cycle of Lunar Phases

As the Moon travels around the Earth on its month-long orbit, we observe different slices of its sunlit half. These shifting shapes of the bright portion are the **phases of the Moon**.

![Phases of the Moon from new Moon to full Moon and back, showing waxing crescent, half, gibbous, and waning stages](the-cycle-of-lunar-phases.png)

### The Shrinking Phase (Krishna Paksha)

Starting from **Purnima (full Moon)**, the visible bright area begins to reduce night after night:
1. **Full Moon** — the complete sunlit face is turned toward us
2. **Waning Gibbous** — a large portion is still visible, but slightly less than before
3. **Third-Quarter Half Moon** — precisely half the disc is bright (roughly one week after Purnima)
4. **Waning Crescent** — only a thin curved sliver remains
5. **Amavasya (new Moon)** — the sunlit half faces entirely away from Earth; the Moon seems to vanish

This fortnight of diminishing brightness is called **Krishna Paksha**.

### The Growing Phase (Shukla Paksha)

After Amavasya, the bright sliver reappears and expands:
1. **Waxing Crescent** — a delicate arc of light emerges
2. **First-Quarter Half Moon** — half the disc is illuminated
3. **Waxing Gibbous** — more than half is visible, growing nightly
4. **Full Moon** — the entire sunlit face is visible once again

This fortnight of increasing brightness is called **Shukla Paksha**.

> **Key idea:** One complete cycle — from Purnima to the next Purnima — takes roughly 29.5 days. The phases are NOT caused by Earth casting a shadow on the Moon (that would be a lunar eclipse, a rare event). Phases occur simply because our viewing angle of the sunlit half keeps changing as the Moon orbits Earth.

### Tips for Spotting the Moon

- A **shrinking (waning) Moon** is easiest to see around **sunrise**
- A **growing (waxing) Moon** is easiest to see around **sunset**
- On Purnima night, the Moon appears nearly **opposite** the Sun — rising as the Sun sets
- Each day, the Moon rises approximately **50 minutes later** than the previous day

## How Humans Invented Calendars

![How humans invented calendars — day, month, year from sky patterns](how-humans-invented-calendars.png)

Ancient civilisations noticed three repeating patterns in the sky and used them to measure time:

1. **The Day** — Earth spins on its axis, making the Sun appear to rise and set. One full spin — noon to the next noon — averages **24 hours**.

2. **The Month** — The Moon completes its full cycle of phases in about **29.5 days**. This natural rhythm became the basis of a month.

3. **The Year** — Earth takes roughly **365 and one-quarter days** to complete one loop around the Sun. Over this period, all four seasons come and go once.

### Calendars Based on the Moon (Lunar Calendars)

![Calendars based on the Moon — lunar calendars](calendars-based-on-the-moon-lunar-calendars.png)

A **lunar calendar** counts time using the Moon's phases. Twelve lunar months add up to about 354 days.

**The catch:** A lunar year is 11 days shorter than a solar year. Over time, seasons drift away from their calendar months — a summer festival could eventually fall in winter!

### Calendars Based on the Sun (Solar Calendars)

![Calendars based on the Sun — solar calendars](calendars-based-on-the-sun-solar-calendars.png)

A **solar calendar** is designed to keep seasons anchored to the same months every year. The **Gregorian calendar**, used around the globe today, is a solar calendar:
- Month lengths are adjusted to total **365 days** (some months have 30 days, some have 31, and February has 28)
- Every **four years**, an extra day is added to February (February 29) to account for the leftover quarter-day

### Calendars That Combine Both (Luni-Solar Calendars)

![Calendars that combine both — luni-solar calendars with Adhika Maasa](calendars-that-combine-both-luni-solar-calendars.png)

**Luni-solar calendars** track months by the Moon but periodically insert a bonus month — known as **Adhika Maasa** — every two to three years to stay aligned with the seasons. Most traditional Indian calendars work this way.

The months of the Indian luni-solar calendar are: Chaitra, Vaisakha, Jyeshtha, Ashadha, Shravana, Bhadrapada, Ashwin, Kartika, Margashirsha, Pausha, Magha, and Phalguna.

> **Key idea:** Indian celebrations like Diwali (Amavasya of Kartika), Holi (Purnima of Phalguna), and Eid-ul-Fitr (after sighting the new crescent Moon) are pinned to specific lunar phases. Because the lunar and Gregorian calendars do not align, these festivals shift dates in the Gregorian calendar every year.

## India's Official National Calendar

The **Indian National Calendar** (Saka calendar), officially adopted by the Government of India in 1957, is a solar calendar:
- It contains **365 days** in a regular year
- The year opens on **22 March** (the day following the spring equinox)
- Its months carry traditional Indian names (Chaitra, Vaisakha, and so on)
- Months are either **30 or 31 days** long
- Leap years follow the same rule as the Gregorian calendar

### Uttarayan and Dakshinayan

Careful sky-watchers in ancient India noticed that the Sun does not always rise at the exact same point on the horizon:
- During the warmer months, sunrise shifts a bit **northward** of due East
- During the cooler months, sunrise shifts a bit **southward** of due East
- The Sun's apparent march toward the north (roughly December to June) is called **Uttarayan**
- Its apparent journey southward (roughly June to December) is called **Dakshinayan**

## Artificial Satellites — Human-Made Moons

![Artificial satellites — human-made moons in orbit](artificial-satellites-human-made-moons.png)

Apart from the Moon, which is Earth's natural satellite, thousands of **artificial satellites** orbit our planet. Countries and agencies send them up for:
- **Communication** — television broadcasts, mobile phone signals, broadband internet
- **Navigation** — GPS for maps and ride-hailing apps
- **Weather forecasting** — tracking monsoons, cyclones, and rainfall patterns
- **Disaster response** — mapping flood-affected areas and guiding rescue teams
- **Scientific exploration** — studying stars, planets, and Earth itself

The **Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO)** operates a wide range of satellites:

![ISRO operates a wide range of satellites — Cartosat, AstroSat, Chandrayaan, Aditya L1, Mangalyaan](the-indian-space-research-organisation-isro-operates-a-wide-range-of-satellites.png)
- **Cartosat series** — photograph Earth's surface in fine detail for map-making and urban planning
- **AstroSat** — observes distant stars and galaxies across multiple wavelengths
- **Chandrayaan missions** — dedicated to exploring the Moon's geology and minerals
- **Aditya L1** — positioned to study the Sun's corona and solar storms
- **Mangalyaan** — India's celebrated Mars orbiter, which reached the red planet on its very first attempt

> **Key idea:** Old, non-functioning satellites and spent rocket stages continue to circle Earth as space debris (space junk). Travelling at roughly 28,000 km per hour, even a tiny fragment can damage an active satellite. Space agencies worldwide are developing plans to clean up this growing hazard.

## Key Takeaways

- The **phases of the Moon** arise because we see varying portions of the sunlit half as the Moon orbits Earth.
- A complete phase cycle lasts about **29.5 days**.
- The waning fortnight (decreasing brightness) is **Krishna Paksha**; the waxing fortnight (increasing brightness) is **Shukla Paksha**.
- **Lunar calendars** follow the Moon's cycle; **solar calendars** follow the cycle of seasons; **luni-solar calendars** blend both approaches.
- The **Gregorian calendar** is solar. Most traditional Indian calendars are luni-solar.
- The **Indian National Calendar** begins on 22 March and is a solar calendar.
- Indian festivals linked to Moon phases shift dates in the Gregorian calendar each year.
- **Artificial satellites** launched by ISRO and other organisations serve communication, navigation, weather forecasting, and scientific research.
