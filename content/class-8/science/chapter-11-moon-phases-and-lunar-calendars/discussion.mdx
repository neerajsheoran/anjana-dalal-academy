## Why Does the Moon Change Shape?

**Meera:** Arjun, last night the Moon was a full bright circle. A week ago it was a thin curve. Is the Moon actually shrinking and growing?

**Arjun:** No yaar, the Moon is always a solid sphere. It does not make light of its own — it just reflects sunlight. At any moment, the Sun lights up one whole half of the Moon.

**Meera:** So how do the shapes change then?

**Arjun:** As the Moon goes around the Earth, we see different slices of that sunlit half. On Purnima we see the full bright face. On Amavasya the sunlit side is turned completely away from us.

**Meera:** And in between we get crescents and half-moons and gibbous shapes. My Daadi calls the growing phase Shukla Paksha and the shrinking one Krishna Paksha.

**Arjun:** Yes. A lot of people think the phases happen because Earth's shadow falls on the Moon, but that is wrong. Earth's shadow on the Moon is a lunar eclipse — that's rare, only a few times a year.

**Meera:** I noticed something else. A waxing Moon is best seen at sunset, and a waning one at sunrise. Why is that?

**Arjun:** Think about where the Moon is relative to the Sun. A young waxing crescent sits a bit east of the Sun, so after sunset it stays above the horizon for a while. A waning crescent rises just before the Sun does. Varahamihira and other ancient astronomers tracked all this so precisely they could predict eclipses centuries in advance.

## The Puzzle of Calendar-Making

**Meera:** Yaar, three things in the sky give us time — Earth spinning gives us the day, the Moon's cycle gives us the month, Earth's orbit gives us the year. But they don't add up neatly, do they?

**Arjun:** That has been the calendar-maker's headache for thousands of years. Twelve lunar months come to only 354 days. That is 11 days short of a solar year.

**Meera:** So a pure lunar calendar would drift away from the seasons over time?

**Arjun:** Exactly. Imagine celebrating Makar Sankranti in the middle of monsoon! The Gregorian calendar fixes this by making months 30 or 31 days, February 28 or 29, ignoring the Moon entirely.

**Meera:** Our Indian luni-solar calendars are cleverer in a way. They mark months by the Moon but slip in an extra month — Adhika Maasa — every two or three years to stay in sync with the seasons.

**Arjun:** That is why Diwali and Holi fall on different Gregorian dates each year. Diwali is the Amavasya of Kartika, Holi is the Purnima of Phalguna. The Moon decides.

**Meera:** When did India adopt its National Calendar?

**Arjun:** 1957. We had dozens of regional calendars before — Tamil, Bengali, Marathi, all different. The government adopted the Saka calendar starting on 22 March, the day after the spring equinox, as a common reference. Same Indian month names but fixed day counts.

## Sky-Watching in Ancient India

**Meera:** Uttarayan and Dakshinayan — these terms always confused me. Daadi mentions them every January.

**Arjun:** Ancient Indians watched where the Sun rose each morning. The sunrise point shifts slightly northward from December to June — that is Uttarayan. Then it shifts southward from June to December — Dakshinayan.

**Meera:** And Makar Sankranti marks the switch from Dakshinayan to Uttarayan?

**Arjun:** Yes. Different states celebrate it differently — Pongal in Tamil Nadu, Lohri in Punjab, Magh Bihu in Assam. Same astronomical moment, different cultural names.

**Meera:** Aryabhata said the Earth rotates on its axis — over 1,500 years ago. How did he even figure that out without telescopes?

**Arjun:** Just patient observation, year after year. He also calculated the length of a year as 365 days, 6 hours, 12 minutes, 30 seconds. The modern accepted value is 365 days, 5 hours, 48 minutes, 46 seconds. He was off by less than half an hour!

**Meera:** Did Indian astronomers predict eclipses too?

**Arjun:** The Surya Siddhanta — the text Aryabhata and others built on — has detailed methods for calculating both solar and lunar eclipses. And in the 1700s, Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II built Jantar Mantar at Jaipur, Delhi and a few other cities. Those huge stone instruments tracked stars and planets with crazy precision. You can still visit them.

## India's Eyes in Space

**Meera:** Yaar, ISRO has now sent missions to the Moon, Mars, and even the Sun. That is amazing for a country that started its space programme with rockets launched from a fishing village.

**Arjun:** Thumba, near Thiruvananthapuram, in 1963. Today ISRO runs satellites for communication, weather, navigation, deep-space science. Chandrayaan-3 landed near the south pole of the Moon — no country had done that before.

**Meera:** And Mangalyaan reached Mars orbit on the very first attempt. My cousin said it cost less than a Hollywood film.

**Arjun:** About 450 crore rupees, yes. Aditya-L1 is now studying the Sun's corona. And Cartosat satellites take detailed photos of Earth.

**Meera:** Just for making maps?

**Arjun:** That is the obvious use. But during the Kerala floods of 2018, those same satellite images helped rescue teams find stranded villages and plan evacuation routes. INSAT weather satellites feed data to IMD for cyclone tracking and monsoon forecasts. Farmers, fishermen, disaster managers — all rely on this every single day.

**Meera:** The chapter mentions space debris. Old rocket parts and dead satellites circling Earth and possibly crashing into working ones.

**Arjun:** Over 30,000 tracked pieces larger than 10 cm, racing around at 28,000 km per hour. At that speed even a fleck of paint can puncture a satellite. ISRO, NASA, all the space agencies are working on ways to de-orbit old satellites safely before debris causes a chain reaction.

## Observing the Moon Yourself

**Meera:** Arjun, can I actually track the Moon's phases from my balcony? With no equipment?

**Arjun:** Yaar, that is exactly what humans have done for thousands of years before telescopes. Just eyes and a notebook. Every evening or early morning, write down the date, the time, a quick sketch of the Moon's shape and where it is in the sky.

**Meera:** And over a month I will see the crescent grow into a half-moon, then gibbous, then full, then shrink back.

**Arjun:** The Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day. So if you see it at 6:30 PM tonight, tomorrow it will be around 7:20 PM.

**Meera:** On Purnima night, the Moon basically rises as the Sun sets and stays up the whole night?

**Arjun:** Yes. And on Amavasya the Moon travels across the sky alongside the Sun, lost in its glare, so we cannot see it. Try sketching one Moon a night for a month — it is the same observation Aryabhata and Varahamihira were doing.

**Meera:** This is the same idea as our very first chapter — observe, record, look for patterns. Science really does come full circle, doesn't it?

**Arjun:** The sky is the oldest classroom humanity has had. Every civilisation learned from it. We can just step out on the balcony and join that tradition.
