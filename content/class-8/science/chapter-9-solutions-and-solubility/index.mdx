## Solute, Solvent, and Solution -- The Basics

![Solute, solvent, and solution — the basics](solute-solvent-and-solution-the-basics.png)

Think about a glass of **lassi** from a roadside stall in Amritsar. Every sip tastes equally creamy and salty. It is not thick in one spot and watery in another. This evenness is the hallmark of a **solution** -- a mixture where the components are blended so thoroughly that every part is identical.

When a solid is stirred into a liquid to form a solution:
- The **solid** that dissolves is called the **solute**
- The **liquid** that does the dissolving is called the **solvent**

When two liquids are mixed together, the one present in a **greater amount** is the solvent, and the one in a **lesser amount** is the solute.

> **Key idea:** Solutions are not limited to solids in liquids! Air is a **gaseous solution**. Nitrogen, being the most abundant gas, acts as the solvent. Oxygen, carbon dioxide, argon, and other trace gases are the solutes.

### A Curious Kitchen Example

When making **jalebi**, the sugar syrup (chasni) is boiled until it becomes extremely thick. There is actually more sugar than water in this syrup! Yet water is still considered the solvent because it is the liquid medium in which the sugar dissolves. The label of solute and solvent is about **roles**, not just quantities.

---

## How Much Can a Solvent Hold?

Imagine adding spoon after spoon of sugar to a cup of chai. The first few spoons dissolve perfectly. But after a point, no matter how hard you stir, the sugar just sits at the bottom, refusing to dissolve. The chai has reached its **limit**.

### Saturated and Unsaturated Solutions

![Saturated and unsaturated solutions](saturated-and-unsaturated-solutions.png)

- An **unsaturated solution** still has room for more solute -- it has not reached its dissolving capacity at that temperature
- A **saturated solution** has absorbed the **maximum possible amount** of solute at a given temperature -- adding more will simply leave undissolved solute at the bottom

### Concentration

![Concentration — dilute versus concentrated solutions](concentration.png)

**Concentration** describes how much solute is present in a given amount of solution.

- A **dilute solution** has relatively little solute (light chai with barely any sugar)
- A **concentrated solution** has a lot of solute (syrupy chai loaded with sugar)

These terms are relative: 3 spoons of sugar in 200 mL of water might be "concentrated" for tea but "dilute" for making gulab jamun syrup.

### Solubility

**Solubility** is the **maximum quantity of solute** that can dissolve in a fixed amount of solvent at a specific temperature. It puts an exact number on how much a solvent can handle.

## Temperature and Solubility

### Solids Dissolving in Liquids

![Solids dissolving in liquids — solubility rises with temperature](solids-dissolving-in-liquids.png)

For most solid solutes, **solubility goes up when the temperature rises**. Try dissolving baking soda (meetha soda) in water:
- At 25 degrees C, only a limited amount dissolves before the solution becomes saturated
- Warm the water to 50 degrees C and stir -- more baking soda dissolves
- Heat it further to 75 degrees C -- even more dissolves

A solution that was saturated at a cooler temperature can become **unsaturated** when heated, because the warmer solvent can now accommodate extra solute particles.

### Gases Dissolving in Liquids

![Gases dissolving in liquids — solubility drops with temperature](gases-dissolving-in-liquids.png)

For gases, the rule flips: **gas solubility generally drops as temperature climbs**.

- **Cold water** holds more dissolved oxygen than warm water -- fish and other aquatic creatures depend on this dissolved oxygen to breathe
- When lakes or rivers warm up in summer, the dissolved oxygen falls, and aquatic life can struggle

> **Key idea:** Cold water is oxygen-rich, which is why rivers fed by mountain glaciers tend to support thriving fish populations. Warmer stagnant water holds less oxygen and is harder for aquatic organisms to survive in.

## Why Do Some Things Float and Others Sink?

![Why some things float and others sink — relative density to the liquid](why-do-some-things-float-and-others-sink.png)

When you wash rice before cooking, you notice that the lightweight husk and dry chaff float to the surface, while the heavier rice grains settle at the bottom. If you pour mustard oil into a bowl of water, the oil sits on top.

The reason is straightforward: objects that are **lighter (less dense)** than a liquid float on it, and objects that are **heavier (denser)** than the liquid sink.

## Understanding Density

Picture the crowd at a Durga Puja pandal in Kolkata on Ashtami night. Thousands of people packed tightly into a small area -- that is high density. The same pandal at 6 AM the next morning, with only a handful of workers cleaning up -- that is low density. Density tells us how much **stuff** (mass) is packed into a given **space** (volume).

**Density = Mass / Volume**

![Comparison of equal volumes of three materials with different densities, showing the heavier material sinks and lighter one floats in water](understanding-density.png)

### Units of Density

- **SI unit:** kilogram per cubic metre (kg/m3)
- Commonly used in labs: **g/mL** or **g/cm3**

### Essential Facts About Density

- Density is a property of the **material**, not the object -- a tiny ball of copper and a large copper sheet have the same density
- Density changes with **temperature** and **pressure**
- At room temperature, 1 mL of water has a mass of approximately 1 g (so water's density is roughly 1 g/mL)

### Relative Density

**Relative density** compares a substance's density to the density of water. It is a simple ratio with **no units**.

If copper has a density of 8.9 g/cm3, its relative density is 8.9 -- meaning copper is 8.9 times denser than water. Any substance with a relative density greater than 1 sinks in water; any substance with relative density less than 1 floats.

## Measuring Mass and Volume

### How to Measure Mass

Use a **digital balance**:
1. Turn it on and wait for the display to show zero
2. Place an empty watch glass or container on the pan
3. Press the **tare** button to reset the reading to zero (this subtracts the container's weight)
4. Carefully place your sample on the watch glass
5. Read the mass from the display

### How to Measure the Volume of a Liquid

Pour the liquid into a **measuring cylinder** -- a tall, narrow, transparent container marked with volume gradations.

**Tips for accurate reading:**
- The liquid surface curves inside the cylinder, forming a curved shape called the **meniscus**
- For **colourless liquids** (like water), read the volume at the **lowest point** of the meniscus
- For **coloured liquids** (like coloured ink), read at the **highest point** of the meniscus
- Position your eyes at the **same horizontal level** as the liquid surface to avoid a reading error called parallax

### Volume of Regularly Shaped Solids

If the object has a regular geometric shape (like a rectangular box):

**Volume = Length x Breadth x Height**

### Volume of Irregularly Shaped Solids

For objects with odd shapes (like a pebble or a small idol), use the **water displacement method**:
1. Pour water into a measuring cylinder and note the level (say, 40 mL)
2. Gently slide the object into the water
3. Note the new water level (say, 47 mL)
4. Volume of the object = 47 - 40 = **7 cm3**

## How Temperature Affects Density

![How temperature affects density — heating decreases density](how-temperature-affects-density.png)

As a general rule, **heating a substance decreases its density** and **cooling increases its density**.

Here is why:
- Heating makes particles move apart -- the substance **expands** (volume increases)
- Mass stays the same, but volume is now larger
- Since Density = Mass / Volume, density drops

This explains:
- Why **hot air rises** -- it is less dense than the cooler air around it
- How **hot air balloons** work -- heating the air inside the balloon makes it lighter than the surrounding air, generating an upward force

### The Remarkable Exception: Ice Floating on Water

![The remarkable exception — ice floating on water](the-remarkable-exception-ice-floating-on-water.png)

Water behaves unusually near its freezing point. Its density is **highest at 4 degrees C**. As water cools below 4 degrees C and eventually freezes at 0 degrees C, the molecules lock into a crystal pattern that actually takes up **more space**. This means ice is **less dense** than liquid water -- which is why ice floats.

This quirk of nature is vital for life in cold climates: a layer of ice forms on top of ponds and lakes, insulating the liquid water below and keeping it at about 4 degrees C -- warm enough for fish and other creatures to survive through the winter.

## How Pressure Affects Density

![How pressure affects density — gases, liquids, solids](how-pressure-affects-density.png)

- **Gases:** Squeezing a gas (increasing pressure) forces its particles closer, shrinks the volume, and **raises the density** significantly
- **Liquids:** Pressure has only a tiny effect -- liquids are already packed fairly tightly
- **Solids:** Pressure has almost no measurable effect -- particles are already as close as they can get

## Layers of the Earth

![Layers of the Earth — crust, mantle, outer core, inner core](layers-of-the-earth.png)

Our planet itself is a study in density. The Earth is layered, with each layer denser than the one above:

- **Crust** -- the outermost, thinnest, and least dense layer (the ground beneath your feet)
- **Upper mantle** -- denser rock beneath the crust
- **Lower mantle** -- even denser, under immense pressure
- **Outer core** -- a liquid layer of molten iron and nickel
- **Inner core** -- the densest part of the Earth, solid iron and nickel under extreme pressure and temperature

When Earth was young and molten, heavier materials sank towards the centre and lighter materials rose to the surface -- a natural sorting process driven entirely by density.

## Key Takeaways

- A **solution** is a uniform mixture. The substance that dissolves is the **solute**; the substance doing the dissolving is the **solvent**.
- A **saturated solution** holds the maximum possible solute at a given temperature. An **unsaturated solution** can still dissolve more.
- **Solubility** is the maximum amount of solute that dissolves in a fixed quantity of solvent at a particular temperature.
- For most solid solutes, solubility **rises** with temperature. For gases in liquids, solubility **falls** with temperature.
- **Density = Mass / Volume**. Its SI unit is kg/m3.
- Density depends on the material, not on the size or shape of the object. It varies with temperature and pressure.
- Objects **float** when they are less dense than the surrounding liquid and **sink** when they are denser.
- Density generally **decreases** with rising temperature. Ice floating on water is a notable exception -- water is densest at 4 degrees C, and ice at 0 degrees C is less dense than liquid water.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**1. Thermal pollution and dying fish.** Walk along the Ganga near an industrial town like Kanpur, or the Yamuna near Delhi, and on some days you will see dead fish floating. One reason is **thermal pollution** — factories dumping hot water into the river. Cold water can hold much more dissolved oxygen than warm water. As the river heats up, the dissolved oxygen drops, and fish — which breathe through their gills using that oxygen — literally suffocate. The solubility rule you learned this week (gases dissolve less in warmer liquids) directly affects whether crores of rupees worth of riverine ecosystems survive.

**2. Why nimbu paani evaporates if you forget about it.** Pour a glass of nimbu paani and leave it on the kitchen counter on a summer afternoon in Ahmedabad or Chennai. Come back in a few hours — the level has dropped. The water (solvent) is **evaporating**, but the sugar, salt, and lemon juice (solutes) are not. So the solution slowly becomes more **concentrated**. Take it further and you would eventually reach the **saturation** point, where no more water can leave without the salt or sugar starting to crystallise out. This is exactly how Kutch's salt farmers in Gujarat make their famous salt — letting sea water evaporate in shallow flat pans under the sun until the dissolved salt is forced to crystallise.

**3. The gold purity test at the jeweller's.** When your family takes a gold chain to be valued at the local jeweller, they often use a small set of weights and a measuring cylinder of water. The jeweller weighs the chain, then drops it into water to measure displaced volume — and calculates the **density**. Pure gold has a relative density of 19.3. Anything mixed in with lighter metals like copper or silver brings that number down. Density has been used to check gold purity for centuries — Archimedes is said to have invented this method to catch a dishonest goldsmith in ancient Greece.

### Try it at home: Make a Saturated Salt Solution and Watch Salt Reappear

*⏱ 30 minutes active, then leave overnight · 🧹 Low mess · 👨‍👩‍👧 Need an adult only when handling warm water*

**You will need:** A clear glass jar or tall glass, table salt (about 10-15 teaspoons), a teaspoon, a small saucepan, warm-to-hot water (NOT boiling), a clean spoon for stirring, a cotton string or thread, a pencil, a saucer.

**What to do:**

1. Ask an adult to heat about a cup (200 mL) of water in the saucepan until it is warm but safe to touch. Pour it into your jar.
2. Add one teaspoon of salt and stir until it dissolves completely.
3. Add another teaspoon. Stir again. Keep adding salt, one teaspoon at a time, stirring after each.
4. Eventually you will reach a point where no matter how much you stir, the salt does not dissolve and sits at the bottom. **You have just made a saturated solution.** Note how many teaspoons of salt you added.
5. Pour the clear top layer (without the undissolved salt) carefully into a fresh glass.
6. Tie one end of your cotton string to the middle of the pencil. Lay the pencil across the top of the glass so the string hangs down into the salt solution, but does not touch the bottom.
7. Place the glass somewhere undisturbed. Cover loosely with a saucer to keep dust out. **Leave it for 24-48 hours.**

**What to notice:**
- As the water slowly **cools**, salt's solubility drops. The solution becomes **supersaturated** — holding more salt than it should at the cooler temperature.
- As water **evaporates** over a day or two, the salt has even less water to dissolve in.
- Tiny salt **crystals begin to form on the string and walls of the glass** — neat cubic shapes if you are lucky.

**Show what you found:** Photograph the crystals on your string (use macro mode if your phone has it) or sketch them. Show your daadi, dadu, or mummy. Ask if they remember the **Kutch salt farms** or have seen salt pans on the way to the Gujarat or Tamil Nadu coast — and explain that the same process you just did in your kitchen is happening on a giant scale in those salt fields. Tell them one new thing you understood about saturation and solubility.
