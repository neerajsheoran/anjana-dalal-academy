## Can Electricity Create Magnetism?

You are familiar with magnets attracting iron pins and paper clips. But here is a surprising connection -- **electricity can produce magnetism**! When electric current passes through a wire, the wire begins to behave like a magnet, capable of deflecting a compass needle nearby.

### Oersted's Accidental Breakthrough

![Oersted's accidental breakthrough — compass needle deflecting near a current-carrying wire](oersteds-accidental-breakthrough-1.png)

![Oersted's accidental breakthrough — second view of the setup](oersteds-accidental-breakthrough-2.png)

Place a magnetic compass directly below a straight wire connected to a battery. The moment you close the circuit and current starts flowing, the compass needle swings away from its usual north-south position. Open the circuit and the needle snaps back.

This reveals that a **wire carrying electric current generates a magnetic field** around itself. The moment the current stops, the magnetic effect vanishes.

> **Key idea:** A conductor carrying electric current creates a magnetic field in the space surrounding it. This phenomenon is called the **magnetic effect of electric current**.

### The Scientist Who Made the Connection

![Hans Christian Oersted — the scientist who connected electricity and magnetism](the-scientist-who-made-the-connection.png)

Danish professor **Hans Christian Oersted** (1777-1851) stumbled upon this discovery in 1820 during a lecture demonstration. He noticed that every time he switched his electrical circuit on or off, a compass lying on the table twitched. Rather than ignoring this oddity, he investigated it systematically and published his findings. Oersted's discovery revealed that electricity and magnetism -- two forces everyone had assumed were completely separate -- were in fact deeply related, opening the door to the field of **electromagnetism**.

## Electromagnets: Magnetism You Can Control

![Electromagnets — magnetism you can control](electromagnets-magnetism-you-can-control.png)

An **electromagnet** is created by winding a wire into a coil and passing electric current through it. The coil behaves like a magnet while the current flows. Switch the current off, and the magnetism disappears entirely.

### Building a Simple Electromagnet

1. Take a long iron nail and about 50 cm of thin, insulated copper wire
2. Wind the wire tightly around the nail, creating many loops (a coil)
3. Connect both free ends of the wire to an electric cell (battery)
4. Bring the nail near some small iron stapler pins -- they will cling to the nail!
5. Disconnect the wire -- the pins drop off immediately

![Electromagnet made from an iron nail wrapped with insulated wire connected to a cell, attracting paper clips](building-a-simple-electromagnet.png)

The coil alone produces a magnetic field when current flows through it. Placing an **iron core** (like the nail) inside the coil dramatically amplifies the magnetic strength.

### What Makes Electromagnets Special

Like ordinary bar magnets, electromagnets possess **two poles** -- North and South. However, they have abilities that permanent magnets lack:

**You can adjust the strength by:**

![Adjusting electromagnet strength — current and number of turns](what-makes-electromagnets-special-you-can-adjust-the-strength-by.png)

- Increasing the **electric current** (adding more cells to the circuit)
- Increasing the **number of wire turns** in the coil
- Doing both together for maximum effect

**You can flip the poles** simply by reversing the direction of current flow.

![Flipping electromagnet poles by reversing current direction](what-makes-electromagnets-special-you-can-flip-the-poles.png)

> **Key idea:** Unlike fixed permanent magnets, electromagnets can be switched on and off, strengthened or weakened, and have their poles reversed at will. This controllability is what makes them indispensable in modern technology.

### Heavy-Duty Electromagnets

![Heavy-duty lifting electromagnets in steel factories and scrapyards](heavy-duty-electromagnets.png)

In steel factories and vehicle scrapyards, enormous **lifting electromagnets** are suspended from cranes:

- Current ON: The powerful magnetic field grabs and hoists tonnes of iron and steel
- Current OFF: The field disappears and the metal load drops exactly where needed

This simple on-off control makes sorting and moving heavy metal objects safe and efficient.

### Where Electromagnets Are Used

![Where electromagnets are used — bells, motors, speakers, locks](where-electromagnets-are-used.png)

The magnetic effect of current powers a wide variety of devices in daily life:
- **Electric bells** (the hammer strikes repeatedly because the electromagnet switches rapidly)
- **Motors** in ceiling fans, mixer-grinders, washing machines, and electric scooters
- **Loudspeakers** and headphones (electromagnetic vibrations produce sound)
- **Magnetic door locks** and security systems

## Earth: A Planet-Sized Magnet

![Earth as a planet-sized magnet — magnetic field from molten iron core](earth-a-planet-sized-magnet.png)

Deep beneath the surface, Earth's outer core consists of liquid iron in constant motion. This flowing metallic liquid generates electric currents, which in turn produce a vast magnetic field -- making our **planet behave like a colossal magnet**.

This natural magnetic field serves critical purposes:

![Earth's magnetic field serving critical purposes — navigation and protection from solar wind](earth-a-planet-sized-magnet-this-natural-magnetic-field-serves-critical-purposes.png)

- **Navigation** -- Migratory birds like the Arctic tern and Siberian crane, sea turtles, and salmon use Earth's magnetic field to navigate enormous distances. Compass needles, too, align with this field.
- **Protection** -- The magnetic field deflects a stream of dangerous charged particles constantly blasted towards Earth by the Sun (the solar wind). Without this magnetic shield, these particles would strip away our atmosphere and expose all life to lethal radiation.

## Why Does a Current-Carrying Wire Get Hot?

![Why a current-carrying wire gets hot — resistance and energy conversion](why-does-a-current-carrying-wire-get-hot.png)

When electric current flows through any conductor, the moving charges encounter opposition from the material of the wire. This opposition is called **resistance**. As charges push through against this resistance, some electrical energy transforms into **heat energy**, warming the wire.

### The Heating Effect of Electric Current

![The heating effect of electric current](the-heating-effect-of-electric-current.png)

This warming of a conductor by current flow is called the **heating effect of electric current**.

Not all materials resist current equally:
- **Nichrome** (an alloy of nickel and chromium) has much higher resistance than copper for the same length and thickness
- Higher resistance means more electrical energy converts to heat

**The amount of heat produced depends on:**

![Factors the amount of heat produced depends on](the-amount-of-heat-produced-depends-on.png)

- The **material** of the wire (high-resistance materials generate more heat)
- The **thickness** of the wire (thinner wires produce more heat)
- The **length** of the wire (longer wires generate more heat)
- The **strength of current** flowing (more current means more heat)
- The **duration** for which current flows (longer time means more total heat)

> **Key idea:** Increasing the current through a wire increases the heat generated. This is why appliances that draw heavy current require specially rated wires and plugs.

### Appliances That Use the Heating Effect

![Appliances that use the heating effect — heaters, irons, kettles, bulbs](appliances-that-use-the-heating-effect.png)

Many devices in Indian homes deliberately exploit this effect:

- **Electric room heaters** -- A coiled wire glows red-hot, warming the room on cold winter nights
- **Electric stoves and induction cooktops** -- Heating elements cook food
- **Electric kettles** -- Heat water for chai or soup
- **Clothes irons (istri)** -- A hot metal plate presses wrinkles out of fabric
- **Immersion rods** -- A portable coil heats water directly in a bucket or vessel
- **Hair dryers** -- A heating element warms air, which is blown over wet hair
- **Incandescent bulbs** -- A thin tungsten filament gets so hot that it glows white, producing light

All these devices contain a **heating element** -- a coil or strip made from a high-resistance material like nichrome.

### Safety Matters

![Safety matters — electrical hazards from overheating](safety-matters.png)

While the heating effect is useful, it can become dangerous if not managed:
- Wires carrying too much current can overheat, melting their insulation
- **Overheated plugs and sockets** can melt or catch fire
- Electrical fires in homes are often caused by faulty wiring or overloaded circuits

To stay safe, always use electrical components (wires, plugs, extension boards) that are rated for the correct current, and never overload a single socket with too many appliances.

## How Does a Battery Produce Electricity?

Electric cells and batteries are portable energy sources. But what process inside them generates current?

### The Voltaic Cell

![The Voltaic cell — electrodes, electrolyte, container](the-voltaic-cell.png)

The **Voltaic cell** (also known as a Galvanic cell) is among the oldest designs of electric cells. It consists of:

1. **Two metal plates** (called electrodes) made from different metals
2. A **liquid electrolyte** -- typically a dilute acid or salt solution
3. A **container** made of glass or plastic

**How it generates current:**
- A chemical reaction takes place between the metal plates and the electrolyte
- This reaction pushes electric charges through the external circuit, creating current
- When the circuit is connected, current flows from the positive terminal, through the device, and back to the negative terminal
- Gradually, the chemicals are consumed, and the cell "dies"

### The Rivalry That Sparked the Battery

In the 1790s, Italian anatomist **Luigi Galvani** observed that a dead frog's leg twitched when touched simultaneously with two different metals -- copper and iron. Galvani concluded that the frog's body contained a form of "animal electricity."

Fellow Italian **Alessandro Volta** disagreed. He believed the electricity originated from the metals, not the frog. To prove his point, Volta replaced the frog's leg with cloth soaked in saltwater and connected two different metals to it. Current flowed -- without any animal tissue involved! This elegant experiment established that the combination of dissimilar metals and a conducting liquid generates electricity, leading Volta to build the world's first practical battery.

### A Kitchen Experiment: The Lemon Battery

![A kitchen experiment — the lemon battery](a-kitchen-experiment-the-lemon-battery.png)

You can replicate Volta's principle at home:

1. Gather five or six ripe, juicy lemons (nimbu)
2. Push a copper coin or wire and a zinc-coated iron nail into each lemon, keeping them apart
3. Using short wires, connect the copper piece of one lemon to the iron nail of the next
4. Attach an LED between the copper of the first lemon and the nail of the last lemon
5. If the LED lights up, you have built a working battery!

Here, the **copper and zinc** serve as electrodes, and the **acidic lemon juice** acts as the electrolyte.

## Dry Cells: Power in Your Pocket

Voltaic cells with sloshing liquids are impractical for everyday use. The **dry cell** solves this by replacing the liquid electrolyte with a **thick, moist paste** that will not spill.

![Cross-section of a dry cell showing zinc container, carbon rod, paste electrolyte, and terminal labels](dry-cells-power-in-your-pocket.png)

### Inside a Dry Cell

- **Zinc casing** -- forms the outer container and serves as the negative terminal
- **Carbon rod** in the centre (topped with a metal cap) -- acts as the positive terminal
- **Paste electrolyte** -- a moist chemical mixture packed around the carbon rod

Dry cells are **single-use**: once the chemical paste is exhausted, the cell must be discarded responsibly.

## Rechargeable Batteries: Use, Recharge, Repeat

![Rechargeable batteries — use, recharge, repeat](rechargeable-batteries-use-recharge-repeat.png)

**Rechargeable batteries** contain chemicals whose reactions can be reversed by feeding electricity back into the cell. This allows them to be charged and used hundreds of times.

### Common Uses of Rechargeable Batteries

- **Wristwatches and wireless earphones** -- tiny cells
- **Smartphones and laptops** -- compact lithium-ion batteries
- **Home inverters** -- large lead-acid batteries for power backup
- **Electric rickshaws and cars** -- high-capacity battery packs

Even rechargeable batteries wear out eventually. After hundreds of charge-discharge cycles, they hold progressively less energy -- which is why an old phone needs charging far more frequently than a new one.

### What Lies Ahead in Battery Technology

![What lies ahead in battery technology — Li-ion and solid-state](what-lies-ahead-in-battery-technology.png)

- **Lithium-ion (Li-ion) batteries** dominate today, powering everything from phones to electric buses
- Researchers worldwide are developing **solid-state batteries**, which replace the liquid or paste electrolyte with a solid material
- Solid-state batteries promise to be safer (less fire risk), faster-charging, and longer-lasting
- Advances in battery technology are vital for India's push towards electric vehicles and solar energy storage

> **Key idea:** Used batteries must never be tossed into household garbage. They contain toxic substances -- acids, lead, cadmium, lithium -- that can seep into soil and groundwater, poisoning ecosystems. Always hand them over to designated e-waste collection centres for safe recycling.

## Key Takeaways

- A conductor carrying electric current generates a **magnetic field** around it -- this is the **magnetic effect of electric current**.
- A coil of wire carrying current behaves as a magnet and is called an **electromagnet**. Inserting an iron core strengthens it greatly.
- Electromagnet strength depends on the **number of wire turns** and the **current strength**. Its poles can be reversed by changing current direction.
- Current flowing through a conductor produces heat -- this is the **heating effect of electric current**.
- The heat generated depends on the wire's material, thickness, length, the current flowing, and the duration.
- A **cell or battery** converts chemical energy into electrical energy through internal chemical reactions.
- **Dry cells** use a paste electrolyte and are meant for single use.
- **Rechargeable batteries** can be charged and reused many times but degrade gradually over their lifespan.
