## Everything Is Connected

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says monkeys raid farms when their forests are cut down. So deforestation does not just remove trees — it pushes animals into villages too?

**Mother:** That is exactly right, beta. A habitat has biotic parts — the living creatures — and abiotic parts like sunlight, water, and soil. When we destroy forests, animals lose their food and shelter. Monkeys, elephants, even leopards wander into human areas because they have nowhere else to go. It is a chain reaction that starts with one act — cutting trees — and ends with human-wildlife conflict.

**Child:** So an ecosystem is the whole package — all the living things plus the non-living environment they depend on?

**Mother:** Yes. A pond, a mango orchard, even a single peepal tree buzzing with birds and insects — each is an ecosystem. India's ecosystems are incredibly varied. The Western Ghats rainforest, the Thar Desert, the coral reefs around Lakshadweep, the alpine meadows of Ladakh — every one of them has its own unique web of life.

**Child:** The frog-mosquito-butterfly example in the chapter surprised me. Frogs eating mosquito larvae actually helps butterflies, which helps farmers grow better vegetables!

**Mother:** Those are called indirect effects. Remove one species and unexpected consequences cascade through the ecosystem. A forest ranger once told me that when a village pond was cleaned of frogs, mosquito populations exploded within weeks. Sometimes we do not appreciate a creature's role until it is gone.

## The Flow of Energy Through Food

**Child:** Maa, a food chain starts with producers — green plants — who make their own food using sunlight. Then herbivores eat the plants, carnivores eat the herbivores, and so on. Each step is a trophic level.

**Mother:** And at every step, some energy is used up by the organism for its own living — moving, breathing, growing. So less energy is available at each higher level. That is why there are always more plants than deer, and more deer than tigers. The pyramid of energy is a beautiful pattern in nature.

**Child:** But real life is messier than a single chain, right? A mouse might be eaten by a snake, an owl, or a cat. When you connect all these overlapping chains, you get a food web.

**Mother:** Exactly. Think of our own kitchen garden. The spinach is eaten by caterpillars, which are eaten by sparrows, which might be caught by the neighbour's cat. But the same caterpillars are also eaten by garden lizards. And sparrows also eat grain. All these connections create a web — messy but resilient. If one link weakens, other paths can still carry the energy forward.

**Child:** Decomposers close the loop, do they not? Bacteria and fungi break down dead leaves and animals and put the nutrients back into the soil for plants to use again.

**Mother:** Yes, beta. When Nani makes compost in the backyard from vegetable peels, tea leaves, and dry leaves, she is putting decomposers to work. The dark, crumbly compost that results is rich in nutrients — nature's own fertiliser. Without decomposers, dead material would just pile up and the soil would become barren.

## The Many Ways Organisms Relate

**Child:** Maa, beyond eating each other, organisms interact in other ways — mutualism, commensalism, parasitism, and competition. Can you give me examples from around our home?

**Mother:** Of course. Mutualism — where both partners benefit — happens right outside our window. The sunflowers in our balcony pots attract bees. The bees drink nectar for food, and while doing so, they carry pollen from one flower to another, helping the plant reproduce. Both gain something.

**Child:** Commensalism is where one benefits and the other does not care either way — like the egrets that follow Ramu kaka's buffaloes in the field?

**Mother:** Perfect example. The buffaloes stir up insects as they walk through the grass, making it easy for the egrets to catch a meal. The buffaloes are not helped or harmed — they just carry on chewing cud.

**Child:** Parasitism is the unfair one — one gains and the other suffers. Like the amarbel vine that wraps around the hedge and sucks out its water and nutrients.

**Mother:** Yes, cuscuta — amarbel — is a classic parasite. It has no green leaves of its own, so it cannot make food by photosynthesis. It depends entirely on its host plant. Over time, the host weakens and may even die. Malaria is another example of parasitism — the Plasmodium parasite lives inside mosquitoes and human blood cells, harming the human host.

**Child:** And competition? That happens when two species want the same thing — food, water, space?

**Mother:** Right. In our colony park, mynas and sparrows both compete for the same insects and grain. Competition keeps populations from growing beyond what the habitat can support. It is nature's way of maintaining balance.

## When Balance Breaks Down

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says ecosystem balance is dynamic — always shifting, never perfectly still. What does that really mean?

**Mother:** Think of the nilgai (blue bull) population near Dada's farm in Rajasthan. When rains are good, grass grows tall, and nilgai numbers increase because food is plentiful. But more nilgai attract more predators and also eat down the grass faster. Eventually food becomes scarce and the population drops. Then the grass recovers, and the cycle begins again. That constant rising and falling is dynamic balance.

**Child:** Problems start when humans push the system beyond what it can recover from?

**Mother:** Exactly. The frog leg export story from the 1980s is a perfect case. India exported millions of frogs. Without frogs eating insects, crop pests multiplied. Farmers poured on chemical pesticides, which poisoned the soil and water. One broken link — the removal of frogs — set off a chain of damage that took years to recognise. The government finally banned frog exports to let the ecosystem recover.

**Child:** The Sundarbans protect the coast from cyclones, but they are being damaged by logging and pollution. If the mangroves go, coastal villages lose their shield.

**Mother:** The Sundarbans became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987 precisely because of their ecological importance. Millions of people in West Bengal and Bangladesh depend on them — for fishing, for clean water, and for storm protection. Protecting the Sundarbans is not just about saving tigers; it is about safeguarding human communities too.

## Farming Without Harming

**Child:** Maa, the Green Revolution made India self-sufficient in food, but the chapter says chemical farming has hurt the soil in states like Punjab and Haryana.

**Mother:** That is the dilemma, beta. High-yield seeds and chemical fertilisers boosted grain production enormously — India went from importing wheat to exporting it. But after decades of heavy chemical use, soils have lost their natural richness. Earthworms and helpful bacteria have been killed. Groundwater in some areas is contaminated with nitrates.

**Child:** The ancient text Vrikshayurveda talks about organic manures like Kunapa Jala — a liquid fertiliser made by fermenting plant and animal waste. Were our ancestors practising organic farming all along?

**Mother:** In many ways, yes. They understood that healthy soil produces healthy crops. Today, states like Sikkim — which became fully organic in 2016 — and Andhra Pradesh — with its large-scale natural farming programme — are reviving these principles. The goal is to grow enough food for everyone without poisoning the land that feeds us.

**Child:** So protecting ecosystems is not just a feel-good idea — it is essential for our own survival.

**Mother:** Beautifully put. Every glass of water we drink, every plate of rice we eat, every breath of clean air — all of it comes from a functioning ecosystem. Take care of nature, and nature takes care of us.
