## Living in a Connected World

![Living in a connected world — biotic and abiotic components of a habitat](living-in-a-connected-world.png)

In many parts of Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, wild monkeys regularly raid farms and kitchens when their forest habitat shrinks due to road construction and urbanisation. As trees disappear, the monkeys lose their natural food sources and wander into human settlements. This chain of cause and effect illustrates a powerful truth: **every element in nature is linked to every other**.

A **habitat** is the natural home of an organism — it could be a marsh, a hillside forest, a wheat field, or even the underside of a fallen log. Every habitat is made of two types of components:

- **Biotic components** — the living inhabitants (plants, animals, bacteria, fungi)
- **Abiotic components** — the non-living surroundings (sunlight, air, water, soil, temperature)

Different organisms need different conditions to survive, which is why no two habitats host exactly the same set of species.

> **Key idea:** The biotic and abiotic parts of any habitat are in constant conversation. These interactions determine which organisms thrive and how the entire living community functions.

## Population and Community

![Population and community — populations of species sharing a habitat](population-and-community.png)

### Population

A **population** is all the individuals of one species living together in a particular area at a particular time. For instance, all the neem trees along a village road form one population.

### Community

A **community** is the collection of different populations sharing the same habitat. The neem trees, the parakeets nesting in them, the insects crawling on the bark, and the fungi growing at the base — together, these form a community. Members of a community depend on and interact with one another in countless ways.

## Does Every Single Organism Matter?

![Does every single organism matter — frogs, mosquitoes, butterflies, crops chain](does-every-single-organism-matter.png)

Absolutely. Consider this example from a village pond:

- **Frogs** in the pond eat **mosquito larvae**
- Fewer mosquitoes means more **butterflies and moths** survive (since they compete with mosquitoes for certain resources)
- More butterflies means better **pollination** of nearby brinjal and pumpkin plants
- Better pollination leads to **higher vegetable yields** for the farmer

So frogs in a pond indirectly help the farmer's kitchen garden! This is how every creature, no matter how small, is woven into the fabric of its ecosystem.

> **Key idea:** A change in even one population can trigger a chain of effects that ripples across the entire ecosystem.

## Ecosystems — Where Life and Environment Meet

An **ecosystem** is the system formed by the interactions between all the biotic and abiotic components in an area.

### Varieties of Ecosystems

![Varieties of ecosystems — terrestrial and aquatic](varieties-of-ecosystems.png)

- **Land-based (terrestrial)** — forests, grasslands, deserts, farmlands
- **Water-based (aquatic)** — ponds, rivers, lakes, coral reefs, open oceans

An ecosystem can be as vast as the Western Ghats or as compact as a single banyan tree and all the organisms living on and around it.

### Ecosystems Built by Humans

![Ecosystems built by humans — fish tanks, paddies, gardens, parks](ecosystems-built-by-humans.png)

People have created artificial ecosystems such as fish tanks, rice paddies, kitchen gardens, and city parks. Unlike natural ecosystems, these need regular human intervention — watering, weeding, feeding — to keep functioning.

## Who Eats What? — Feeding Roles

![Who eats what — producers, consumers, decomposers](who-eats-what-feeding-roles.png)

Organisms in an ecosystem can be sorted by how they obtain food:

### Producers
**Green plants** manufacture their own food through photosynthesis — using sunlight, carbon dioxide, and water. They are the **producers**, the foundation upon which every other organism ultimately depends.

### Consumers
Animals that eat other organisms for food are **consumers**:
- **Herbivores** — feed exclusively on plants (e.g., goat, elephant, parrot)
- **Carnivores** — feed on other animals (e.g., lion, hawk, cobra)
- **Omnivores** — eat both plants and animals (e.g., monkey, crow, human)

### Decomposers
**Bacteria** and **fungi** break down dead plants and animals into simpler substances, releasing nutrients back into the soil. Without these silent workers, the soil would never be replenished.

> **Key idea:** If decomposers vanished, dead matter would accumulate endlessly and essential nutrients would remain locked away. Eventually, plants would starve, and the whole ecosystem would grind to a halt.

## Food Chains and Food Webs

### Food Chain

A **food chain** traces the path of energy from one organism to the next. Each step along the chain is called a **trophic level**.

![Food chain showing grass, grasshopper, frog, snake, and eagle with arrows indicating energy flow](food-chain.png)

Example: **Paddy plant** (producer) --> **Field mouse** (herbivore) --> **Snake** (carnivore) --> **Hawk** (top carnivore)

### Food Web

![Food web diagram showing interconnected food chains with multiple organisms linked by feeding relationships](food-web.png)

In nature, animals rarely eat only one thing, and most organisms have multiple predators. When many food chains criss-cross and overlap, they form a **food web** — a far more realistic picture of who depends on whom.

## How Organisms Interact Beyond Eating

![How organisms interact beyond eating — competition, mutualism, commensalism, parasitism](how-organisms-interact-beyond-eating.png)

### Competition
When two species need the same limited resource — food, water, nesting space, or sunlight — they compete. Competition naturally **regulates population sizes** and prevents any single species from taking over.

### Mutualism
A relationship where **both partners gain something valuable**.
- Example: **Fig trees and fig wasps** — the wasp pollinates the fig flower while laying its eggs inside; the fig provides a safe nursery for the wasp's young

### Commensalism
One organism **benefits** while the other is **neither helped nor harmed**.
- Example: **Cattle egrets and grazing buffaloes** — as buffaloes walk through grass, they stir up insects that the egrets eat; the buffaloes are unaffected

### Parasitism
One organism **benefits** at the expense of the other, which is **harmed**.
- Example: **Cuscuta (amarbel) on hedges** — the parasitic vine wraps around a host plant and draws water and nutrients from it, weakening the host

## The Dynamic Balance of Ecosystems

![The dynamic balance of ecosystems — populations rise and fall around stability](the-dynamic-balance-of-ecosystems.png)

A healthy ecosystem maintains a **dynamic balance** — populations rise and fall, but the overall system stays roughly stable. This balance is not fixed like a statue; it is more like a tightrope walker making constant tiny adjustments.

### When the Balance Tips Too Far

During the 1980s, India exported vast quantities of frog legs to European markets. Frog populations crashed. Since frogs are voracious insect-eaters, the sudden drop in frog numbers led to a **surge in crop-damaging pests**. Farmers responded by spraying heavier doses of **chemical pesticides**, which poisoned the soil, contaminated groundwater, and harmed human health. Recognising the cascading damage, the Government of India eventually **banned frog leg exports**.

## What Ecosystems Give Us

![What ecosystems give us — air, water, food, climate, recreation](what-ecosystems-give-us.png)

Ecosystems deliver a host of services that we often take for granted:
- **Forests** supply clean air, timber, medicinal herbs, fertile soil, and rainfall regulation
- **Wetlands and rivers** provide drinking water, irrigation, and fish
- **Mangrove forests** (like the Sundarbans) act as natural storm barriers, shielding coastal villages from cyclones and tidal surges
- **All green ecosystems** absorb carbon dioxide and release oxygen
- **Natural landscapes** offer recreation and mental well-being

### The Sundarbans

The **Sundarbans** — straddling the delta where the Ganga and Brahmaputra empty into the Bay of Bengal — is the planet's largest mangrove forest. Home to the Royal Bengal Tiger and countless other species, it was designated a **UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987**. Today it faces pressure from illegal logging, shrimp farming, pollution, and rising sea levels.

## Threats Facing Ecosystems

![Threats facing ecosystems — deforestation, pollution, overexploitation, invasives](threats-facing-ecosystems.png)

- **Deforestation** — clearing forests for farmland, roads, and buildings
- **Pollution** — factory discharge, sewage, and plastic waste
- **Overexploitation** — overfishing, excessive groundwater pumping
- **Invasive species** — foreign organisms that outcompete local species (e.g., water hyacinth choking Indian lakes)
- **Monoculture** — planting the same crop season after season, which saps soil nutrients and reduces biodiversity
- **Chemical overload** — excessive use of synthetic fertilisers and pesticides, which kills beneficial soil life

### India's Protected Natural Areas

![India's protected natural areas — national parks and wildlife sanctuaries](indias-protected-natural-areas.png)

India safeguards its biodiversity through a network of protected areas:
- **Kaziranga National Park** (Assam) — home to the one-horned rhinoceros
- **Periyar Wildlife Sanctuary** (Kerala) — protects elephants and tigers in the Western Ghats
- **Rann of Kutch Wildlife Sanctuary** (Gujarat) — habitat of the Indian wild ass
- **Sundarbans National Park** (West Bengal) — mangrove ecosystem and Bengal tigers
- **Valley of Flowers** (Uttarakhand) — a UNESCO site known for its alpine meadow flowers
- **Keibul Lamjao National Park** (Manipur) — the world's only floating national park

## Farming in Harmony with Nature

![Farming in harmony with nature — organic and natural farming methods](farming-in-harmony-with-nature.png)

The **Green Revolution** of the 1960s and 1970s transformed Indian agriculture with high-yield seeds, chemical fertilisers, and pesticides. Food production soared, but decades of heavy chemical use have caused:
- **Declining soil fertility** and loss of humus
- **Destruction of helpful soil bacteria and earthworms**
- **Groundwater contamination**
- **Pest resistance** — insects evolving to withstand the chemicals meant to kill them

To heal the land, many farmers are turning to **organic and natural farming** methods that work with nature rather than against it. The ancient Indian agricultural text **Vrikshayurveda** describes preparations like **Kunapa Jala** — a liquid manure brewed from decomposed plant and animal waste — that enriches soil without synthetic chemicals.

## Key Takeaways

- **Habitats** consist of biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) components.
- The interplay between biotic and abiotic elements forms an **ecosystem**.
- Organisms are classified as **producers** (plants), **consumers** (herbivores, carnivores, omnivores), and **decomposers** (bacteria, fungi).
- **Food chains** trace the flow of energy from one organism to the next; **food webs** depict the interconnected network of many food chains.
- Organisms relate through **competition**, **mutualism**, **commensalism**, and **parasitism**.
- Ecosystems provide vital services: breathable air, clean water, food, medicines, and climate stability.
- Human activities such as pollution, deforestation, and resource overuse endanger ecosystems.
- **Protected areas** across India — national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves — help conserve the country's extraordinary biodiversity.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Project Tiger and the Sunderbans tigers.** When Project Tiger began in 1973, India had only around 1,800 tigers left. Today the count is over 3,600 — including the Royal Bengal Tigers of the Sunderbans. But this is not just about tigers. Tigers are at the top of the food chain, so protecting them means protecting deer, monkeys, the trees they live among, and the mangroves the deer feed on. Pulling one species out from the top would unravel the whole web below. The Sunderbans also shield coastal villages in West Bengal from cyclones — saving tigers turned out to be the same project as saving fishermen's homes.

**Amarbel choking your colony hedges.** That orange thread-like vine smothering the hibiscus or the boundary hedge in your colony is cuscuta — the amarbel parasite. It has no green leaves of its own and cannot photosynthesise. So it wraps around healthy plants, sinks tiny suckers into their stems, and drinks their water and nutrients. Over a few weeks the host weakens and may die. The next time the malis weed it out, you are watching a parasitism intervention — removing one organism so others can survive.

**Sikkim's organic farming.** In 2016, Sikkim became India's first fully organic state — no chemical fertilisers, no synthetic pesticides allowed on any farm. Crop yields dipped at first, but the soil started recovering. Earthworms returned. Birds came back to the fields. Tourists started visiting for "organic farm stays." Andhra Pradesh now runs the world's largest natural farming programme. The principle is straight out of Vrikshayurveda, a 1,500-year-old Indian text: healthy soil produces healthy crops, and chemicals that kill pests also kill the helpful insects, bacteria and fungi that keep soil alive.

### Try it at home -- the garden ecosystem mapper

*⏱ 15 min a day for one week · 🧹 zero mess · 👨‍👩‍👧 no adult help needed (but Daadi may know the names of plants you spot)*

**What you'll need:**

- A notebook and pencil
- A small garden, park, balcony with potted plants, or even a tree-lined street
- Optional: a phone camera

**Steps:**

1. Pick one spot — a corner of your colony park, a balcony, a school garden patch. Visit the same spot for 15 minutes each day for a week.
2. Each day, list **5 different living things** you see (or hear). One plant, one insect, one bird, one small animal, one decomposer (a fungus, a worm, ants carrying away crumbs). Sketch each one.
3. Note any **abiotic clues** — sunny or shady spot, wet or dry soil, presence of fallen leaves, neem leaves on the ground.
4. By the end of the week, you should have around 25-35 organisms listed.
5. On the last day, **draw arrows** between them to show "who eats whom." A caterpillar eats spinach leaves — arrow from spinach to caterpillar. A sparrow eats the caterpillar — arrow from caterpillar to sparrow. You are now building your own food web.

**What to look for:**

- How many trophic levels can you trace? (Producers like grass → herbivores like caterpillars/squirrels → carnivores like lizards/cats → decomposers like ants and fungi.)
- Which species had the most arrows pointing at it? (That is a popular food source — fragile if it disappears.)
- Are there any pairs that seem to help each other? (Bees on flowers = mutualism. Egrets near a buffalo or cow = commensalism.)

**Show what you found:** Sketch your food web in your notebook with arrows showing energy flow. Show your Daadi (or dadu, mummy, older cousin) and tell them: "If we remove just one species from this web — say, the sparrows — the caterpillars would multiply, eat all the plants, and the whole web would collapse." Ask them if they remember more sparrows in their childhood than today.

**A question to keep asking all year:** *When I see any creature — a butterfly, a stray dog, a fungus on a rotting fruit — what other lives is it connected to, and what happens if it disappears?*
