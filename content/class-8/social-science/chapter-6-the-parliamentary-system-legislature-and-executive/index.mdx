## A School Trip to Sansad Bhavan

Imagine a Class 8 group from a school in Patna stepping off a bus on a winter morning at the edge of Lutyens' Delhi. Right ahead of them rises a soft, triangular building — not the round, columned one their parents grew up seeing in newspapers, but a brand-new structure: **Sansad Bhavan**, the new Parliament House inaugurated in May 2023.

The students stop to look. The walls inside carry carvings of peacocks, lotuses, temple panels, tribal art, and portraits of freedom fighters. The **Ashoka emblem** — the four lions of Sarnath — looks down from above. Near the Speaker's chair stands the **Sengol**, a gold-plated silver sceptre handed over to Pandit Jawaharlal Nehru on the night of 14 August 1947 as a Chola-era symbol of righteous, just rule. The whole building feels less like an office and more like a museum of India.

But this building is also where **decisions that affect 140 crore Indians** are made every single working day. Tax rates, education laws, road budgets, women's reservation, the price of LPG cylinders — most of it begins or ends here.

This chapter is about what actually happens inside that building and the State Assemblies that mirror it. We will look at the **two houses of Parliament**, the **President**, the **Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers**, how a **bill becomes a law**, and why our system is called a **parliamentary democracy** rather than a presidential one.

## Why India Chose the Parliamentary System

When India became independent in 1947, the Constituent Assembly had a choice. They could pick the **American (presidential) model**, where the President is directly elected and runs the government separately from the legislature. Or they could pick the **British (parliamentary) model**, where the head of the government comes from the legislature and answers to it every day.

They picked the **parliamentary model**. Three reasons stand out.

1. **Familiarity.** Indians had worked with parliamentary procedures during British rule, even if in a very limited way. The 1935 Government of India Act had set up legislatures in the provinces. The vocabulary — Speaker, sitting, bill, motion — was already in use.
2. **The memory of older Indian institutions.** Ancient **mahajanapadas** like Vajji had been republics. Village **panchayats** had practised collective decision-making for centuries. The Uttaramerur inscription described 10th-century elections in a Chola village. The idea of a council of elders deliberating and deciding was deeply Indian.
3. **A government that can be removed.** In the parliamentary system, if the Prime Minister loses the confidence of the Lok Sabha, the government falls. In the presidential system, a deeply unpopular leader could continue for the full term. The Constituent Assembly preferred a government that **stays accountable every single day**.

So India became a **parliamentary democracy with a written constitution** — borrowing structure from Britain but rooting it firmly in the Indian Constitution as the supreme document.

## The Composition of Parliament

The **Parliament of India** is made up of three parts:

1. The **President of India**
2. The **Lok Sabha** — the House of the People (Lower House)
3. The **Rajya Sabha** — the Council of States (Upper House)

A legislature with two houses is called a **bicameral** system ("bi" = two, "cameral" = chamber). India is bicameral at the Union level. Most states are unicameral (one house), but six states — Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, and Uttar Pradesh — also have two houses at the state level.

### The Lok Sabha — The House of the People

The Lok Sabha is the **directly elected** house. Every five years (or sooner, if it is dissolved), Indians aged 18 and above go to the polls and vote for one **Member of Parliament (MP)** from their constituency. There are currently **543 elected seats**, with the maximum envisaged by the Constitution being 550. The 18th Lok Sabha was constituted in **June 2024** — the seventeenth before it began with the very first general election of 1952.

The number of Lok Sabha seats from each state is **based on its population**. So Uttar Pradesh sends 80 MPs while Sikkim sends only 1. The presiding officer of the Lok Sabha is the **Speaker**, who is elected from among the MPs themselves. The Speaker conducts sessions, decides who can speak, maintains discipline, and rules on points of procedure. A good Speaker, by convention, rises above party loyalty once elected.

### The Rajya Sabha — The Council of States

The Rajya Sabha is the **indirectly elected** house. Its members are chosen not by the people directly but by the **elected members of state Legislative Assemblies (MLAs)** through a proportional system. It has up to **245 members**: 233 elected from states and Union Territories, and up to 12 **nominated by the President** for their distinction in literature, science, art, or social service. Lata Mangeshkar, Sachin Tendulkar, M.S. Subbulakshmi, and Rekha have all served as nominated Rajya Sabha members at different times.

Unlike the Lok Sabha, the Rajya Sabha is a **permanent body** — it is never dissolved. One-third of its members retire every two years, and fresh members take their place. Each Rajya Sabha member has a term of **six years**. The **Vice President of India** is the ex-officio Chairperson of the Rajya Sabha.

The Rajya Sabha was created in the spirit of **federalism**: a separate space where the **states**, not just the people, are represented. It also acts as a steadier, more deliberative house — less swayed by the day's political mood.

## The Functions of Parliament

The Constitution gives Parliament four broad jobs. Let's walk through them.

### 1. Constitutional Functions

Parliament is the **guardian of the Constitution**. It elects the President and Vice President (along with state MLAs in the case of the President), and it can **amend the Constitution** itself, subject to the procedures laid out in Article 368. Some amendments need a two-thirds majority in both houses; some additionally need the approval of at least half the state legislatures. Either way, no government can casually change the Constitution.

Parliament is also expected to uphold the **core values** of the Constitution:
- **Universal Adult Franchise** — every adult votes regardless of caste, gender, religion, or wealth.
- **Separation of powers** — legislature, executive, judiciary stay in their lanes.
- **Federalism** — power shared between Union and states.
- **Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles** — these guide every law made.

### 2. Lawmaking

This is the function people associate most strongly with Parliament. A proposed law begins as a **bill**. Most bills are introduced by **ministers** on behalf of the government; a few are introduced by individual MPs (called "Private Members' Bills") and rarely become law without government support.

A bill goes through a long journey before it becomes law. The standard process is:

1. **Introduction** in either House (the "First Reading"). The minister or MP introduces the bill; copies are distributed.
2. **Referral to a Standing Committee.** Not every bill is referred, but many are. The committee studies the bill, holds consultations, and submits a report with suggested changes.
3. **Second Reading.** The House discusses the bill in general, then clause by clause. Amendments are moved and voted on.
4. **Third Reading and Vote.** The bill as amended is put to a vote.
5. **Process repeated in the other House.** If the Lok Sabha passes it, it goes to the Rajya Sabha (or vice versa).
6. **Presidential Assent.** The President signs. Once assented, the bill becomes an **Act**.
7. **Gazette Notification.** The Act is published in the official Gazette and comes into force.

A famous example is the **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 (RTE)**. The idea came from the Directive Principles. The 86th Constitutional Amendment in 2002 made education a Fundamental Right under **Article 21A**. The bill was tabled in the Rajya Sabha, studied by a committee, debated heavily (mainly about funding the new teachers, schools, and infrastructure needed for millions of children), passed in the Lok Sabha in August 2009, received Presidential assent, and became law. Today, RTE guarantees free education to every Indian child between 6 and 14 years.

Some bills can only start in the Lok Sabha. The most important is a **Money Bill** — any bill that deals with taxation, government borrowing, or government spending. A Money Bill can only be introduced in the Lok Sabha and only with the **prior recommendation of the President**. The Rajya Sabha can suggest changes but cannot reject a Money Bill.

### 3. Executive Accountability

Once laws are passed, the **Council of Ministers** has to implement them. But what stops ministers from doing as they please? Parliament does — through several mechanisms.

- **Question Hour.** The first hour of every parliamentary sitting (typically 11 am to 12 noon) is reserved for MPs to ask ministers questions about their ministries. Why are vacancies in central schools unfilled? How many railway accidents this year? What is the status of a particular scheme in Jharkhand? Ministers must answer.
- **Zero Hour.** Right after Question Hour, MPs can raise matters of urgent public importance without prior notice. The name comes from the fact that it begins at "zero" — 12 noon — and was not originally planned in the rulebook.
- **Standing Committees.** These are permanent committees with MPs from different parties. They scrutinise government activities, examine bills, and call ministers and officials to explain their work. The committee on **Health and Family Welfare**, for example, reviews how the Ministry of Health is implementing programmes like Ayush.
- **No-Confidence Motion.** If a majority of Lok Sabha MPs vote that they no longer have confidence in the government, the government must resign. This is the ultimate accountability — it can bring down a Prime Minister.
- **Debates.** Through general debates, calling-attention motions, and adjournment motions, Parliament puts the government's actions in public view.

### 4. Financial Accountability

The government cannot spend a single rupee of the taxpayer's money without Parliament's approval. Every year, the **Union Budget** is presented in the Lok Sabha — usually on the first day of February. The budget lists every ministry's proposed spending. MPs debate it. Then it is voted on, one section at a time. Only after this does the government get the legal authority to spend.

After the spending has happened, the **Public Accounts Committee (PAC)** — a powerful committee of MPs, traditionally chaired by an Opposition MP — examines whether the money was actually spent the way Parliament had authorised. The **Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG)** independently audits government accounts and reports to Parliament. If money has been misused, the PAC drags it into the open. This is the **financial leash** Parliament keeps on the executive.

## The Executive — Who Actually Runs the Country

Parliament makes laws. But somebody has to **carry them out**, day after day. That somebody is the **Union Executive**:

1. The **President of India** (nominal head)
2. The **Vice President of India**
3. The **Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister** (real, working head)

### The President

The President is the **Head of State** and the **nominal head of the executive**. The Prime Minister and Council of Ministers actually run the government, but they do so technically "in the name of the President." The President:
- **Appoints the Prime Minister** (always the leader of the majority party or coalition in the Lok Sabha).
- **Appoints other ministers** on the PM's advice.
- **Summons and prorogues Parliament**, and can dissolve the Lok Sabha.
- **Gives assent to bills** — without the President's signature, a bill cannot become law.
- **Is the Supreme Commander of the Armed Forces.**
- **Can exercise discretionary powers** in unusual situations — for example, when no party has a clear majority and the President has to decide whom to invite to form a government.

The President is **elected indirectly** by an electoral college of elected MPs and MLAs, for a term of five years. Dr Rajendra Prasad was the first; **Smt. Droupadi Murmu** — India's second woman President and the first tribal President — currently holds the office.

### The Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers

The **Prime Minister** is the **de facto executive authority** in India. The PM is appointed by the President but only because they lead the majority in the Lok Sabha. They:
- **Lead the Council of Ministers** — chair Cabinet meetings, decide who gets which portfolio.
- **Coordinate work across ministries** — defence, finance, education, health, external affairs.
- **Advise the President** on key decisions.
- **Shape national policy** — Make in India, Digital India, National Education Policy, foreign policy moves.

The **Council of Ministers** is divided into three ranks: **Cabinet Ministers** (the most senior, who run major ministries), **Ministers of State (Independent Charge)**, and **Ministers of State**. Together they take decisions in **Cabinet meetings** and run the day-to-day government. They are **collectively responsible** to the Lok Sabha — if the Lok Sabha votes no-confidence, the whole government falls together, not just the PM.

The famous case of **Lal Bahadur Shastri** captures this spirit. In 1956, when he was Railway Minister, a major train accident took many lives. Shastri was not personally at fault, but he believed a minister must take **moral responsibility** for what happens in their ministry. He offered his resignation. Pandit Nehru declined it twice; Shastri insisted, and his resignation was finally accepted. Shastri went on to become Prime Minister in 1964 — partly because India remembered this act of integrity.

### The Civil Service — The Permanent Government

Behind the ministers stands a permanent group of officers — the **civil service** or **bureaucracy**. IAS, IPS, IFS, IRS officers — selected through tough national exams — implement policies, run ministries, manage districts, and keep the system going even when governments change. Ministers come and go after every election; civil servants stay. This balance gives India both **democratic accountability** (through ministers) and **administrative continuity** (through officers).

## Separation of Powers — and Why the Executive Sits Inside the Legislature

A natural question arises: if the Prime Minister and ministers come **from** Parliament, how is there a "separation of powers"?

In India's parliamentary system, the executive is **fused with** the legislature, but the two functions are still distinct. The PM and Council of Ministers are **members of Parliament** — but their **role as executives** is to implement laws and run ministries, while their **role as parliamentarians** is to make laws and represent constituents. The **Lok Sabha as a whole** keeps the executive accountable through Question Hour, debates, committees, and the no-confidence motion. Most MPs are not in the government — they are backbenchers and Opposition members whose job is to question and scrutinise.

Then comes the **third organ — the Judiciary**, completely separate from both. The Supreme Court and High Courts can:
- Strike down a law passed by Parliament if it violates the Constitution.
- Strike down executive actions if they violate fundamental rights.
- Settle disputes between the Union and states, between citizens and the state.

This is the famous **system of checks and balances**. The legislature checks the executive. The judiciary checks both. And the people, every five years, check Parliament itself through elections.

## Parliament at the State Level

The structure at the Centre is mirrored at the State level. Each state has:

- A **Governor** (appointed by the President) as nominal head — equivalent to the President.
- A **Chief Minister** who is the real executive head — equivalent to the PM.
- A **Vidhan Sabha** (Legislative Assembly) — the directly elected house, equivalent to the Lok Sabha. Its members are **MLAs**.
- In six states, a **Vidhan Parishad** (Legislative Council) — the upper house, equivalent to the Rajya Sabha.

The Constitution divides law-making subjects into three lists in the Seventh Schedule:

- **Union List** — only Parliament can legislate (defence, foreign affairs, currency, atomic energy, railways).
- **State List** — only states can legislate (police, public health, agriculture, local government).
- **Concurrent List** — both can legislate (education, criminal law, marriage and divorce). If a Union law conflicts with a state law on a Concurrent subject, the **Union law prevails**.

The RTE Act is a Concurrent List example — education is a shared subject, but Parliament's RTE Act applies across all states.

## Challenges Facing the Indian Parliament

Indian Parliament is one of the world's largest deliberative bodies — and one of its more troubled ones. Several real concerns have grown over the decades.

**Falling productivity.** In the **1st Lok Sabha (1952-57)**, MPs met for **677 sittings** over five years. The **13th Lok Sabha (1999-2004)** managed only **356 sittings** — almost half. In 2018, the Rajya Sabha recorded its **lowest-ever productivity of 35.75%** due to disruptions, according to its then-Chairman M. Venkaiah Naidu. When sessions are washed out, important bills get postponed or rushed through without proper debate.

**Disruptions of Question Hour.** Question Hour is the single biggest tool Parliament has to hold the executive accountable. When it is regularly disrupted, ministers escape scrutiny.

**Criminalisation.** A large share of MPs have criminal cases against them. The Supreme Court has tried to push reforms requiring disclosure of cases at the time of nominations.

**Underrepresentation.** Women have historically been under 15% of Lok Sabha MPs. The **Women's Reservation Bill, 2023**, which reserves one-third of Lok Sabha and State Assembly seats for women, was passed after 27 years of debate and will come into effect after the next delimitation.

**Long delays.** Some bills sit for years before being discussed. Others are pushed through in days without enough scrutiny — including being passed in a noisy House where no real debate happens.

But Indian Parliament has also shown its strength. It debated and passed the **Right to Information Act, 2005**, the **MGNREGA Act, 2005**, the **GST Acts of 2016-17**, and the **Women's Reservation Bill, 2023**. It has held the executive accountable through committees, no-confidence motions, and impeachment processes. It is, despite all its problems, the **living voice of the world's largest democracy**.

The former PM Atal Bihari Vajpayee once said in the Lok Sabha: *"Sarkaarein aayengi, jaayengi. Paartiyaan banengi, bigadengi. Lekin yeh desh rehna chaahiye, is desh ka loktantra amar rehna chaahiye."* (Governments will come and go, parties will rise and fall. But the nation must endure, and its democracy must live on.)

That is the spirit of the parliamentary system. It will not always be tidy. It will not always be efficient. But it is **ours** — chosen by our Constituent Assembly, used by our parents and grandparents, and entrusted to us.

## Before We Close

- India is a **parliamentary democracy** with the **Constitution** as the supreme law.
- Parliament has **three parts**: the **President**, the **Lok Sabha** (543 seats), and the **Rajya Sabha** (up to 245 seats).
- The Lok Sabha is **directly elected** and presided over by the **Speaker**; the Rajya Sabha is **indirectly elected** and chaired by the **Vice President**.
- Parliament's four big jobs: **constitutional functions, lawmaking, executive accountability, financial accountability**.
- A bill becomes a law through introduction, committee study, clause-by-clause debate, vote in both houses, **Presidential assent**, and gazette notification.
- **Money Bills** can only originate in the Lok Sabha and require the President's prior recommendation.
- The **Union Executive** is the President, Vice President, and Council of Ministers led by the **Prime Minister**.
- Tools of accountability include **Question Hour, Zero Hour, Standing Committees, the Public Accounts Committee, debates,** and the **no-confidence motion**.
- At the state level, the **Vidhan Sabha** mirrors the Lok Sabha; in six states a **Vidhan Parishad** also exists.
- Power is shared across **Union, State, and Concurrent lists**; the **judiciary** keeps the legislature and executive within the Constitution through **checks and balances**.

## Apply — Bring It Into Your World

### Stories from the Parliament Floor

**1. A late-night debate that changed India.** On the night of **27 May 1964**, Pandit Nehru, India's first PM, passed away. Parliament met later that week to pay tributes and choose the next leader. Lal Bahadur Shastri — small in build, soft-spoken, son of a schoolteacher — was elected by the Congress Parliamentary Party. His acceptance speech in the Lok Sabha was full of humility. Within weeks he handled the 1965 war with Pakistan. His coining of *"Jai Jawan, Jai Kisan"* in a Lok Sabha-era speech became a national slogan. One short Parliament moment shaped a decade.

**2. Your own MP.** Every Indian lives in a Lok Sabha constituency. Yours might be Patna Sahib, Bengaluru South, Mumbai North, Sambalpur, or Jaipur Rural — the country has 543. Your MP attends Parliament sessions on your behalf. They get a budget called **MPLADS** (around Rs. 5 crore per year) to spend on local development. Many MPs ask questions during Question Hour. Some never speak. Some have brilliant attendance; some skip most sessions. Knowing who they are and what they do is the first step in being a citizen.

**3. The youngest MP ever.** The Indian Constitution sets the **minimum age for Lok Sabha membership at 25**. The youngest person ever elected was **Chandrani Murmu** from Keonjhar, Odisha — she was **25 years 11 months** when she took oath in 2019. Several MPs in the 18th Lok Sabha (2024-) are in their late 20s and early 30s. As the median age of India is around 28, our Parliament is slowly looking more like the country it represents.

### DIY — Try This

1. **Find your MP.** Use the Lok Sabha website (loksabha.nic.in) or PRS Legislative Research (prsindia.org) to find out which Lok Sabha constituency you live in, who your current MP is, which party they belong to, and how many questions they have asked in Parliament this term.
2. **Watch Question Hour.** Sansad TV streams Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha sittings live. Or search "Lok Sabha Question Hour" on YouTube and watch any 10-minute clip. Notice how ministers answer, who interrupts, what topics come up.
3. **Interview a parent or grandparent.** Ask: *"How do you think Parliament has changed in your lifetime? When you were young, did people watch it? Did your MP ever come to your area? Do you think it works better now or worse?"* Write down their answer in 200 words.

### Show What You Found

Bring your findings to class. Make a simple poster: **MY MP**, with name, party, constituency, terms served, top issue they raise, and one good thing and one weak thing about their record. Compare with classmates.

### A Question to Keep Asking

If your MP, your MLA, and the local mayor all claim credit for the same new road in your area — **who actually decided it, who paid for it, and whom do you hold responsible if the road breaks in the first monsoon?** Knowing the answer is what separates a citizen from a spectator.
