## When Does Something Become a "Resource"?

Look around the room you are sitting in. A wooden chair. A cotton shirt. A glass of water. A switch on the wall that brings light. A phone in someone's hand. Every single object — even the plastic button on a shirt — began somewhere in **Nature**. None of these things were invented from nothing.

But here is a curious thought. A tree growing wild in a forest is not yet a chair. A river flowing down a hillside is not yet drinking water. Coal sitting two kilometres under the earth is not yet electricity. These are parts of **Nature** — they exist whether humans are there or not.

They become **resources** only when three things line up:

- We have the **technology** to reach them
- It is **economical** to extract and use them
- It is **culturally acceptable** to take them

The second and third conditions are easy to miss. There is petroleum deep below the Indian Ocean floor — but if drilling it would cost more than selling it, it is not a resource yet. Trees in a **sacred grove** like the *kavu* of Kerala or the *orans* of Rajasthan are not cut for furniture — because the community considers them sacred. The trees are there. The wood is there. But culturally, it is not a resource.

So a **natural resource** is any material or substance found in Nature that humans can reach, can afford, and feel right about using.

## Three Ways to Sort Them by What They Do for Us

Sorting things into categories is one of the most useful tricks in science. Once you give a category a name, you can talk about a whole group at once instead of describing each member. With natural resources, one simple system is to sort them by **what we use them for**.

### Resources Essential for Life

Some resources we cannot live without — not for an hour, not for a day. The **air** in our lungs. The **water** we drink. The **soil** that grows our food. We did not invent any of these. We cannot manufacture a substitute for breathable air. These are the resources Nature gives us for life itself.

### Resources for Materials

Other resources we turn into **things** — a plank of teak becomes a desk; a block of Makrana marble becomes the floor of a hotel; iron ore from Bailadila becomes steel beams; cotton from Maharashtra becomes a school uniform. India's land gives us an unusually wide range of these material resources, from sandalwood to slate and from bauxite to bamboo.

### Resources for Energy

A third group powers our buildings, vehicles and machines. **Coal** lights our cities, **petroleum** runs our trucks, **natural gas** cooks our food, **flowing water** spins turbines in dams, **sunshine** strikes the solar panels at Bhadla, **wind** turns the giant blades near Kanyakumari. The kind of energy a place uses says a lot about what kind of life people there live.

## Renewable and Non-Renewable

There is a second way of sorting resources, and it is the one that matters most for thinking about the future. We ask one simple question of every resource: **does Nature make more of it at roughly the speed we use it?**

### What Nature Does on Her Own

Left alone, Nature works in **cycles**. A tree falls in a forest. Fungi and bacteria break it down. Insects feed on it. Its body becomes soil. New seeds sprout from that soil. Eventually one of those new trees will also fall, and the cycle starts again. There is no waste in this cycle — every "leftover" is some other creature's meal. This is what scientists call **restoration** and **regeneration**.

Restoration means returning to health after damage — a forest re-growing after a wildfire, a cut on your hand sealing itself. Regeneration goes further: it is Nature's ability to grow new life altogether.

### Renewable Resources

A **renewable resource** is one that Nature keeps making — as long as we do not interfere with her rhythm. Sunlight will fall on Rajasthan tomorrow whether or not we collected today's. Rivers fed by monsoons and Himalayan glaciers will fill again next year. A managed forest can be cut a little each year, year after year, forever.

But notice the catch in that last sentence: **"a little each year"**. Renewable does not mean unlimited. If we cut timber from a forest faster than the forest can grow new trees, the forest stops being renewable for us. If a river is asked to give more water than the monsoon refills, the river runs dry. Renewability is a relationship between **how fast Nature replenishes** and **how fast we take**.

A worrying example: the Himalayan glaciers are sometimes called India's **"water tower"** because the Ganga, Yamuna, Brahmaputra and many others depend on glacial melt. Rising temperatures, partly caused by burning fossil fuels and clearing forests, are melting those glaciers faster than fresh snow can replace them. A renewable resource is being pushed toward non-renewable territory by our own actions.

### Non-Renewable Resources

A **non-renewable resource** is one Nature makes only over geological time — millions of years. **Coal**, **petroleum**, **natural gas**, **iron ore**, **copper**, **gold**, **diamonds**: these formed across vast spans of Earth's history. Once we burn them or use them up, we have no way to wait for more.

India has large reserves of coal — currently estimated to last about **50 more years** at present rates of extraction. That sounds like a long time, but it really isn't. A baby born today will still be 50 when those reserves run out. The challenge isn't only to use what we have carefully; it is to **switch to renewables before we run out**.

## Where Things Are — and Why That Matters

Open any geography map of India that shows minerals or fuels, and one fact jumps out: **resources are not spread evenly**. Look at the map of important minerals — coal clusters in the **Damodar Valley** (Jharia, Bokaro, Raniganj), in the **Mahanadi basin** (Talcher, Korba), and in the **Godavari basin** (Singareni). Iron ore concentrates in **Bailadila** in Chhattisgarh, **Keonjhar** and **Mayurbhanj** in Odisha, and **Ballari** in Karnataka. Petroleum comes from **Mumbai High**, **Ankleshwar** in Gujarat, and the **Digboi-Naharkatia** fields of Assam. Bauxite is mined at **Amarkantak** and **Koraput**. Almost no district has everything; almost every district has something.

This uneven distribution shapes a great deal about how we live.

### Where Resources Are, Industries Follow

When a mineral is found in a region, factories tend to grow up nearby. The factories employ local people. A township spreads around the factory — shops, schools, hospitals, roads. **Jamshedpur**, **Bokaro** and **Rourkela** are textbook examples of cities that grew because of steel; **Digboi** in Assam is a town built on petroleum.

But these benefits come with costs. People who lived on the land before the mine arrived are sometimes **displaced** from their homes. Forests and farms make way for pits and conveyor belts. Sacred groves and ancestral shrines can come under threat — and that has led to long, painful conflicts in several parts of India.

### Where Resources Are, Trade Begins

Long before borders were drawn on maps, resources moved through trade. India's famous **Wootz steel** — the metal that became the legendary Damascus blades — left Indian ports because the iron ore, the carbon-rich crucibles and the skilled smiths happened to be in the same neighbourhood of the Deccan. Spices from Kerala, cotton from Gujarat, silk from Karnataka travelled the world for the same reason. **Trade fuelled the rise of empires** in India for two thousand years.

### Where Resources Are, Disputes Follow

Nature does not care about state lines or international borders. A river is born in one state, irrigates a second, and reaches the sea in a third. The **Kaveri**, which rises in Karnataka and flows through Tamil Nadu, Kerala and Puducherry, has been the subject of negotiations and tribunals for decades — because all four want a fair share of its water. The Brahmaputra (called Yarlung Tsangpo in Tibet, Jamuna in Bangladesh) is shared by three countries. These are not unsolvable problems — but they require patience, technical understanding, and goodwill.

## The "Resource Curse"

Here is a paradox to think about. You would expect a country sitting on huge reserves of oil, gold, or diamonds to be wealthy. But economists have noticed that this is **often not the case**. Several resource-rich nations have grown more slowly than resource-poor ones. They have nicknamed this puzzle the **resource curse** or the **paradox of plenty**.

How can that be? One common explanation is that countries which only **dig up and sell raw materials** miss the larger profits of turning them into finished goods. Selling raw iron ore earns far less than selling steel; selling crude oil earns far less than selling petrol, plastics and chemicals. Without industries to transform raw materials, the easy money from exports can crowd out the harder work of building factories, schools and skills.

India has worked hard to **avoid** this curse. It has built steel plants near its iron ore, refineries near its oil fields, aluminium smelters near its bauxite. Mineral wealth alone never made any country rich. Wealth comes from **knowledge, skill, and patient industry-building** on top of the raw material.

## When We Use Things Faster Than Nature Can Replace Them

Scientists studying the planet have raised three loud warnings: **pollution**, **biodiversity loss** and **climate change**. All three are connected. All three are speeding up. All three trace back to the same root — using natural resources faster than Nature can restore or regenerate them. Let us look at two examples close to home.

### The Punjab Groundwater Story

In the 1960s, Punjab was the home of India's **Green Revolution**. High-yielding varieties of wheat and rice gave huge harvests and helped India feed itself for the first time in centuries. These crops needed much more water than traditional ones, so farmers began **pumping groundwater** in vast quantities. Electricity for pumps was free or nearly free, which only encouraged more pumping. Chemical fertilisers and pesticides went in by the truckload.

Sixty years later the bill has come due. In about **80% of Punjab**, groundwater is now classified as **"overexploited"** — meaning we are pulling water out far faster than rain can put it back. In many districts the water table has fallen to thirty metres or more below the ground. Chemicals from the fertilisers and pesticides have soaked into that water, making it unsafe to drink. Short-term food security was won. The long-term cost will take generations to repair.

This problem is not Punjab's alone. **Bengaluru**, **Chennai** and several other growing cities have been warned they may run out of usable groundwater. Traditional answers — **rainwater harvesting**, the rejuvenation of village ponds and tanks, drip irrigation, the **reuse of treated wastewater** — are being tried again.

### The Cement Story

Walk past any construction site in India and you will see cement: in walls, in beams, in the road outside. We cannot imagine modern living without it. But the production of cement is one of the **most polluting industries** in the world. The fine dust harms human and animal lungs, coats the leaves of nearby crops and reduces their yields, and pollutes soil and water nearby. The **Central Pollution Control Board** has issued strict rules for cement factories — but the deeper answer is to use less cement in the first place.

Old materials are coming back. **Stone**, **mud**, **lime** and **bamboo** — once thought "village" materials — are being studied as serious building substitutes. The **Auroville Earth Institute** in Tamil Nadu has built schools, libraries and homes entirely of earth, using techniques refined over decades, and now holds the **UNESCO Chair for Earthen Architecture**. Mud structures are not new in India — the **Jaisalmer Fort** has stood for nearly a thousand years.

## Stewardship: A Word with Roots

The English word for using resources responsibly is **stewardship** — caring for something on behalf of others, including others not yet born. India's traditions have their own words for the same idea. The **Bhagavad Gita** speaks of **lokasangraha** — acting for the welfare of all, not just for one's own desires. Many indigenous traditions in India consider Nature itself as sacred — the river as **mother**, the mountain as **father**, the tree as **friend**. Touching the earth at the start of a journey, offering **arghyam** to the rising sun, planting tulsi at the doorstep — these are not just rituals; they are reminders.

Stewardship has two practical sides for the resources we have studied.

### Letting Renewables Stay Renewable

This means watching the **rate** at which we use water, soil and forests so they have time to regenerate. Some practical examples already in motion across India:

- **Rainwater harvesting** in homes and schools (mandatory in cities like Chennai and Bengaluru)
- **Watershed management** that catches monsoon water in the upper hills so it sinks back into the ground
- **Organic farming**, made famous by **Sikkim**, which became India's first fully organic state in 2016 — farmer incomes rose by 20%, soil recovered, beneficial insects returned
- **Mixed cropping** and **crop rotation**, traditions described in the ancient Indian text **Vrikshayurveda** more than a thousand years ago
- **Joint forest management** schemes where local communities are paid to protect rather than cut

### Stretching Non-Renewables Until We Don't Need Them

The strategy for coal, oil and metals is different: use them sparingly, recycle them where we can, and **race to replace them** with renewable alternatives.

This is why India has invested so heavily in **solar power**. The **Bhadla Solar Park** in Rajasthan is one of the largest solar farms in the world and can supply about 15% of Rajasthan's electricity. India also co-founded the **International Solar Alliance** with France in 2015 — a club of sun-rich countries, mostly tropical, sharing technology, financing and expertise so that even small developing nations can leapfrog the coal era and go straight to solar. Wind farms along the **Tamil Nadu** and **Gujarat** coasts add to the mix. Recycled paper, recycled steel, recycled aluminium and lately even recycled lithium from old phones all extend the life of resources we have already taken.

## Fairness: Who Gets the Resource and Who Pays the Price?

There is one more thread we should not miss. Resources and pollution are **not distributed fairly** within India. In many cities, posh colonies get clean water 24 hours a day while poorer settlements two kilometres away get an unreliable tanker. The smoke from a factory may pay for someone's profits but enter someone else's lungs. The villages displaced for a dam often get less of the electricity than the cities far away.

A responsible relationship with Nature is not only about the **planet** — it is also about **people**. Stewardship that ignores the poorest is not really stewardship at all.

## So, What Is This Generation Being Asked to Do?

The previous generation discovered the scale of damage. Your generation is being asked to **fix and prevent**. That sounds heavy, but the ingredients are familiar — and many of them you already practise:

- Turning off taps and lights when not needed
- Refusing single-use plastic where you can
- Walking, cycling or using public transport
- Choosing repair over replacement when something breaks
- Planting and protecting a tree
- Asking, in every choice, **"where did this come from, and what happens when I throw it away?"**

Multiply small habits by 140 crore people and the maths becomes very large indeed.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Three Stories from Around India

**1. The Water Tanker on Vimanapura Road**
Anika lives in Bengaluru. Every Monday and Thursday a private tanker arrives in her colony with water her family must buy by the litre — the borewell their building drilled last year has already gone dry. Two streets away, her cousin's apartment has piped supply twice a day for free. Both buildings are inside the same city. Both households use the same kind of taps. The difference is which side of the road taps into the city supply and which side relies on groundwater that fell faster than it could refill.

**2. Pema's Cardamom in Sikkim**
Pema's family farm had been losing money — chemical fertilisers cost more every year while yields kept falling. When Sikkim went fully organic in 2016, her parents switched to **compost**, **neem-and-garlic pest spray**, and **mixed cropping** of cardamom, ginger and traditional greens. The first two years were hard. By year five, beneficial insects returned, soils held water better, and Pema's cardamom now sells at premium prices to buyers in Bengaluru and Mumbai.

**3. Jharia's Underground Fire**
Beneath the coal town of Jharia in Jharkhand, an underground fire has been burning for more than a hundred years — sparked by mining, fed by coal seams. Houses crack as the earth caves in below them; families have been moved many times. The same coal that powered India's growth has cost Jharia its ground. The lesson is uncomfortable: every resource has a place where someone pays the price for it.

### Try This at Home

**A Resource Audit of Your Own House**

*⏱ duration: one weekend · 🧹 mess level: low · 👨‍👩‍👧 adult help: small (need to read the water meter and electricity bill)*

Pick one of these audits. Spend a Saturday on it. Write up what you find.

- **Water audit**: For one full day, count every place water is used in your home — wash basin, kitchen tap, flush, washing machine, mopping, plant pot. Estimate (or measure with a bucket) how many litres each one uses. Add it up. Compare to the city average of around 135 litres per person per day. Where could you cut 20% painlessly?
- **Electricity audit**: List every appliance switched on in your house right now. Note its wattage (printed on a small plate or sticker). Multiply by hours of use. Add up the day's usage. Which one device eats the most? Could it be replaced with a more efficient model?
- **Daadi's resource map**: Interview your grandmother or any elder over 65 about how their household used water, fuel and food when **they** were 13. How many buckets did they fetch? What was thrown away? What got reused? Write a one-page comparison with your own household today.

### A Question to Keep Asking All Year

> *"Where did this come from, and what will happen to it after I am done with it?"*

Ask this about the water in your bottle, the food on your plate, the phone in your pocket, the clothes on your back. The answers will surprise you — and they will change what you choose.
