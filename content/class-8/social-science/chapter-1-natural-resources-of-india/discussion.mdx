chapterRef: Chapter 1 — Natural Resources and Their Use
---

**Child:** Maa, our teacher said water is a "resource". But it falls from the sky for free. How can free things be a resource?
**Mother:** Beta, that's a sharp question. Come, sit. The rain that falls from the sky is part of **Nature** — it is there whether or not we are there to catch it. But the moment we put a bucket under the drainpipe, or pump it out from the borewell, or pipe it from the Krishna river into your school's tap — that water has become a **resource**. A resource is just Nature that we have figured out how to use.

## What Makes Something a Resource?

**Child:** So if I just pluck a mango from the tree, is the mango a resource?
**Mother:** Yes — once you eat it or sell it, it is. But think about this: the same mango at the top of a 30-foot tree that nobody can climb… is it a resource?
**Child:** Hmm. Not really, because nobody can reach it.
**Mother:** Exactly. For Nature to become a resource, three things must come together. First, we need the **technology** to reach it. Second, the cost of reaching it must be less than what we gain — that's the **economic** part. Third, our **culture** has to allow us to use it.
**Child:** Culture? What does culture have to do with mangoes?
**Mother:** Take the **sacred groves** I have told you about — the *devarakadu* near your daadi's village in Karnataka. There are huge old trees there. Wood is wood, beta. But nobody cuts those trees because the village has considered them sacred for generations. The wood is there. The technology is there. The cost would be cheap. But the culture says no. So the trees are not a resource. That is also the meaning of being human — we don't take just because we can.

## Why Is Indian Soil So Special?

**Child:** Maa, you keep telling me to wash the rice properly. Why does rice need so much water anyway?
**Mother:** That is a good link, beta. Indian rice — especially paddy — needs flooded fields. Because of that, much of India's farming happens where there are big rivers. The Ganga plain, the Krishna-Godavari delta, the Kaveri delta. The **soil** there is something special.
**Child:** Why?
**Mother:** For thousands of years, rivers from the Himalayas have brought down fine silt — broken-down rock and minerals from the mountains. That silt becomes the topsoil of north India. It is so rich that one rupee's worth of seed can give back twenty rupees' worth of grain. That kind of soil is why empires grew along these rivers — the Mauryas, the Guptas, the Mughals.
**Child:** So soil is also a resource.
**Mother:** A very precious one. And here is the thing — even soil is **renewable**, but slowly. Nature makes about one centimetre of fresh topsoil in 100 to 400 years. If we lose it faster than that — through over-ploughing, chemical overuse, or rain washing it away because there are no roots to hold it — we are stealing from the future.
**Child:** Daadi always says she used cow dung in the field.
**Mother:** That is exactly the point. Your daadi's generation understood **mulching**, **mixed cropping**, leaving fields fallow. They used dung, leaf compost, *vermicompost* before they had a word for it. The chemical fertiliser years gave us food quickly, but the soil paid a price. Sikkim is now showing that organic farming can come back without giving up yields.

## What Happens When We Use Things Faster Than Nature Can Replace Them?

**Child:** Maa, the matka water tastes better than the tap water. Why don't we just always use the matka?
**Mother:** *(smiling)* Beta, both come from the same place — the tap fills the matka. But your question has something deep in it. Where does the tap water come from?
**Child:** From the Krishna river, you said.
**Mother:** And before the dam was built, where did Hyderabad's water come from?
**Child:** Wells? Lakes?
**Mother:** Yes — the **Hussain Sagar**, the **Osman Sagar**, hundreds of step-wells across the Deccan. The city's old neighbourhoods had a tank in every fourth galli. But as the population grew, those tanks dried up or got built over. So we went deeper — borewells, 100 feet, then 200, then 600. In Punjab, where the same thing happened to grow wheat for the whole country, the borewells now go down 30 metres before they find water. The land is being **drained from below** faster than monsoons can refill it.
**Child:** So the water is finishing?
**Mother:** Eighty percent of Punjab is what the scientists call **"overexploited"**. That word means we are taking water out faster than rain can put it back. It is happening in Bengaluru too, in Chennai, in many big cities. Every renewable resource has a speed limit — if we cross it, it stops being renewable.
**Child:** Then what should we do?
**Mother:** We do what your daadi's generation did instinctively. Catch rainwater on the roof. Repair the village pond. Don't run the tap while brushing your teeth — that simple thing alone saves about ten litres a day. Multiply that by 140 crore people, beta.

## Why Are Some Resources "Common"?

**Child:** Maa, when I drop a plastic wrapper on the road, you scold me. But uncle from the next building throws his garbage on the road every morning. So why does it matter what I do?
**Mother:** That is exactly the question great thinkers have been asking for two hundred years, beta. It even has a name — the **"tragedy of the commons"**.
**Child:** What does that mean?
**Mother:** Imagine the village pond. It is shared by everyone — nobody owns it. If one family washes its buffaloes in it, the pond is fine. If every family does it every day, the pond gets dirty for everybody. Each family thinks, *"What difference does my one buffalo make?"* — and that is exactly how the pond dies.
**Child:** So the air is also "common"?
**Mother:** Yes. And the river, the forest, the climate. **Common resources** are the hardest to protect because no single person feels responsible. That is why we need rules — the **Central Pollution Control Board**, the **Forest Rights Act**, the laws on plastic. And we need community — when neighbours decide together that nobody throws garbage on this lane, it works better than any law.
**Child:** Daadi said her village had rules about when to fish in the pond.
**Mother:** Exactly! Many traditional communities had wisdom about this. They didn't fish during the breeding season. They cut firewood only from fallen branches. The marriage feast served food on **patta** — banana leaves — that the cows could eat afterwards. Nothing was wasted. That is the model — not because it is "old", but because it works.

## What Can a Class 8 Student Actually Do?

**Child:** Maa, all of this is so big — climate, oil, glaciers… I'm just one boy in Class 8. What can I really do?
**Mother:** Beta, look at me. *(takes his hands)* When daadi tells me to switch off the geyser after my bath, I sometimes feel the same way — "Maa, the whole world is using electricity, what difference does my geyser make?" But then I think — that is exactly the thinking that empties the village pond. Every person waiting for someone else to start.
**Child:** So what should I actually do?
**Mother:** Three kinds of things. First, **the daily small things** — close the tap, switch off lights, walk to the corner shop, eat what is on your plate. These look tiny but they shape your habits for life.
**Child:** And the second?
**Mother:** **Learn how things connect.** When you understand that your phone charger pulls electricity from coal at Talcher, that the cotton in your shirt drank groundwater from Punjab, that the plastic in your snack wrapper started as petroleum from Mumbai High — that knowledge changes how you choose. Asking *"where did this come from, and where will it go?"* is itself an act of stewardship.
**Child:** And third?
**Mother:** **Speak up and join in.** In your school's eco-club. In your colony's rainwater harvesting project. In conversations with friends. You are not alone — there are millions of kids your age realising the same things. The previous generation discovered the damage. Your generation will fix it. That is the work you have been given.

## What Should I Take From This?

**Child:** Maa, can you tell me in one sentence what this whole chapter is about?
**Mother:** *(thinks)* One sentence is hard. Let me try two. *Nature has given us beautiful gifts — sunlight, rivers, soil, forests, minerals — but most of them have a speed limit. Take faster than that and the gift turns into a debt.*
**Child:** And the Bhagavad Gita word teacher mentioned?
**Mother:** **Lokasangraha.** Act for the welfare of all — not just yourself, not just your family, not just today. *All* — that includes the daadi who came before, and the great-great-grandchild who will come after. Every time you switch off a tap or refuse a plastic bag, you are doing a tiny piece of *lokasangraha*. Don't ever think small things don't matter, beta. The matka fills drop by drop.
