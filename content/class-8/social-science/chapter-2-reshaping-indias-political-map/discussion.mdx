chapterRef: Chapter 2 — Reshaping India's Political Map
---

**Child:** Maa, why were there so many kingdoms in India before the British came? Why couldn't they just be one country?

**Mother:** That is a beautiful question, beta, and the honest answer is — "India" as one country is a very new idea. For most of our history, what we call India today was a huge landmass with many languages, many crops, many ways of being. And every region had people strong and proud enough to want to run their own affairs.

---

## Why So Many Kingdoms?

**Child:** But still, so many at once?

**Mother:** Think of it geographically. Between the Aravallis and the Vindhyas you have the great northern plains — flat, fertile, easy to invade. In the south you have the Deccan plateau, broken up by rivers and hills, where Vijayanagara could grow without anyone reaching it easily. Then there are the Himalayas, the Northeast hills where the Ahoms lived, the deserts of Rajasthan with Mewar tucked behind the Aravallis, the long coastlines with their own port cities. Each landscape encouraged its own kingdom.

**Child:** And the languages too?

**Mother:** Exactly. A Tamil farmer in 1400 and a Punjabi farmer in 1400 did not speak the same language, did not eat the same food, did not worship the same way. They had no reason to think of themselves as one people. Their "country" was their kingdom, their village, their region.

**Child:** So when the chapter talks about the Delhi Sultanate "expanding," it really means it was trying to rule over people who already had their own rulers.

**Mother:** Yes. And those people resisted. Sometimes for decades. Sometimes for centuries.

---

## How Did the Delhi Sultanate Rise?

**Child:** Where did the Sultanate even come from? It says these were Turkic and Afghan people — they came from outside India?

**Mother:** They came from beyond the Hindu Kush mountains — present-day Afghanistan, Central Asia, parts of what is now Turkmenistan and Uzbekistan. From around the 11th century, waves of cavalry began crossing into north India. Some came for plunder, some for territory, some to spread their faith by force. In 1192, Muhammad Ghori defeated Prithviraj Chauhan in the Second Battle of Tarain. After Ghori, his general Qutub-ud-din Aibak set himself up as ruler from Delhi in 1206. That's the start.

**Child:** Aibak — like the Qutub Minar?

**Mother:** Yes, he began the Qutub Minar. Right next to it is the Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque, which the chapter mentions was built using stones from twenty-seven destroyed Hindu and Jain temples. The Sultanate's beginning had violence in its very foundations.

**Child:** And it kept going for three hundred years?

**Mother:** With five different dynasties — Mamluk, Khilji, Tughlaq, Sayyid, Lodi — and a lot of palace murders in between. Did you notice the chapter said the average sultan ruled barely nine years? Two out of three sultans killed their predecessor.

**Child:** That sounds terrifying for the regular farmer outside Delhi.

**Mother:** Imagine paying taxes to one ruler and a year later a completely different ruler is demanding the same taxes — sometimes more. The chapter is very honest about the strain on the peasantry.

---

## Was Vijayanagara Really as Glorious as the Stories Say?

**Child:** The chapter spends a lot of pages on Vijayanagara. The Portuguese traveller says it was "as large as Rome." Was it really?

**Mother:** Have you ever been to Hampi?

**Child:** No.

**Mother:** When you stand inside what is left, you understand the description. Stone bazaars two kilometres long. Temple complexes the size of small towns. Tanks, palaces, elephant stables. The Vitthala temple has stone pillars that ring out musical notes when struck.

**Child:** That's wild.

**Mother:** Domingo Paes, the Portuguese traveller, wrote that the markets had "in great abundance everything that you wanted." This was during Krishnadevaraya's reign in the early 1500s. Vijayanagara at its peak was a global trading capital.

**Child:** Then how did all of that just — end?

**Mother:** In 1565, the five Deccan Sultanates that had broken off from the Bahmani Sultanate — Bijapur, Golconda, Berar, Ahmednagar, Bidar — set aside their rivalries and joined forces. They crushed the Vijayanagara army under Ramaraya, Krishnadevaraya's son-in-law, at the Battle of Talikota.

**Child:** Just one battle?

**Mother:** One battle. But then the victorious armies marched into the city and spent months destroying it. Houses, palaces, temples, the people inside them. By the time they were done, Hampi was the ruins you can still walk through today.

**Child:** That makes me really sad.

**Mother:** It is sad. Krishnadevaraya wrote poetry in Telugu — his epic *Amuktamalyada* tells the story of Andal, a Tamil poet-saint. The same man who funded that poetry also fielded armies. That is the strange mixture of beauty and violence in this whole period.

---

## Why Did Akbar Change?

**Child:** Maa, this part confuses me. The chapter says young Akbar massacred 30,000 civilians at Chittorgarh. Then a few pages later he is "tolerant of all faiths" and getting the Mahabharata translated into Persian. Same person?

**Mother:** Same person. And the chapter is brave to put both facts side by side.

**Child:** So what changed?

**Mother:** Several things, I think. One — Akbar realised early that he could not actually rule a Subcontinent the size of India by sword alone. There were too many proud regional rulers. So he began making marriage alliances with Rajput princesses and bringing Rajput nobles into his court. That was political.

**Child:** And the other things?

**Mother:** The other thing was personal. Akbar was illiterate but intensely curious. He invited scholars from every faith to his court — Hindu pandits, Jain monks, Muslim theologians, Jesuit priests from Goa. He listened to them debate. His chronicler Abul Fazl quotes him saying he was "overwhelmed with shame" at having once forced his faith on others. That is a man who actually changed his mind based on what he learned.

**Child:** So he abolished the jizya tax.

**Mother:** Abolished it. Promoted sulh-i-kul — peace with all. Established the house of translation at Fatehpur Sikri. Even appointed Hindu officers, although the chapter is honest that non-Muslims still rarely made up more than a third of senior posts under him.

**Child:** And then Aurangzeb undid all of it.

**Mother:** Reimposed the jizya. Banned music and dance at court. In 1669 ordered his governors to "demolish schools and temples of the infidels." Aurangzeb is the great counter-example. He pushed the Mughal Empire to its largest size but he also drained it dry — twenty-five years of wars in the Deccan — and in his last letters to his sons he wrote: "I came alone and am going away alone … I have not done well for the country and the people."

**Child:** What a sad way to end.

---

## What Did "India" Even Mean Back Then?

**Child:** Maa, I want to come back to my first question. If there was no "India" yet, what did people actually call this place?

**Mother:** The Persians had been calling it "Hind" — that's where Hindustan comes from, "land of the Hindu" meaning land beyond the Sindhu river. The Greeks called it "India" from the same root. To the rulers in Persia or Central Asia, it was one Hindustan. To the people living here, it was many homelands.

**Child:** Did anyone here think of it as one place?

**Mother:** Spiritually, yes. Pilgrims went from Rameswaram to Badrinath. The four dhams. Adi Shankaracharya in the 8th century is said to have set up monastic seats in the four corners of the Subcontinent. Sufi saints walked from Bukhara to Bengal. Traders moved their hundis across kingdoms. So there was a sense of a shared sacred geography. But politically, no — politically it was many kingdoms.

**Child:** So "India" as one country really is a modern idea.

**Mother:** It is a modern idea given old roots. Our freedom fighters made the case that a place tied together by pilgrim routes, language families, shared epics, shared trade networks deserved to be tied together politically too. That argument won. But it would have seemed strange to a farmer in 1500.

**Child:** Interesting. That makes me look at modern India differently.

---

## Why Did Some Kingdoms Last Centuries and Others Fade Quickly?

**Child:** This one really bothers me. Vijayanagara lasted from 1336 to the mid-1600s — almost three hundred years. The Sayyids lasted barely forty. What made the difference?

**Mother:** Several things. Let me list what I see.

**Child:** Go.

**Mother:** **One — geography.** The Ahoms in the Brahmaputra Valley held out against both the Sultanate and the Mughals because Assam's rivers, hills, and forests made it almost impossible to conquer. Mewar held out behind the Aravallis. Vijayanagara was protected by the rocky Deccan terrain on three sides. Geography matters.

**Child:** **Two?**

**Mother:** **Two — administration.** A kingdom doesn't survive on the king alone. It needs a working tax system, a working army, working roads. Akbar's mansabdari and Todar Mal's revenue system made the Mughal Empire much harder to overthrow than the Sultanate had been. Vijayanagara had its own organised provincial system.

**Child:** **Three?**

**Mother:** **Three — relationships with the local people.** Akbar made alliances with the Rajputs. Maharana Pratap kept the Bhils close — the Mewar emblem still has a Bhil warrior on it. The Ahoms developed the paik system that gave every able-bodied man a stake in the kingdom. The kingdoms that survived treated their own people as partners, not just as tax-payers.

**Child:** **Four?**

**Mother:** **Four — succession.** The Sultanate destroyed itself with murders every nine years. The Mughals averaged twenty-seven-year reigns up to Aurangzeb. A stable transition lets a kingdom plan beyond the next crisis.

**Child:** That's a really clear list. Thank you.

**Mother:** It is also a list that applies to modern countries. Geography, administration, relationship with the people, stable transitions. The names change. The pattern doesn't.

## What Should I Take From This?

**Child:** Maa, if I had to remember three things from this whole chapter, what should they be?

**Mother:** I would say —

**Child:** Slowly. I want to write them down.

**Mother:** **One.** The Indian Subcontinent was never one country during this period. It was a quilt of kingdoms with very different languages, faiths, and economies. The British did not break up "India" — they unified a place that had been many places for most of recorded history. Whether we are stronger as one nation or as many is one of the oldest questions of our politics.

**Child:** **Two?**

**Mother:** **Two.** History is honest. Some rulers in this chapter did terrible things — massacres, forced conversions, destroyed temples, brutal taxation. The textbook does not hide it. But the textbook also notes that we, today, of any community, are not responsible for what people did six hundred years ago. We owe the victims our memory and our respect. We do not owe their killers our hatred passed down through generations.

**Child:** That is a hard line to hold.

**Mother:** It is. But it is the only honest line.

**Child:** **And three?**

**Mother:** **Three.** Through every invasion, every conquest, every ruined city, ordinary Indians kept farming, weaving, trading, building temples and gurudwaras and mosques and forts, writing poetry, raising children. India was the wealthiest region in the world through most of this period — not because of the kings, but because of the people who did the actual work. When you walk past a 600-year-old temple or stepwell or fort, remember whose hands built it. That is the real India of this chapter.

**Child:** Maa, that is a beautiful way to end. Thank you.

**Mother:** Go and finish your worksheet, beta.
