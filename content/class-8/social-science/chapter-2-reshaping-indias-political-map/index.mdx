## A Subcontinent in Motion

Stand for a moment in front of any old fort in India — Chittorgarh in Rajasthan, Daulatabad in Maharashtra, Golconda in Telangana, the Qutub complex in Delhi. The stones are silent now, but each one was once the centre of a kingdom. Around it lived farmers, weavers, traders, soldiers, poets. Then, sometimes within a single lifetime, the king died, an army marched in, the flag changed, and the same farmer paid taxes to a new master.

Between roughly the 13th and 17th centuries, India was reshaped again and again by exactly this kind of churning. This is the period historians often call the **later medieval period**. Cavalry from beyond the Hindu Kush, regional dynasties pushing outward from their hills and river valleys, southern empires guarding their independence, tribal kingdoms holding the forested edges — all of them carved and re-carved the map of the Subcontinent. Some kingdoms survived for centuries. Others lasted barely a few decades. By 1707, when the last of the great Mughal emperors died, the political map looked nothing like it had in 1192.

This chapter follows that long churning.

---

## What "Medieval" Means Here

Indian textbooks borrow the word **medieval** from European history. In Europe, "medieval" meant the centuries between the fall of the Roman Empire and the Renaissance. For us, it is simply a label of convenience — roughly the period from the **11th to the 17th centuries**. Indian and European histories developed very differently in those centuries, so do not assume the European word carries the same meaning here.

A second small note: Persian sounds do not map neatly onto English letters. You will see **Khilji** and **Khalji**, **Mughal** and **Mughul**, **Tughlaq** and **Tughluq** in different books. They refer to the same people. We will use one spelling each and stay consistent.

## The Rise of the Delhi Sultanate

The story of the Delhi Sultanate begins with a battlefield. In **1192**, Muhammad Ghori defeated **Prithviraj Chauhan**, the Rajput ruler of parts of northwestern India, at the Second Battle of Tarain. A few years later, Ghori's general **Qutub-ud-din Aibak** declared himself ruler from Delhi. The year **1206** is conventionally taken as the founding of the **Delhi Sultanate**.

Over the next three centuries the throne at Delhi passed between **five successive dynasties of Turkic-Afghan origin**:

- **Mamluks** (the "Slave dynasty"), starting with Qutub-ud-din Aibak
- **Khiljis**, of whom Ala-ud-din Khilji was the most ambitious
- **Tughlaqs**, whose Muhammad bin Tughlaq pushed the Sultanate to its widest extent
- **Sayyids**, a short-lived dynasty after Timur's invasion
- **Lodis**, who lost Delhi to Babur in 1526

These rulers carried the title **Sultan**, which simply means "authority" or "ruler" in Arabic. The early symbol of the Sultanate's power is the **Qutub Minar** in Delhi, begun by Aibak. Beside it stands the **Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque**, built partly with stone from twenty-seven destroyed Hindu and Jain temples — a detail recorded in the mosque's own inscription, and a hint of the violence woven into the Sultanate's beginnings.

Two features defined this whole period. **First**, the Sultanate kept expanding through military campaigns that plundered cities, villages, and temples to pay for the next campaign. **Second**, the throne itself was dangerously unstable. Of every three sultans, roughly two seized power by killing the previous one. The average reign of a Delhi sultan was barely **nine years**.

### Ala-ud-din Khilji and the Mongol Threat

At the turn of the 14th century, **Ala-ud-din Khilji** marched his armies across north and central India, sacking city after city. His coins called him **Sikander Sani** — the "second Alexander." At the same time he had to repel **Mongol invasions** from the northwest. The Mongol Empire then covered most of Asia, and India was the prize they could not capture. Ala-ud-din held them off.

His slave-general **Malik Kafur** carried the campaigns deep into the south, plundering temple towns like **Srirangam, Madurai, Chidambaram**, and possibly **Rameswaram**. The wealth taken from these centres financed the Sultanate's enormous army.

### Muhammad bin Tughlaq: Big Ideas, Botched Execution

A few decades later, **Muhammad bin Tughlaq** stretched the Sultanate to its widest extent. For the first time since the **Mauryas**, most of the Subcontinent was under one ruler. The dominance did not last. Two of his decisions became famous warnings against ambition without planning.

He shifted his capital from Delhi to **Daulatabad** (then called Devagiri, near present-day Sambhaji Nagar), hoping the more central location would give him better control of the south. He forced the people of Delhi to march over **1,000 kilometres**. A few years later he shifted the capital back. Both moves caused enormous suffering and loss of life.

He also introduced **token currency** — cheap copper coins declared to have the value of silver or gold. The idea was not bad in principle; almost all of today's currency is "token." But people simply counterfeited the copper coins, trade collapsed, and the experiment had to be abandoned.

### Plunder, Taxes, and Jizya

The Sultanate's wealth came from three streams. **Plunder** from military campaigns. **Taxes** on the peasantry and on conquered regions. And the **slave trade** — captured people were either kept as bonded labour or sold into Central Asia. Many of the sultans also levied **jizya**, a tax on non-Muslim subjects. In theory it was a fee for protection and exemption from military service; in practice it pressed heavily on Hindu, Jain, and Buddhist subjects and created an incentive to convert.

The same campaigns that filled the treasury also targeted **temples**. Destruction was driven partly by greed for the gold and jewels temples held, and partly by **iconoclasm** — the religious rejection of "idol" worship.

### Timur's Invasion

In **1398**, just when the Sultanate seemed to be settling down, a new horror arrived. **Timur**, a Turkic-Mongol conqueror from Central Asia, swept into northwest India and sacked Delhi. He wrote frankly in his memoirs that his purpose was war against "infidels" and plunder of their wealth. Tens of thousands were killed or enslaved; the city was left in ruins. Timur withdrew within months, leaving behind chaos. From this collapse emerged the **Sayyids** and then the **Lodis** — the last and weakest sultans of Delhi.

## Resistance to the Delhi Sultanate

The Sultanate's expansion was never uncontested. Across India, regional kingdoms either defeated its armies, wore them down, or simply outlasted them.

### The Eastern Gangas of Kalinga

In the east, the **Eastern Ganga kingdom** of Kalinga — covering present-day **Odisha** and parts of Bengal and Andhra Pradesh — never fell to Delhi. Its mid-13th-century ruler **Narasimhadeva I** beat back several Sultanate raids and even defeated the Sultanate's governor of Bengal. To mark these victories he commissioned one of the great architectural marvels of India: the **Sun Temple at Konark**.

### The Musunuri Nayakas

Around 1330–1336, during the Tughlaq period, **Telugu chieftains** known as the **Musunuri Nayakas** rallied **over 75 regional leaders** into a confederacy. Their joint army expelled Tughlaq's forces from **Warangal** (in present-day Telangana). Bringing seventy-five proud chieftains under one banner was no small feat — and it shows the south was prepared to organise against Delhi.

### The Hoysalas

The **Hoysalas** ruled much of present-day **Karnataka**. They held off several Khilji and Tughlaq attacks before the kingdom weakened from internal conflict and was absorbed into the rising **Vijayanagara Empire** in the mid-14th century. Their temples at **Belur** and **Halebidu** remain masterpieces of stone carving.

### Regional Sultanates

The Delhi Sultanate's troubles were not only from Hindu kingdoms. Several of its own governors broke away and founded **independent regional Sultanates** — the **Bahmani Sultanate** in the Deccan (founded 1347), and powerful Sultanates in **Gujarat, Bengal, Malwa**, and **Jaunpur**. The political map became a quilt of alliances, betrayals, and constant warfare.

### Rana Kumbha of Mewar

In Rajasthan, the **Mewar kingdom** re-established itself in **1326** and grew into a stronghold of Rajput resistance. In the 15th century, **Rana Kumbha** held off both the Delhi Sultanate and the regional sultanates of Gujarat and Malwa. He built the famous **Kumbhalgarh Fort** in the Aravalli hills, whose **36-kilometre-long wall** is one of the longest continuous walls in the world. Forts in this period were typically built on hills, deep inside forests, or beside difficult river crossings — for the same reasons castles were built on European cliffs.

## The Vijayanagara Empire

While Delhi's grip weakened, a new power rose in the south. In the **14th century**, two brothers — **Harihara** and **Bukka** — had served as governors under Muhammad bin Tughlaq. They broke away, declared independence, and founded the kingdom that grew into the **Vijayanagara Empire** in **1336**.

Popular folklore says the brothers chose the site of their new capital after seeing a hare turn around and chase a pack of hounds at **Hampi** (in present-day Karnataka). Their guru **Vidyaranya** read the omen as a sign of courage and resilience and told them to build their capital there. Whatever the truth of the legend, the Vijayanagara rulers held their ground against the Bahmani Sultanate to the north, the **Gajapati kingdom** of Odisha to the east, and the Deccan Sultanates that emerged when the Bahmanis fragmented.

### Five Deccan Sultanates

By the late 15th century, the Bahmani Sultanate had broken into **five Deccan Sultanates** — **Bijapur, Golconda, Berar, Ahmednagar, and Bidar**. Each was ruled by a former governor (a **tarafdar**) who simply declared autonomy. Vijayanagara fought constantly against the first two.

### Krishnadevaraya

The empire reached its peak under **Krishnadevaraya** in the early 16th century. He was a soldier-king who expanded territory and a scholar-king who composed poetry. He patronised writers in **Sanskrit, Telugu, and Kannada**. His own Telugu epic, **Amuktamalyada**, tells the story of the Tamil poet-saint Andal and contains a section called **Rajaniti** ("royal policy") in which he laid out his ideas of good governance. He made huge grants to temples, including the famous **Tirupati** temple in Andhra Pradesh and the **Vitthala temple** at Hampi, whose intricately carved **musical pillars** still ring out different notes when struck.

Portuguese travellers like **Domingo Paes** visited Vijayanagara and described its grandeur. Paes wrote that the city seemed to him "as large as Rome, and very beautiful to the sight … the best provided city in the world." The Vijayanagara kings welcomed the Portuguese in particular — they came to sell **horses**, and the king was anxious to keep those horses out of rival hands.

### The Battle of Talikota

Krishnadevaraya died of illness in **1529**. In **1565**, the five Deccan Sultanates set aside their own rivalries, formed a coalition, and crushed the Vijayanagara forces under Krishnadevaraya's son-in-law **Ramaraya** at the **Battle of Talikota**. The victors marched into the city and spent months sacking it. Houses, shops, palaces, and most of its temples were destroyed; much of the civilian population was massacred. Hampi was left in ruins. The empire fragmented into smaller chiefdoms run by **Nayakas** — former military governors — and finally ended in the mid-17th century.

## The Mughals

In **1526**, while the Lodis still nominally held Delhi, a Turkic-Mongol prince named **Babur** marched into north India. He was a descendant of both Timur and Chinggis Khan. Driven out of Samarkand in Central Asia, he turned south, and at **the First Battle of Panipat** he defeated **Ibrahim Lodi**. His army was smaller but it had **gunpowder artillery and matchlock guns** — weapons just then arriving in Indian warfare. The Sultanate ended that day. The **Mughal Empire** began.

### Babur's Two Faces

Babur left an extraordinary autobiography, the **Baburnama**. In it he comes across as a cultured man with a sharp eye for architecture, poetry, fruit trees, and birds — he lists Indian birds in careful detail. But he was also a brutal conqueror who slaughtered entire city populations and took pride in building **"towers of skulls"** from their dead. He found India a "country of few charms" but admitted its **wealth and abundance of artisans** were beyond compare. He decided to stay.

### Humayun, Sher Shah Suri, and Hemu

Babur died in **1530**. His son **Humayun** struggled to hold the empire. **Sher Shah Suri**, an Afghan chieftain, drove Humayun out and built the **Sur Empire**. Sher Shah's reign was short but he introduced lasting reforms — the rupiya silver coin, a vast road network including the **Grand Trunk Road**, and a structured revenue system. Humayun eventually reclaimed Delhi.

Just before Humayun's return, a Hindu general named **Hemu** (or Hemchandra Vikramaditya) — chief minister to one of the last Suri rulers — actually captured Delhi and ruled briefly under his own royal name. At the **Second Battle of Panipat in 1556**, Hemu was struck in the eye by an arrow, captured, and beheaded by Babur's young grandson, **Akbar**.

### Akbar

Akbar was declared emperor at the age of **13** when his father Humayun died suddenly. His long reign of nearly **50 years (1556–1605)** is the period when the Mughals truly become a Subcontinental power. Akbar was a mix of contradictions.

In his early years he was as ruthless as any of his predecessors. At the siege of **Chittorgarh** in Rajasthan, after months of resistance, his army broke through and Akbar ordered the **massacre of around 30,000 civilians**. Hundreds of Rajput women, led by their queens, committed **jauhar** — leaping into mass fires to avoid capture. Akbar wrote that he had erased "the signs of infidelity" from those places and destroyed temples there.

Over time Akbar shifted. He saw that conquest alone could not hold an empire as diverse as India. He:

- Entered **marriage alliances** with Rajput princesses
- Welcomed **Rajput and regional leaders** into his court
- **Abolished the jizya** tax on non-Muslims
- Promoted **sulh-i-kul** — "peace with all" or tolerance of all faiths
- Established a **house of translation** at his city **Fatehpur Sikri** where the **Mahabharata, Ramayana, Bhagavad Gita** and **Panchatantra** were translated into Persian, the last with 176 miniature paintings

Akbar himself was illiterate but intensely curious. His biographer **Abul Fazl** records him as saying that as he grew in knowledge he was "overwhelmed with shame" at having once forced his faith on others, and asked, "what constancy is to be expected from proselytes on compulsion?"

### Jahangir, Shah Jahan, Aurangzeb

Akbar's son **Jahangir** shared his father's love of art and pushed into the Deccan. **Shah Jahan** is remembered above all as the builder of the **Taj Mahal** at Agra, along with the **Red Forts** of Delhi and Agra and **Humayun's tomb**. The Mughal court was now the patron of an extraordinary flowering of miniature painting, calligraphy, classical music, and Indo-Persian architecture.

Shah Jahan fell ill in **1657** and a brutal succession followed. His eldest son **Dara Shikoh** — a scholar who himself translated the **Upanishads** into Persian — was defeated, executed, and his severed head presented to his ailing father. The victor was Dara's younger brother **Aurangzeb**, who imprisoned Shah Jahan in Agra Fort, crowned himself emperor in **1658**, took the title **Alamgir** ("conqueror of the world"), and ruled for almost **49 years**.

Under Aurangzeb the Mughal Empire reached its **greatest territorial extent** but began to crack. He spent the last **25 years of his life fighting wars in the Deccan**, draining the treasury and straining the administration. Deeply religious in the Sunni tradition, he reimposed the **jizya** and a pilgrimage tax on Hindus, banned music and dance at court, and in **1669** ordered governors "to demolish schools and temples of the infidels." Temples at **Banaras, Mathura, Somnath**, Jain temples, Sikh gurdwaras, and even Sufi shrines and Parsi Zoroastrians were targeted. In his last letters to his sons he wrote, "I came alone and am going away alone … I have not done well for the country and the people."

> **A note on history's darker chapters.** Some of the rulers in this chapter committed terrible deeds. The victims of those deeds deserve our respect and remembrance. But none of us today — of any community — bear responsibility for the actions of individuals hundreds of years ago.

## Resistance to the Mughals

The Mughals were powerful but never unopposed. The empire was always being chipped at by rebellions large and small.

### Peasant and Tribal Rebellions

In the 17th century the **Jat peasantry** of present-day western Uttar Pradesh, Haryana, and eastern Rajasthan killed a cruel Mughal officer and fought a pitched battle of **20,000 men** against the imperial army. They lost their leader but the revolt was a sign of things to come. Tribal groups — the **Bhils, Gonds, Santhals, Kochs** — also resisted Mughal attempts to annex their forests or collect taxes from them. Those in the most remote hills and forests often kept their independence.

### Rani Durgavati

The Garha kingdom — one of the **Gond kingdoms** of central India — was ruled in the mid-16th century by **Rani Durgavati**. She kept an army of 20,000 soldiers and 1,000 elephants. When Akbar's general attacked her kingdom in **1564**, she rode out outnumbered and outgunned, was wounded on the battlefield, and took her own life to avoid capture. She was only forty. In Madhya Pradesh she is still revered as a heroic queen.

### The Rajputs

Because of their location in northwest India and the warrior traditions they inherited from earlier dynasties like the **Pratiharas**, the **Rajputs** were among the most stubborn opponents of any invader. Two major clans rose in the **Mewar** and **Marwar** regions of Rajasthan after the Khiljis.

- **Rana Sanga** unified several Rajput clans in the early 16th century and won many battles against the sultans, but lost to Babur at the **Battle of Khanwa** in 1527.
- **Maharana Pratap** of Mewar refused to accept Mughal overlordship. At the **Battle of Haldighati in 1576**, the Mughals had the upper hand, but Pratap escaped into the **Aravalli hills** and for years waged **guerrilla warfare** against them. He was strongly supported by the **Bhils**, who joined his troops as archers and used their knowledge of the terrain. The Mewar emblem still carries a Bhil warrior on the left in their honour.
- Under Aurangzeb, **Durga Das Rathore** of Marwar fought to protect the independence of Jodhpur.

Mughal authority in Rajasthan thus always remained limited.

### The Ahoms

In the 13th century, the **Ahom** people migrated from present-day Myanmar into the **Brahmaputra Valley** and built a kingdom there. They held out against both the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughals. Their unique **paik system** required every able-bodied man to serve the state through labour or military duty in exchange for land — letting the king raise a large army without keeping a permanent one.

In **1671**, at the **Battle of Saraighat** on the Brahmaputra River near present-day **Guwahati**, the Ahom commander **Lachit Borphukan** led 10,000 men to victory against a Mughal army of 30,000. Aurangzeb's general Ram Singh wrote in admiration: "Every Assamese soldier is expert in rowing boats, in shooting arrows, in digging trenches, and in wielding guns and cannons." Assam's rivers, hills, and forests were a fortress no Mughal army could really conquer.

### The Sikhs

In 15th-century **Punjab**, **Guru Nanak** spread the message of one God (**Ik Onkar**), equality, compassion, and truthful living. His followers became known as **Sikhs**. The movement was originally purely spiritual. But persecution under some Mughal emperors forced a change. **Jahangir** had **Guru Arjan** tortured to death. Guru Arjan's son **Guru Hargobind** introduced martial training and raised a Sikh army. In **1675**, **Guru Tegh Bahadur** stood up to defend a group of Kashmiri Pandits facing forced conversion. Aurangzeb had him publicly beheaded at **Chandni Chowk** in Delhi — the spot now marked by **Gurudwara Sis Ganj Sahib**, saluted each Republic Day by the Sikh Regiment. In response, his son **Guru Gobind Singh** — the tenth and last Guru — formed the **Khalsa** in **1699**, a martial brotherhood committed to justice and the defence of faith.

As the Mughal Empire decayed, several Sikh confederacies in Punjab were eventually unified by **Maharaja Ranjit Singh** at the turn of the 19th century into a **Sikh Empire** that stretched across the northwest, including parts of Kashmir, and held off both the remaining Mughals and the early British.

## How These Empires Were Administered

A kingdom is more than a flag. To survive, it has to collect taxes, dispense justice, build roads, and answer to its people. Two administrative systems are worth noting from this period.

### The Sultanate's Iqta System

The Delhi Sultanate revolved around the **Sultan**, who held absolute political and military authority. Below him sat a council of ministers in charge of various departments. The empire was run through the **iqta system** — territories assigned to nobles (**iqtadars**) who collected taxes locally and forwarded the surplus to the Sultan's treasury after keeping enough to maintain their soldiers. Crucially, these assignments were **not hereditary** — the Sultan could move iqtadars around or replace them, preventing them from becoming local kings. Taxes on trade were levied at every stage, but the heaviest burden fell on the **peasantry**, sometimes brutally.

### The Mughal Mansabdari System

Akbar reorganised the system to make it more efficient. He split the empire into **twelve subahs** (provinces), each with its own set of officials:

- **Diwan** — finance
- **Mir Bakhshi** — military
- **Khan-i-Saman** — public works, trade, industry, agriculture, royal household
- **Sadr** — justice, religious and educational affairs

He also instituted the **mansabdari system** described in Abul Fazl's **Ain-i-Akbari**. Each **mansabdar** held a **mansab** (rank) and was required to maintain a precise number of soldiers, horses, elephants, and camels for the emperor's use. The state inspected them regularly. In exchange they were assigned land grants (**jagirs**) — which is why they were also called **jagirdars**. The brilliance of the system was that the empire never had to keep a huge standing army of its own; the mansabdars supplied troops on demand.

At the village level, **traditional self-governance** — village panchayats, jatis, shrenis — continued largely as before.

**Todar Mal**, Akbar's finance minister, ran a careful land survey across the empire, set prices for each crop, and built a revenue system that was efficient and (relatively) fair.

But the system had limits. Non-Muslims rarely held more than a **third** of senior posts under Akbar, and the share was usually less. Even among Muslim officers, those of foreign origin were favoured over those of Indian origin.

## How People Lived

For all the dynastic violence above, the daily life of most Indians was about **fields, looms, markets, and temples**. The Subcontinent remained one of the **wealthiest regions in the world** through this entire period — a fact noticed by every traveller who came from Arabia, Persia, or Europe.

### Agriculture

Agriculture was the backbone of the economy. Rulers typically took **one-fifth** of the crop as land revenue, though some sultans pushed this as high as **one-half**. New irrigation works — **Persian wheels**, tanks, canals — spread, and farmers grew an enormous range of food crops (rice, wheat, barley, pulses, sugarcane, spices) and cash crops (cotton, silk, jute, indigo, timber). Famines did strike, and relief depended on whether the local ruler was kind or callous.

### Crafts, Trade, and Ports

Indian textiles — cottons from Gujarat and Bengal, silks from the south — were exported all over the known world. Other crafts produced weapons, utensils, jewellery, and ornaments. **Ship-building** flourished. Goods left India through ports like **Calicut, Mangalore, Surat, Masulipatnam**, and **Hooghly**, and India imported much less than it exported — mainly horses, silk, metals, and luxury goods. Merchant communities from Arabia, Persia, and Central Asia settled in Indian ports.

### The Hundi System

To move money safely across an India full of soldiers and bandits, merchants invented the **hundi** — a written instruction for payment that could be redeemed across cities and even across political borders. It was an early form of banking, run entirely by traders without the involvement of any sultan or emperor. Communities like the **Marwaris** became experts at operating these credit networks across every regime.

### Temples as Economies

Temples in this period were not only places of worship and learning but also **engines of the regional economy**. Kings and merchants donated land and wealth (**dana**) to temple deities; temple managers used those endowments to build irrigation tanks, run **dharmashalas** and **chhatrams** for pilgrims, finance trade, and even lend money. Markets sprang up around major temples.

### Hard Times Below the Surface

By the late 1600s, however, the economic picture darkened. After taxes and intermediaries, peasants were often left with very little. Many lost their land and slipped into **bonded labour**. Craftspeople and labourers also faced harsh conditions. India was still rich — but the wealth was concentrated in the hands of rulers, courtiers, high officials, and the merchant class.

Despite frequent warfare and occasional clashes over desecrated sacred sites, people of different faiths and communities mostly lived **side by side**, economically dependent on each other. Many art forms, musical traditions, languages, and architectural styles blended Indian and foreign elements during this period, producing a shared heritage — much of which is still visible in our cities today.

## What "Reshaping" Really Meant

If you compare a map of India around 1300 with a map around 1700, hardly any boundary line is the same. The Delhi Sultanate is gone. The Vijayanagara Empire is gone. The Bahmanis are gone. New empires — Mughal, Maratha (coming next chapter), Sikh — have risen and begun to fade. New cities have grown; old ones have been abandoned.

Through all of it, India endured. The political map kept being reshaped, but the people, languages, faiths, crafts, and traditions adapted, blended, and renewed themselves. It is a story of immense hardship — but also of remarkable **resilience**.

## Apply — Stories From Around Us

### Story 1 — The Old Name of My City

Many Indian cities have older names that point straight back to this period. **Bengaluru** was a small village said to have been named after "boiled beans" given to a Hoysala king who got lost in the forest. **Sambhaji Nagar** was once **Daulatabad**, the city Muhammad bin Tughlaq made his short-lived capital. **Hampi** was Vijayanagara. **Vijayapura** was Bijapur. Ask your parents or grandparents what your own town or city was called a few centuries ago — you may be surprised whose flag flew over it.

### Story 2 — The Fort Down the Road

There is almost certainly a fort within driving distance of you, even if you live in a city. Kumbhalgarh in Rajasthan. Chittorgarh. Golconda outside Hyderabad. Bidar in north Karnataka. Daulatabad near Sambhaji Nagar. The Red Fort in Delhi. Each was the headquarters of a dynasty in this chapter. Standing on its walls and looking at the surrounding country tells you something a textbook cannot — why that ruler chose that hill.

### Story 3 — The Word Your Grandmother Uses

Open your ear to the words your daadi or naani uses. Persian and Turkic words flowed into Indian languages during this period — **kursi** (chair), **kitab** (book), **dukan** (shop), **bazaar** (market), **kamiz** (shirt), **roz** (daily). Tamil, Kannada, Telugu, Marathi, Bangla, Punjabi — each carries layers of words inherited from this churning of empires. A regional language is itself a record of who passed through.

### DIY — A Local History Hunt

Pick **one** of these. Spend an afternoon on it. (1) **Visit a local monument** — fort, mosque, temple, gurudwara, or tomb — that is older than 300 years. Note who built it and when. (2) **Find out which dynasty controlled your district 700 years ago** (around 1325) and who controlled it 400 years ago (around 1625). A library reference book, your school library, or a careful online search will tell you. (3) **Interview your daadi or naani** about a folk tale or song tied to a king or queen of your region — Maharana Pratap, Krishnadevaraya, Rani Durgavati, Lachit Borphukan, Guru Tegh Bahadur, or one local to you. Record the story in your own words.

**Tag your work:** `#LocalHistoryHunt`

### Show What You Found

Bring **one** thing to class. A sketch of the monument. A printed map of your district under two different rulers. A short written version of the folk story your daadi told you. Anything tangible. Be ready to say in one sentence why this changed what you thought you knew.

### A Closing Question to Keep Asking

When you walk past an old fort, an old mosque, an old temple, or even just an old street name, ask yourself: **Whose decisions, made hundreds of years ago, still shape this place — and which of those decisions did anyone actually ask the people here to agree to?**
