## How a Trading Company Conquered a Subcontinent

In 1600, a group of London merchants received a royal charter from Queen Elizabeth I to trade with the East. They called themselves the **English East India Company**. They came to India looking for spices and cloth. Within 157 years they had won a battle that handed them an empire. Within 250 years they ruled almost the entire Subcontinent. And by the time the British finally left in 1947, India — once the richest land on earth — had become one of the poorest.

This is the story of how that happened. It is not a comfortable story. But it explains why India today is what it is, what we lost, what we kept, and why **1947** is the most important date in the modern history of our country.

The American historian Will Durant called what the British took from India "**stolen wealth**." The British MP Dadabhai Naoroji called it "**un-British rule**." Whatever you call it, what happened between **1757 and 1947** is the largest sustained transfer of wealth from one country to another in human history. By one recent estimate, the total drain came to about **45 trillion US dollars** in today's value — roughly 13 times Britain's entire GDP in 2023.

How could one small island nation do this to a Subcontinent of hundreds of millions of people? Let us begin.

## What Is Colonialism?

**Colonialism** is the practice in which one country takes control of another region — settling it, ruling it, extracting its wealth, and forcing its political, economic, and cultural systems onto the conquered people.

Colonialism is not unique to modern times. Empires throughout history have done it. But what we usually mean by "the **Age of Colonialism**" is the period from the **15th century** onward, when European powers — **Spain, Portugal, Britain, France, and the Netherlands** — sailed out across the world and conquered large parts of Africa, Asia, the Americas, Australia, and the Pacific.

Why did they go? Several reasons mixed together:
- **Wealth** — access to gold, spices, raw materials, and new markets
- **Competition** between European powers for territory and prestige
- **Conversion** — many missionaries believed they had a duty to spread Christianity
- **Scientific curiosity** — a desire to map the world and catalogue its plants and animals

Colonisers usually claimed they had a **"civilising mission"** — that they were bringing progress, education, and morality to "savage" or "primitive" peoples. The reality was almost always the opposite. The people who were colonised lost their freedom, their wealth, their traditional ways of life, and often their lives. The benefits flowed mostly one way — to the colonisers.

India was a special case. Because India was already, when the Europeans arrived, one of the richest and most cultured civilisations on the planet.

## India Before the Europeans

Two thousand years ago, India was trading with the Greeks and the Romans. Indian spices, cotton, ivory, gems, sandalwood, teak, and **wootz steel** were prized across the Mediterranean. India and China together produced nearly **half of the world's GDP** for most of the past two thousand years. Indian textiles — fine **muslin** so light it could pass through a ring, richly embossed silks, indigo-dyed cottons — were in demand from Cairo to Lisbon to Beijing.

When the first European travellers reached India in the 16th century, they did not describe a backward land. They described India as **"flourishing"** — with sophisticated agriculture, vast trading networks, fine manufactures, and a complex political order. India's wealth was exactly what attracted them.

This is the most important fact to hold in your mind as you read this chapter: **the Europeans did not bring prosperity to India. They came because India was prosperous, and they took the prosperity away.**

## The Portuguese Arrive

In May **1498**, the Portuguese navigator **Vasco da Gama** reached **Kappad** near Kozhikode (Calicut) in Kerala. He was welcomed politely by the local ruler, the Zamorin. But da Gama's intentions were not peaceful. On his second voyage, he seized, tortured, and killed Indian merchants and bombarded Calicut from the sea.

The Portuguese captured **Goa in 1510** and made it the capital of their Indian colony. They controlled trading posts along the Malabar and Coromandel coasts. They invented a system called **cartaz** — a pass that all ships in the Arabian Sea had to buy from them. Any ship without a cartaz was seized. For nearly a century, they monopolised the spice trade between India and Europe.

Alongside this commercial dominance, the Portuguese practised brutal religious persecution. In **1560**, they established the **Goa Inquisition** — a tribunal that tortured and executed Hindus, Muslims, Jews, and Christian converts suspected of practising their original faiths. Forced conversions and temple destruction continued for over **250 years**. The Goa Inquisition was finally abolished only in **1812**.

But Indian rulers resisted. **Rani Abbakka I of Ullal** (in present-day Karnataka) repeatedly turned back Portuguese attacks in the 16th century. Her successor Rani Abbakka II famously used fireballs made from coconut shells to set Portuguese ships ablaze. Their stories are still told today in the **Yakshagana** dance-drama of coastal Karnataka.

## The Dutch and the French

The **Dutch** arrived in the early 17th century. They focused on commerce — particularly on the spice trade — through the **Dutch East India Company**. They held trading posts at Surat, Bharuch, Kochi, Nagapattinam, and Masulipatnam. But the Dutch story in India is short. In **1741**, at the **Battle of Colachel**, **King Marthanda Varma of Travancore** decisively defeated the Dutch on land and at sea. It was one of the rare instances of an Asian power defeating a European colonial force, and it ended Dutch ambitions in India.

The **French** arrived later. They established trading posts at **Surat in 1668** and **Pondicherry in 1674**. Under **Dupleix**, Governor-General from 1742 to 1754, the French developed two strategies that the British would later perfect:
- Training Indian soldiers in European military techniques — these were called **sepoys**
- Ruling indirectly through **puppet Indian rulers** installed during succession disputes

But the French were checked during the **Carnatic Wars (1746–1763)**, a series of conflicts with the British. Despite Dupleix's early successes, the French eventually lost out, and their Indian presence was reduced to Pondicherry and a few small enclaves.

The British were left as the most powerful European force in India.

## The British Strategy: Divide and Rule

The English East India Company was unlike the others in one crucial way — it had been granted by **Queen Elizabeth I** the right to **raise a private army**. A trading company with an army is not really a trading company. It is an empire in waiting.

Through the 17th century, the Company set up trading posts at **Surat, Madras (Chennai), Bombay (Mumbai), and Calcutta (Kolkata)**. Indian rulers welcomed foreign trade — they had welcomed it for centuries — and saw no danger. This was a mistake.

The Company's officers played a clever game. They:
- Cultivated **alliances** with local rulers, offering military help against rivals
- Inserted themselves into **succession disputes** and emerged as kingmakers
- Identified and exploited **religious and regional divisions** in Indian society
- Played one Indian power off against another

This is the strategy known as **"divide and rule."** The British did not invent every fault line in Indian society. But they widened existing cracks until the whole structure collapsed.

## 1757: The Battle of Plassey

The turning point came at a place called **Palashi** — which the British spelt **Plassey** — about 150 kilometres north of present-day Kolkata.

The **Nawab of Bengal**, **Siraj-ud-Daulah**, had quarrelled with the Company. The Company official **Robert Clive** identified disgruntled nobles at the Nawab's court — and conspired with **Mir Jafar**, the Nawab's military commander. Clive promised Mir Jafar that if he stood aside during the coming battle, the British would make him the next Nawab.

On **23 June 1757**, the battle took place. Mir Jafar's forces — the majority of the Nawab's army — did not fight. The Company's smaller army won. Siraj-ud-Daulah was killed soon after. Mir Jafar became the puppet Nawab. The British became the real rulers of Bengal.

Even today, in many Indian languages, **"Mir Jafar"** means **"traitor."**

Plassey is the date from which everything else flowed. After Plassey, the Company secured the right to **collect revenue** in **Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha** — three of the richest regions in India. They began squeezing wealth out of the land. The American historian Brooks Adams later wrote that the **British Industrial Revolution itself** began around 1760 — funded, he argued, by the **"plunder of Bengal."**

## The Doctrine of Lapse and Subsidiary Alliances

After Plassey, the Company expanded territory using two infamous instruments.

The **Doctrine of Lapse**, applied most ruthlessly by **Lord Dalhousie** in the 1840s and 1850s, declared that any princely state whose ruler died **without a natural male heir** would be annexed by the Company. This deliberately ignored the long-standing Hindu tradition of **adoption** as a legitimate way of continuing a royal line. Through the Doctrine of Lapse, the Company swallowed states like **Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur, and Awadh** in quick succession.

The **Subsidiary Alliance** system, introduced by Lord Wellesley, was even more cunning. A princely state would agree to:
- Accept a British **"Resident"** at its court
- Maintain British troops within its borders **at its own expense**
- Conduct its foreign affairs only through the British

In return, the British promised "protection." On paper, the prince remained sovereign. In practice, he had become a tax collector for the Company. The ruler of **Hyderabad** signed the first such alliance in **1798**. Many others followed. The British called it **"an empire on the cheap."** A more honest name would be **"an empire where Indians pay for their own subjugation."**

At the time of Independence in 1947, there were **over 500 princely states**, covering roughly **40 per cent** of the Subcontinent.

## Famine: From Paradise to Hell

Robert Clive had described Bengal as **"the paradise of the earth."** Within a few years of Company rule, paradise had turned into a graveyard.

The Company set its **land revenue targets so high** that farmers had to pay heavy cash taxes whether the harvest was good or bad. In **1769**, the monsoon failed. By **1770**, Bengal was starving. The Company did not lower its taxes. It **raised** them.

The result was the **Great Bengal Famine of 1770–72**, which killed an estimated **10 million people** — roughly **one-third** of Bengal's population.

A British official, **W.W. Hunter**, wrote a century later:

> "All through the stifling summer of 1770 the people went on dying. The husbandmen sold their cattle; they sold their implements of agriculture; they devoured their seed-grain; they sold their sons and daughters, till at length no buyer of children could be found; they ate the leaves of trees and the grass of the field. Day and night a torrent of famished and disease-stricken wretches poured into the great cities."

This was not the last. Famines recurred throughout British rule. The **Great Famine of 1876–78** killed up to **8 million** people, mostly across the Deccan. Even at the height of that famine, **Viceroy Lord Lytton** ordered that the government must **not interfere** in food prices — and threw a week-long imperial **durbar in Delhi** feeding 68,000 officials and maharajas, while peasants starved.

By the most reliable estimates, the total number of Indians who died in famines under British rule lies between **50 million and 100 million** — comparable to the death toll of the Second World War. Some, including the **1943 Bengal Famine**, came as late as the final years of the Raj.

Famines had occurred in India before. They had never reached this scale. During the colonial era, India — and especially rural India — **sank into a deep poverty from which it has not fully recovered to this day.**

## The Drain of Wealth

In **1901**, the Parsi statesman **Dadabhai Naoroji** — the first Indian elected to the British Parliament — published a book called **Poverty and Un-British Rule in India**. In it, using the British government's own statistics, he showed that **billions of pounds** of wealth had been drained from India to Britain over the previous century and a half. He called this the **"Drain Theory."**

The Bengali historian **Romesh Chunder Dutt** made similar calculations in his **Economic History of India**. The American historian Brooks Adams and the British journalist William Digby agreed.

A more recent estimate by the economist **Utsa Patnaik** puts the total wealth drained from India between **1765 and 1938** at about **45 trillion US dollars** in today's value — about **13 times** Britain's entire GDP in 2023.

How did the British extract so much? Several ways:
- **Land revenue** taxes were set crushingly high
- Indians were forced to **pay for British wars** — including wars fought in Burma, Afghanistan, and even Europe
- Indians were forced to **pay for the building of the railways and the telegraph**, even though these were designed mostly for British use
- The **trade pattern** was rigged so that India became a buyer of British manufactures and a seller of cheap raw materials

Had this wealth remained in India and been invested in Indian factories, schools, and farms — Naoroji and Dutt argued — India would have been an entirely different country at Independence.

## The Killing of Indian Industry

Before the 18th century, India was the **largest manufacturer of cotton textiles in the world**. Indian muslins, calicoes, chintzes, and silks were celebrated across continents. Indian iron and steel were prized. Skilled weavers, dyers, smiths, and artisans formed the backbone of countless village economies.

The British changed this through trade policy.
- **Heavy duties** were placed on Indian textiles entering Britain — sometimes 70–80 per cent
- **Low or zero duties** were placed on British textiles entering India
- The British controlled the **sea trade** and the **exchange rates**

Indian weavers could no longer sell abroad. They could no longer compete at home, even against inferior British cloth, because the rules were rigged against them. By the mid-19th century, the great weaving towns of Bengal — Dhaka, Murshidabad, Santipur — were ruined. The Governor-General **William Bentinck** himself observed in 1834:

> **"The bones of the cotton weavers are bleaching the plains of India."**

This was not industrialisation. It was **deindustrialisation** — the deliberate destruction of an existing industry. The same fate fell on India's makers of iron, steel, paper, glass, and dozens of other crafts.

In **1700**, India contributed about **25 per cent** of the world's GDP. By **1947**, that share had collapsed to **less than 5 per cent**. In less than two centuries, one of the richest lands on earth had become one of the poorest.

## Macaulay's Minute: Creating "Brown Englishmen"

The British attack on India was not only economic. It was also cultural.

In **1835**, the British politician **Thomas B. Macaulay** wrote a document called the **"Minute on Indian Education."** Macaulay admitted he knew neither Sanskrit nor Arabic. But he was confident anyway:

> "I have never found one among them who could deny that **a single shelf of a good European library was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia**."

The aim of education in India, he argued, should be to create a class of Indians **"Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect."**

Before Macaulay, India had **hundreds of thousands of village schools** — **pathshalas, madrasas, viharas** — teaching reading, writing, mathematics, and traditional knowledge. British surveys in Bengal and Bihar alone counted over **100,000** such schools. After Macaulay, English-medium schools were promoted, traditional schools were starved of support, and gradually they disappeared.

English became the language of prestige, government, courts, and higher jobs. A new class arose — English-educated Indians, often cut off from their own cultural traditions, and often resented by the wider population. The damage caused by this division between **English-educated elites and the masses** is something we still live with today.

## Railways, Telegraph, and the Myth of the "Colonial Gift"

Some say the British "gave" India the railways, the telegraph, the postal system, and modern administration. Let us look honestly at this claim.

The railways were impressive. They did connect distant parts of India and stitched the country together as never before. But:
- They were **designed** primarily to move **raw materials from the interior to the ports**, and **British manufactures from the ports to the interior**
- They were **routed** to ignore existing Indian trade patterns
- They were **paid for** by Indian taxpayers, not by British investors
- Their secondary purpose was **military** — to move British troops quickly to put down rebellions

The same was true of the telegraph, the harbours, and most colonial infrastructure. **Indians funded their own subjugation.** The British charged India for the privilege of being ruled.

## The Vellore Mutiny and Tribal Uprisings

Long before 1857, Indians resisted. The first major sepoy revolt came at **Vellore in 1806**, when the British introduced new uniform rules that forbade Hindu sepoys from wearing religious marks and required Muslim sepoys to shave their beards. The sepoys seized the Vellore fort and killed many British officers. The British crushed the revolt — and executed hundreds.

Then came the **Sannyasi-Fakir Rebellion** in Bengal, beginning after the 1770 famine, in which wandering Hindu and Muslim ascetics attacked Company treasuries. **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay** based his famous novel **Anandamath** on this rebellion — and in it appeared the song **"Vande Mataram,"** which would later become India's national song.

Tribal communities resisted even more fiercely. The **Kol Uprising** of 1831–32 in Chota Nagpur, the **Khasi Uprising** of 1829–33 in present-day Meghalaya, and above all the **Santhal Rebellion of 1855–56** led by the brothers **Sidhu and Kanhu Murmu**. The Santhals declared their own government and vowed to **"fight to the last drop of blood."** The British burned their villages and killed thousands. But the spirit of these uprisings did not die.

Peasants too rebelled. The **Indigo Revolt of 1859–62** in Bengal saw farmers refuse to grow indigo — which European planters had forced them to grow at starvation wages — until the British were forced to restrict the worst abuses.

All these uprisings led, in 1857, to one enormous explosion.

## 1857: The Great Rebellion

The British called it the **"Sepoy Mutiny."** Indian historians today usually call it the **Great Rebellion of 1857**, or the **First War of Independence**.

Why did it begin? The triggers and the deeper causes were both important.

**The deeper causes:**
- Decades of harsh land revenue had crushed peasant communities
- The Doctrine of Lapse had annexed Awadh, Jhansi, and other states
- The Subsidiary Alliance system had humiliated Indian princes
- The Macaulay reforms had insulted Indian traditions
- The sepoys themselves were largely from peasant families and saw their relatives suffering

**The trigger:** In early 1857, a rumour spread through the army that the new **Enfield rifle cartridges** — which had to be bitten open before loading — were greased with **cow fat and pig fat**. This was offensive to **both Hindu and Muslim** sepoys.

On **29 March 1857**, at **Barrackpore** in Bengal, the sepoy **Mangal Pandey** attacked his British officers. He was hanged. His execution lit the fuse.

In **May 1857**, sepoys at **Meerut** killed their British officers and marched to **Delhi**, where they proclaimed the elderly, politically powerless Mughal emperor **Bahadur Shah Zafar** as their leader.

The revolt spread rapidly across northern and central India — Kanpur, Lucknow, Jhansi, Bareilly. **Nana Saheb** led the rebellion at Kanpur. **Begum Hazrat Mahal** led the defence of Lucknow. And **Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi** — assisted by **Tatya Tope** — became the most legendary of all the rebels.

Rani Lakshmibai escaped a besieged Jhansi, captured the Gwalior fort, and died on the battlefield on **18 June 1858**, sword in hand. The British general who fought her recorded that she was **"the best and bravest of the rebel leaders."** She was twenty-nine years old.

The British response was systematic, prolonged, and exceptionally brutal. Delhi was recaptured in **September 1857** with house-to-house massacres. At Kanpur, the British staged mass executions designed to spread terror. Villages were burned across the Gangetic plain. The dead, on the Indian side, ran into the hundreds of thousands.

The Rebellion failed — partly because the leaders lacked a unified command, partly because not all of India joined in, partly because British military and technological power was overwhelming. **Bahadur Shah Zafar** was exiled to Rangoon, where he died a prisoner. With him died **800 years** of Mughal rule.

But 1857 was a turning point. It planted a seed.

## The Birth of the Raj

In **1858**, the British Crown took direct control of India from the East India Company. The age of the **British Raj** began. Queen Victoria issued a proclamation promising fair treatment and respect for Indian religions. Few Indians believed her. **Begum Hazrat Mahal** issued a counter-proclamation warning Indians:

> "It is the unvarying custom of the English never to forgive a fault be it great or small."

The Indian Army was reorganised so that no single group could ever again coordinate a rebellion. The princely states that had stood by the British were rewarded. The princely states that had wavered were punished. The empire settled in for what the British imagined would be a permanent rule. They called India **"the jewel in the Crown"** and boasted that **"the sun never sets on the British Empire."**

They were wrong. The seed of 1857 was growing.

## From 1857 to 1947: The Long Road to Freedom

A detailed history of the freedom movement belongs to higher grades, but the outline matters here.

- In **1885**, the **Indian National Congress** was founded — at first asking only for reforms, later demanding self-government, finally demanding complete independence
- In **1905**, the partition of Bengal triggered the **Swadeshi movement** — boycott foreign goods, buy Indian
- After the First World War (1914–18), in which over **a million Indian soldiers** fought for Britain in return for unfulfilled promises, the demand for freedom hardened
- In **1919**, the British massacred unarmed civilians at **Jallianwala Bagh** in Amritsar
- From the 1920s, **Mahatma Gandhi** transformed the movement into a mass struggle based on **non-violence (ahimsa)** and **truth-force (satyagraha)** — the Non-Cooperation Movement, the Salt March of 1930, the Quit India movement of 1942
- Alongside Gandhi, leaders like **Jawaharlal Nehru, Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, Subhas Chandra Bose, Bhagat Singh, Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, Sarojini Naidu, and Bhimrao Ambedkar** carried the freedom struggle forward in their own ways
- The Second World War (1939–45) drained British strength. The Indian National Army of Subhas Chandra Bose threatened the loyalty of British-Indian soldiers
- On **15 August 1947**, India became independent — though the partition that came with it tore the Subcontinent apart and killed perhaps a million people

The British had ruled India for **190 years** counting from Plassey. They left behind an India profoundly changed — and profoundly damaged.

## The Long Shadow

What did India lose to colonialism?

- **Tens of millions of lives** to famine and violence
- **Industries** built over centuries, destroyed in decades
- **Trillions of dollars** of wealth, transferred to Britain
- **Confidence** in our own knowledge systems, languages, and traditions
- **The unity** of the Subcontinent itself, divided into India, Pakistan, and later Bangladesh
- **Vast cultural treasures** — statues, paintings, manuscripts, jewels — looted into European museums

What survived?

- **The civilisation** itself — its languages, religions, music, philosophy, and family life
- **The village** — battered, but still alive
- **The spirit of resistance** — visible from Rani Abbakka to Mangal Pandey to Bhagat Singh to Gandhi

And there were some unintended legacies. The British and other European scholars **translated Sanskrit texts** into European languages for the first time, and the great philosophers of Europe — Goethe, Schopenhauer, Hegel, Emerson, Thoreau — were profoundly influenced by Indian thought. The German philosopher Hegel called the discovery of Sanskrit literature **"the discovery of a new continent."** Political domination flowed one way; **cultural influence sometimes flowed the other way.**

## Why This Matters Now

You are reading this chapter eighty years after Independence. Some questions may seem distant. They are not. The poverty of rural India today, the gap between English-educated elites and the rest, the borders of Pakistan and Bangladesh, the loot in the British Museum, even the running of the railways — all carry the imprint of those 190 years.

History is not the past. History is the past that is still inside the present. **The Colonial Era ended in 1947 on paper. Its consequences are still being worked out, decision by decision, generation by generation, today.**

## Apply: Bringing It Home

### Story 1 — Daadi's Independence Day

In a small house in Lucknow, **Daadi** remembers the night of **14 August 1947**. She was nine years old. Her father had been working all evening on a paper Indian flag — orange, white, green — by hand. At midnight, the neighbours gathered on the rooftop. Her father unfurled the flag. The grown-ups cried. The children did not understand. The next morning, the British officials at the local kacheri were gone, and an Indian collector was at the desk. Daadi says that for the first time in her life, the air smelled different.

### Story 2 — The Freedom Fighter Monument

In the centre of the small town of **Phulpur** stands a stone column. On it, eleven names. They are the local boys who joined the **Quit India movement** in 1942, were arrested, and never came home. The column is unswept most days. But on **15 August** and **26 January**, the panchayat lays a garland. The local school children read out the names. None of these eleven were famous. None of them are in textbooks. But without thousands of such unfamous people across thousands of such small towns, freedom would not have come.

### Story 3 — Made in India vs Made in England

Open the steel almirah in any Indian house older than 1947 and you may find both. A heavy "**Made in England**" iron lock, with the manufacturer's crest. A "**Made in India**" steel tiffin from the 1950s. The English lock works perfectly even today. The Indian tiffin was made by a swadeshi factory that started right after Independence — a small Mumbai workshop that grew, employing a hundred Indians, paying Indian wages. Two objects. Two histories. One was profit for them. The other was a beginning for us.

### A Question to Keep Asking

The British called what they did to India a **"civilising mission."** They believed it. Many of them believed it sincerely.

**How do we know when a powerful country is fooling itself about what it is doing to a weaker one?**
