## How Did Britain Manage to Rule India for So Long?

**Child:** Maa, how did Britain — a tiny island far away — manage to rule India for 200 years? India was huge. India was richer. India had more people. It doesn't add up.

**Mother:** It's the question every Indian asks at some point. And the honest answer surprises most people.

**Britain did not conquer India because Britain was stronger. Britain conquered India because India was divided.**

**Child:** Divided how?

**Mother:** In 1700, India was politically a patchwork. The **Mughal Empire** was crumbling. The **Marathas** were rising in the west. **Mysore** under Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan was rising in the south. The **Nizam of Hyderabad**, the **Nawab of Bengal**, the **Sikhs in Punjab**, hundreds of smaller princely states — all separate. All with their own armies, their own ambitions, their own quarrels.

The British did not fight them all at once. They could never have done that. They fought them **one at a time** — usually with help from another Indian power that wanted the first one defeated.

**Child:** So they used Indians to fight Indians?

**Mother:** Almost always. At **Plassey in 1757**, Robert Clive had a few thousand troops. **The Nawab of Bengal had fifty thousand.** Clive should have lost. But Clive bribed **Mir Jafar**, the Nawab's own commander, who held back the bulk of the army during the battle.

That is the pattern. Find a disgruntled noble. Make him a promise. Win the battle through betrayal. Reward the traitor. Repeat in the next kingdom.

**Child:** And the sepoys — the Indian soldiers — they fought for the British too?

**Mother:** Yes, that is the second piece. **At its height, the British-Indian army was over 90 per cent Indian.** Maybe a quarter of a million Indian men in uniform, paid by Indian taxes, defending British rule. The British officers were a tiny minority on top.

**Child:** Why did they sign up?

**Mother:** Some for pay — soldiering was a regular job in a starving country. Some for status. Some because their fathers and grandfathers had been soldiers and it was a family tradition. Most of them did not see themselves as fighting against India — they saw themselves as serving a particular regiment, a particular salary, a particular flag.

When the sepoys did finally see what they were a part of — in **1857** — that recognition was so explosive it nearly broke the empire.

**Child:** So the British succeeded mostly through Indian help?

**Mother:** Indian help, Indian money, Indian soldiers, and Indian division. The British provided the strategy and the discipline and the technology. India provided everything else.

That is the most painful lesson of this chapter. We were not conquered by an outside force overwhelming us from outside. **We were conquered by a small foreign force that learned to use our own divisions against us.**

**Child:** Could that happen again?

**Mother:** Anywhere divided people live together. Yes. That is why every Indian leader since Gandhi — across every political line — has said the same one thing in different words: **India's unity is non-negotiable.**

## What Was the Real Cost of Colonialism?

**Child:** Maa, I keep reading numbers in this chapter and they don't feel real. **45 trillion dollars drained. 100 million dead in famines.** It's so big it's almost meaningless. What was the real cost?

**Mother:** You're right that big numbers go numb. Let me try to make them small enough to feel.

**Forty-five trillion dollars** is about 13 times Britain's GDP in 2023. If India had kept that wealth and invested it at the rates that the British did — in factories, in railways for Indian use, in schools, in universities — India today would probably be a country at the level of Japan or South Korea. Possibly higher. **It is roughly the entire gap between what India is and what India should have been.**

**Child:** And the famines?

**Mother:** Try to picture this. In **1770**, in Bengal alone, **ten million people** died — in two summers. That is one out of every three Bengalis. **Imagine if a third of your school disappeared this year. A third of your colony. A third of your city.** That is what happened to Bengal in 1770.

And it kept happening. **1783, 1791, 1837, 1860, 1865, 1876, 1896, 1899, 1943.** Every generation under British rule saw a famine. The 1943 Bengal famine — only four years before Independence — killed another **three million** people.

**Child:** Were these natural famines?

**Mother:** This is the most important question in the chapter. **Famines have always happened in India.** Droughts, crop failures, locusts — they have happened for thousands of years. What was different about colonial famines?

Three things were different.

**One — the British took taxes during the famine.** Even when farmers had no harvest, the British insisted on collecting in cash. So peasants sold their seed-grain, their cattle, their tools, finally their children, to pay the tax.

**Two — the British exported food during famines.** In 1876–78, while millions starved in the Deccan, the British shipped a million tonnes of rice out of India every year. **There was food. It was sailing to Britain past dying Indians.**

**Three — the British refused to organise serious relief.** Lord Lytton said the government must not interfere with food prices, even at the height of starvation. He held a feast for 68,000 officials in Delhi while villages emptied.

So these famines were not just bad luck. **They were policy choices.** That is why we count them as colonial deaths.

**Child:** And what about the things we can't put a number on?

**Mother:** That's the deepest cost. **Confidence.** For two centuries, Indians were told — in school, in courts, in offices, in newspapers — that their own civilisation was inferior. Their languages were less than English. Their gods were less than the Christian God. Their crafts were less than European manufactures. Their medicine was less than Western medicine. Their ways of life were less.

We are still recovering from that. **Every time an Indian today feels secretly that something from abroad is better just because it is from abroad — that is the Macaulay residue.** It is the longest tail of colonialism, and it is invisible.

**Child:** That is a lot to carry.

**Mother:** It is. But it also means that healing it is in our hands. The British left in 1947. The job of finishing the freedom they could not give us — that is still ours.

## Was 1857 a 'Mutiny' or the 'First War of Independence'?

**Child:** Maa, my chapter calls 1857 the "Great Rebellion" but my friend's older brother says it was the "First War of Independence." The British called it the "Sepoy Mutiny." Why do the names matter so much?

**Mother:** Because names tell you what someone thinks happened.

**A mutiny** is a small thing. A few soldiers disobey orders. The officers handle it. Order is restored.

**A rebellion** is bigger. People rise up against authority. It is serious but may not have a clear purpose.

**A war of independence** is grand. A people fight as a nation to throw off foreign rule.

Each word puts the event in a different category.

**Child:** And what really happened?

**Mother:** The honest answer is: it was somewhere between a rebellion and a war. Let me explain why each name has a case.

**The "Mutiny" view (the British view):**
- It began with sepoys disobeying about cartridges.
- Most of southern India did not rise.
- Many Indian princes stayed loyal to the British.
- It was crushed in about eighteen months.

By that test, it was a military mutiny that spread into a regional uprising.

**The "First War of Independence" view (which Indian nationalists like Veer Savarkar pushed):**
- It united Hindus and Muslims against the British — they fought together under Bahadur Shah Zafar
- It involved peasants, princes, sepoys, religious leaders
- It was driven by genuine resentment of British rule, not just cartridges
- It planted the idea that India could one day be free

By that test, it was the beginning of the independence movement.

**Child:** Which is right?

**Mother:** Both, partly. **1857 was bigger than a mutiny and smaller than a war of independence.** It was an enormous, partial, leaderless uprising that nearly succeeded but failed because it had no national coordination, no single commander, no clear plan for what would come after.

But here is the deeper point. The events of 1857 — Bahadur Shah Zafar in chains, Rani Lakshmibai dying with sword in hand, Mangal Pandey hanged, villages burned across the Gangetic plain — these became **stories**. And stories travel. By the time Gandhi was born in 1869, every Indian child knew the names. By the time the freedom struggle peaked in the 1930s, those stories were fuel.

**So even if 1857 was not a successful war of independence, it was the spark that fed the war of independence that finally came.** That's why the name matters. It honours the people who tried first.

**Child:** Like a relay race?

**Mother:** Exactly like that. **Rani Lakshmibai passed the baton. Gandhi caught it ninety years later.** That is how freedom struggles work in big countries.

## Why Did Gandhi's Non-Violence Work?

**Child:** Maa, this is what really confuses me. The British had guns. They had armies. They were ruthless — you've shown me how ruthless. So how did **Gandhi**, walking with a stick and asking people not to fight back, get them to leave?

**Mother:** That is the question of the 20th century, and the answer has many layers. Let me try to unwrap them.

First — **Gandhi did not work alone.** He is the face of the movement, but behind him stood **Nehru, Patel, Maulana Azad, Subhas Bose, Bhagat Singh, Bhimrao Ambedkar, Sarojini Naidu, and millions of ordinary Indians** who marched, went to jail, lost jobs, lost businesses, lost lives. Even Bose, who disagreed completely with non-violence, was part of the same overall pressure on the British. **Non-violence worked partly because it was inside a much bigger movement.**

Second — **Gandhi understood the British in a way the 1857 rebels did not.**

The British of 1857 were a small, ruthless trading company. They had nothing to lose by burning villages. By Gandhi's time, the British were a global power that **needed to believe they were the good guys.** They had become "civilised." They had a free press. They had MPs who debated colonial policy. They had churches that preached morality.

**Gandhi's strategy was to force them to choose: be moral, or be honest about your brutality.** When the British shot peaceful protesters at Jallianwala Bagh in 1919 — General Dyer killing about 400 unarmed civilians — the news travelled to London. Even British conservatives were horrified. **Each act of British brutality against non-violent Indians became a victory for the Indians, because it stripped away the British self-image.**

**Child:** So Gandhi was setting a trap?

**Mother:** A very deliberate trap. Look at the **Salt March of 1930.** Gandhi walked 387 kilometres to make a pinch of salt from the sea, in violation of British tax law. **A pinch of salt.** Why?

Because the British monopoly on salt was visible to every Indian. Because salt is something everyone uses. Because picking up a handful of seawater is laughably non-threatening. And because **arresting an old man for picking up salt makes you look ridiculous in front of the world.**

The British arrested 80,000 people during the Salt Satyagraha. Their reputation in the international press collapsed. Even American newspapers ran sympathetic stories.

Third — **timing.** By 1945, after the Second World War, Britain was bankrupt. Half a million British soldiers had died. Cities had been bombed. The empire was costing more to hold than it was earning. **Gandhi's pressure mattered most because it came when Britain was already weakening.**

**Child:** So would non-violence have worked against the East India Company in 1757?

**Mother:** Probably not, no. Non-violence works against **opponents who care about their own self-image**. The Company did not care. Stalin did not care. The Nazis did not care. **Non-violence is a strategy, not a magic spell.** Gandhi was a genius partly because he chose the right strategy for his particular opponent at the particular time.

**Child:** Could non-violence work today?

**Mother:** Yes — on opponents who have a public image to lose. No — on opponents who do not. That is why every protest movement today still studies Gandhi: not to copy him exactly, but to think the way he thought.

## What Did India Lose Permanently to Colonialism?

**Child:** Maa, India is free now. We've had eighty years to recover. What is still broken from colonial times?

**Mother:** That is the hardest question. Some things heal. Some don't.

**Things India is recovering from but hasn't fully fixed:**

**Industry.** India lost two centuries of industrial development. We are catching up — IT, pharmaceuticals, space, manufacturing — but we are still behind countries like Japan or South Korea that were never colonised at that depth. **The 45 trillion dollars never came back.**

**Education.** Macaulay's school system shaped how we still teach. We still rely heavily on English. Our village schools have never been rebuilt to the density they had in 1830. We still produce too few engineers and scientists per capita.

**Confidence in our own ways.** I mentioned this earlier. The unconscious belief that "from abroad is better" still operates in many fields — fashion, medicine, language, even cuisine.

**Things India lost forever:**

**Partition.** In 1947, the Subcontinent split into India and Pakistan; in 1971, Bangladesh broke off from Pakistan. **The colonial age ended in three countries instead of one.** A million people died in Partition violence. Tens of millions were displaced. Three countries that should be partners now spend much of their budget defending against each other. **This is the permanent geopolitical scar of colonialism.**

**The lost civilisations of tribal India.** Hundreds of tribal communities were forced off their forests, broken by British "criminal tribes" laws, converted by missionaries, displaced by land surveys. **Languages and knowledge systems that survived for thousands of years did not survive 200 years of colonial law.** This loss is irreversible.

**Stolen heritage.** Look up the **Kohinoor diamond** sitting in the Tower of London. Look up the **Amaravati marbles** in the British Museum. Look up the **Saraswati statue** from Dhar. These belong to India. They are still there. Some debates about returning them are slowly beginning, but most of it will never come home.

**The lost Indians.** Between 50 and 100 million Indians who died in colonial famines, plus those who died in 1857, in the freedom struggle, and in Partition. **Their descendants are simply not there.** Demographers estimate that without colonialism India today would have between 200 and 400 million more people, with completely different families, traditions, and ideas. **That is a loss we don't even know how to measure.**

**Child:** Maa, this is making me sad.

**Mother:** It should make you sad. Mourning is part of understanding history. **If you cannot feel what colonialism did to India, you cannot fully appreciate what 15 August 1947 means.**

But sadness alone is not enough. **Knowing what was taken should make you fierce about protecting what we still have.** Our unity. Our languages. Our heritage sites. Our forests. Our democracy. Our ability to think for ourselves.

That is the responsibility this chapter hands you.

## What Should I Take From This?

**Child:** Maa, before we close, what should I really remember from this chapter? Years from now, what do you want me to carry?

**Mother:** Three things. Just three.

**One — India was rich before they came, and poor when they left.** Whenever anyone tells you that the British "developed" India or "civilised" India, remember the numbers. **25 per cent of world GDP in 1700. Under 5 per cent in 1947.** The arithmetic alone tells the truth.

**Two — the conquest was made possible by Indian division, not Indian weakness.** Mir Jafar at Plassey. The Marathas and the Mughals fighting each other. Hindu against Muslim, prince against prince, region against region. **Wherever Indians divide, foreigners profit. Wherever Indians unite, the country is free.** That is a lesson that did not stop applying in 1947.

**Three — every generation has its freedom fighters, and most of them are unfamous.** You know Gandhi. You know Bhagat Singh. You know Rani Lakshmibai. But for every famous name there were ten thousand who went to jail, lost their land, lost their lives — and were never written about. **The schoolteacher who taught Hindi when the British taxed Hindi books. The farmer who refused to grow indigo. The mother who hid a Quit India pamphlet under her saree. The clerk who quit a comfortable colonial office to live in poverty.** These people are the real reason India is free.

**Child:** Are there still freedom fighters today?

**Mother:** Always. Different battles, but always. **The freedom to be poor and still treated with dignity. The freedom to be a tribal and not have your forest taken. The freedom of a girl to go to school in a village that says girls shouldn't.** These freedoms still need fighters.

**Child:** So freedom is not just one event in 1947?

**Mother:** Freedom is a daily practice. **1947 won us political freedom from the British. The freedoms inside India still need to be won, day by day, by people like you.**

That is what this chapter is finally trying to say.

**Child:** I'll remember.

**Mother:** Good. That is enough for one day. Go and study.
