**Child:** Maa, why does my one vote even matter? There are millions of votes — mine doesn't change anything.

**Mother:** Beta, I have heard this from your Mamu too every election. Let me ask you something first. Last year your class elected Ananya as the class representative. She won by just two votes, na? What if two of those students had said exactly what you just said — "my vote won't change anything" — and stayed home?

**Child:** Then Rohit would have won, I guess.

**Mother:** Exactly. And the school trip would have gone where Rohit wanted, not where Ananya wanted. Real elections work the same way. In 2008, an MLA in Rajasthan won by ONE vote. In 2017, a Karnataka seat was decided by just nine votes. There are local body wards in India where the margin is sometimes single digits.

**Child:** But Lok Sabha elections? Those are won by lakhs.

**Mother:** True, some are. But your vote is doing more than tipping the scale of who wins. It is counted in the *turnout*. If everyone in our colony votes, our MLA knows that this neighbourhood pays attention — and our complaints about the broken footpath get heard faster. If only forty per cent of us vote, we become a colony that is easy to ignore. Voting is also how you say, "I am a citizen of this country, I am paying attention." Even if your candidate loses, the politician who won knows that thousands of people in the constituency wanted someone else. That keeps her on her toes for the next five years.

**Child:** So even a losing vote isn't a wasted vote.

**Mother:** Never. The wasted vote is the one not cast.

## How Did India Get to Universal Adult Voting So Fast?

**Child:** I read that in 1947 only 14 per cent of Indians could read. Why did the Constitution makers give everyone the vote anyway? Wouldn't it have been safer to wait until people were educated?

**Mother:** That argument was made in the Constituent Assembly itself, beta. Some members said exactly what you said — wait, educate first, then give the vote. But the bigger voices like Ambedkar, Nehru, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar disagreed. They asked one question that settled it: who decides when a citizen is "ready" enough to vote? An educated lawyer? A landlord? A British official? Every time someone draws that line, they leave somebody out.

**Child:** And usually it's the poor people who get left out.

**Mother:** Always. In England, the vote was given to landowners first. In America, slaves were freed in 1865 but Black voters faced restrictions for another century. In Switzerland, *women* could not vote until 1971. India said no — every adult, on day one. No literacy test. No property test. No religion test. One vote, one value.

**Child:** That's actually amazing.

**Mother:** It was a leap of faith. And the leap worked. In the very first election of 1951–52, voters used picture symbols — the cow, the hut, the lamp — to identify their party, because many could not read names. Forty-six crore ballots were cast. Sukumar Sen, the first Chief Election Commissioner, pulled it off with no precedent anywhere in the world to learn from. Imagine telling a country of 36 crore people, "Tomorrow you choose your government" — and seventy years later it is still working.

**Child:** And women voted from the beginning?

**Mother:** From day one. While American women had only had the vote for thirty years and Swiss women would have to wait another twenty.

## What Does the Election Commission Actually Do?

**Child:** Maa, who runs the elections? Like — who decides the date, prints the ballots, counts the votes?

**Mother:** The Election Commission of India. The ECI. It was set up in 1950, even before the Republic. It is an **independent constitutional body** — that word *independent* is important.

**Child:** Independent of whom?

**Mother:** Of the government. The ECI does not answer to the ruling party. It answers to the Constitution. That is what allows it to scold even a Prime Minister if needed during election season.

**Child:** Who is in charge of it?

**Mother:** The Chief Election Commissioner — the CEC — along with two Election Commissioners. Below them, every state has a Chief Electoral Officer, every district has a District Election Officer, every constituency has a Returning Officer. Lakhs of officials get involved on election day — including your school principal, the post office staff, the bank clerk down the road.

**Child:** So my computer teacher might be on election duty?

**Mother:** Very likely. Teachers are the backbone of Indian elections. The ECI sets the dates, registers political parties, manages the voter lists, designs the EVMs, enforces the Model Code of Conduct, runs the counting, declares the results. From the lonely polling booth in the Gir forest to the seven-phase Lok Sabha rollout — all of it is the ECI's planning.

**Child:** And panchayat elections too?

**Mother:** No, those are run by **State Election Commissions** — slightly different body, similar idea. Panchayat and ward elections are run by them, in consultation with the ECI.

## Why Were EVMs a Big Deal?

**Child:** Maa, I have only ever seen EVMs. Were paper ballots really that bad?

**Mother:** Paper ballots took days to count. Boxes could be stuffed, snatched, set on fire. In some places, polling booths were "captured" by goons who would chase voters away and stuff the boxes themselves. Slow counting also meant prolonged uncertainty and tension after election day.

**Child:** And EVMs fixed all of that?

**Mother:** Mostly, yes. EVMs were rolled out gradually through the 1990s and by the 2004 general election the whole country was voting electronically. The machine is dead simple — battery powered, not connected to the internet, has just a control unit and a ballot unit. It records one vote every few seconds, refuses to accept two votes from the same person, and locks itself down if anyone tries to tamper. Counting that took five days now takes five hours.

**Child:** What about people who don't trust the EVM?

**Mother:** That is exactly why VVPAT was added. VVPAT — Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trail. After you press the button on the EVM, a little glass window in the next box flashes a paper slip showing the party and symbol you voted for, for seven seconds. The slip then drops into a sealed box. So you have BOTH an electronic vote AND a paper record. If anyone disputes a result, the VVPAT slips can be counted by hand.

**Child:** And NOTA?

**Mother:** NOTA — None Of The Above — was added in 2013. It is the last button on the EVM. If you don't like any candidate, you can press NOTA to say "I came, I voted, but none of these people deserve my support." It doesn't currently change the winner — but parties watch the NOTA share carefully. A high NOTA in a seat tells them: your candidate was bad, do better next time. It is a quiet protest button.

**Child:** I like that. You're voting against without staying home.

**Mother:** Exactly. Democracy gave you a way to say "no, thanks" without being silent.

## What Could Go Wrong with Elections?

**Child:** It all sounds very well-organised. But you keep saying "be a vigilant voter." What can go wrong?

**Mother:** Plenty. India's elections are admired around the world, but no system is perfect. Here are the worries.

**Child:** Tell me.

**Mother:** First, **money power**. The cost of contesting a Lok Sabha seat has exploded. Big parties can pour crores into advertising, rallies, transport. An honest candidate without funds finds it almost impossible to compete. Second, **criminal candidates**. Far too many MPs and MLAs have serious criminal cases pending against them. The Supreme Court has tried to force disclosure of these cases, but the deeper problem is that voters still elect them.

**Child:** Why?

**Mother:** Sometimes because they are popular, sometimes because they distribute money or sarees, sometimes because the alternative is even worse. Which brings us to the third worry — **bribing voters**. Distributing cash, gift items, alcohol in exchange for votes. The ECI does raids during election season — bundles of 500-rupee notes get seized from candidates' cars. The Model Code of Conduct treats this as a serious violation.

**Child:** And the Model Code stops this?

**Mother:** It tries. Before 1991, the MCC existed but was barely enforced. Then in 1990, **T. N. Seshan** became Chief Election Commissioner. He changed everything. He cracked down on excessive spending, posters everywhere, late-night loudspeakers, government officials campaigning for the ruling party. He brought in the photo Voter ID card to stop proxy voting. He didn't care which party — if you broke the rules, he came after you. By the time he stepped down, the ECI was a feared and respected institution. We owe him a lot.

**Child:** What other challenges?

**Mother:** **Voter apathy**, especially in cities. In 2024, about a third of eligible voters didn't show up. Many rich Indians in Mumbai or Delhi who would never miss a flight for work skip elections completely. And then — **misinformation**. Fake WhatsApp forwards, deepfake videos, polarising reels. Social media has made campaigning faster but also dirtier. A voter today has to actually verify what she sees before believing it.

## What Should I Take From This?

**Child:** OK Maa, so what's the main thing I should remember from all this?

**Mother:** Three things, beta. First — **universal adult franchise is precious**. People around the world fought for decades, sometimes a century, to get what India gave every adult on day one. Don't take it lightly.

**Child:** Second?

**Mother:** **Voting is a right, but it is also a responsibility.** You have to register when you turn 18. You have to show up. You have to actually read about the candidates and not just vote for whoever your friends or relatives tell you to. If a candidate is offering free liquor or cash, that is exactly the kind of person who should not win. Vote with your head, not your wallet.

**Child:** Third?

**Mother:** **Democracy needs vigilant citizens — even between elections.** When the MLA visits the area, ask questions. When the panchayat builds a road, check if it is built well. When you see misinformation, don't forward it. When you see a problem, write to your MP. The ECI runs the election — but what happens after is up to all of us.

**Child:** Three more years till I am eighteen.

**Mother:** Then start watching now. Read the front page. Find out who your MP is, who your MLA is, which party they belong to, what they promised, what they actually did. Walk past a polling booth on election day so you understand the rhythm. By the time you can vote, you will know exactly what you are doing.

**Child:** And then my one vote will matter.

**Mother:** Your one vote, beta, was always going to matter. Now you will use it like it does.
