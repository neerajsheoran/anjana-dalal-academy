## A Bold Experiment Begun in 1950

When the makers of our Constitution sat in Delhi and debated who should be allowed to vote in independent India, they were facing a question no large country had answered yet. In 1947, the literacy rate in India was around 14 per cent. Among women it was barely 8 per cent. The population was over 340 million, spread across deserts, jungles, mountains and tiny island clusters. Many other democracies had moved cautiously — first letting only landowners vote, then only educated men, then men of certain communities, and only much later allowing women. The United Kingdom took nearly a century. Switzerland gave women the vote only in **1971**.

India chose a different path. From the very first general election in **1951–52**, every adult citizen — man or woman, rich or poor, lettered or unlettered, from any caste, creed or religion — would have **one vote of equal value**. Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar called it an experiment "so boldly undertaken" that nothing in world history could compare to it.

This decision was not made lightly. It rested on the belief that the right to choose your own rulers is a basic human right, not a reward for wealth or education. This chapter is the story of that bold experiment — what we call **universal adult franchise** — and the enormous machinery built to make it work, year after year, across the largest democracy on Earth.

---

## What "Universal Adult Franchise" Actually Means

The word **franchise** simply means the right to vote granted by a constitution or a government. Some textbooks use the word **suffrage** with the same meaning. The word **universal** tells us that this right is given to *every* adult citizen without picking and choosing. **Adult** specifies the age at which the right begins — in India, today, it is **18 years**.

Put together, **universal adult franchise** means: every Indian citizen aged 18 and above has the right to vote, with one vote per person and every vote carrying equal weight, regardless of caste, gender, religion, education, income, or any other factor. This principle is written into **Article 326** of the Constitution of India.

Two important details follow from this:

- **No one can vote on someone else's behalf.** Even a husband cannot vote for his wife, and a son cannot vote for his parents.
- **To use the right, you must be registered as a voter in your constituency.** A **constituency** is the geographical area whose voters elect one representative to a legislative body.

There are a few narrow exceptions to the right to vote. A citizen who has been convicted of certain serious crimes can be barred from voting for a defined period.

Until 1988, the minimum voting age in India was 21. A constitutional amendment lowered it to 18 that year — the argument being that an 18-year-old is old enough to drive, to marry, to be tried as an adult for crimes, and therefore old enough to choose her government.

> **Key idea:** Universal adult franchise means one vote, one value, for every adult citizen — and that vote cannot be transferred, sold, or cast by anyone else on your behalf.

## Why Universal Franchise Was the Right Decision

Inside the Constituent Assembly, some members were nervous. They worried that an illiterate voter might be confused, fooled or bribed. Why not, they suggested, give the vote only to those who could read and write?

The Constitution makers rejected this view, and they were right. Several reasons explain why universal franchise has been a strength of Indian democracy:

- **Equality of citizenship.** If a peasant in a village had to wait for education before voting, but a landlord's son could vote at 18, the two would not really be equal citizens. The Constitution refused this hierarchy.
- **No discrimination.** Caste, religion, gender, wealth and education are not allowed to decide who counts and who does not. This is the deepest meaning of "We the people".
- **Power to determine your own future.** A voter chooses the people who will frame the laws, decide the budget, build the roads, run the schools. Without the vote, your future is in someone else's hands.
- **Accountability of leaders.** Politicians who know the poorest farmer can vote them out have a reason to listen to that farmer. Universal franchise is what keeps elected representatives **answerable**.
- **Civic engagement.** When everyone votes, everyone has a stake in fixing the country's problems — pollution, schools, jobs, water — instead of leaving them for "someone else" to sort out.

India was also among the **earliest large nations to give women the right to vote from the very beginning** of its independence. In some of the world's older democracies, women had to fight long, painful struggles to win this right — pictures of the 1913 women's suffrage parade in the United States or the 1971 vote that finally gave Swiss women the franchise are reminders of how recent that victory was elsewhere.

## The Scale of Indian Elections

It helps to feel the size of what we are talking about. In the 2024 Lok Sabha general election:

- About **980 million voters** were eligible to vote — fifteen times the population of the United Kingdom.
- **543 parliamentary constituencies** had to elect one Member of Parliament each.
- More than **1 million polling stations** were set up across the country.
- The voting was held in **seven phases**, spread over six weeks, because no country has ever attempted a single-day election at this scale.

And the Lok Sabha is only one election. India also has **3.1 million elected representatives** — about 1.3 million of them women — sitting in more than 250,000 local bodies (gram panchayats, municipalities, ward committees and so on). All of them are chosen through universal adult franchise.

In any given year, somewhere in India a major election is taking place. Managing this needs an **electoral system** — a set of interconnected rules, institutions and people that together make voting possible.

## The Election Commission of India

The institution at the heart of India's electoral system is the **Election Commission of India (ECI)**. It was set up in **1950** and conducts elections to the Lok Sabha, the Rajya Sabha, every State Legislative Assembly, and the offices of the President and Vice President of India.

The ECI is an **independent constitutional body**. That word *independent* matters. It is not controlled by the ruling party of the day; it answers to the Constitution. Its independence is what lets it tell *any* government — even the one in power — that it has crossed a line.

The ECI is led by the **Chief Election Commissioner (CEC)** and two **Election Commissioners**. Below this national-level team, the structure stretches all the way down to the booth:

- At the **state/union territory level**, a **Chief Electoral Officer** runs election work for that state.
- At the **district level**, a **District Election Officer** coordinates the elections within the district.
- For each constituency, a **Returning Officer (RO)** is in charge of actually conducting the election.
- An **Electoral Registration Officer (ERO)** manages the voter list and adds new voters as they turn 18.

The ECI's main tasks include **setting election dates**, **registering political parties**, preparing and updating the **electoral roll** (voter list), **enforcing the Model Code of Conduct**, training the lakhs of officers and polling staff needed on election day, and overseeing the **entire electoral process** from nomination to the declaration of results.

Note that **local body elections** — panchayat and municipal elections — are run by **State Election Commissions** in consultation with the ECI, not by the ECI directly.

## How a Voter Actually Votes — Inside a Polling Booth

Imagine you are 18 and walking up to your nearest polling station on election day. You have your **Voter ID card** (also called EPIC — Elector's Photo Identity Card) in your pocket. The room has a quiet line of officials and curtained booths. Here is what happens:

- The **First Polling Officer** finds your name on the voter list and checks your ID proof to confirm who you are.
- The **Second Polling Officer** applies a streak of **indelible ink** on your left index finger — that purple mark that takes weeks to wash off. It prevents anyone from voting twice. You also sign the register and receive a chit.
- The **Third Polling Officer** takes your chit and double-checks the ink on your finger to make sure you have not been waved through twice by mistake.
- You then step behind a curtain to the **Electronic Voting Machine (EVM)**. The names and party symbols of all candidates in your constituency are listed on it, with a small button next to each. There is also a **NOTA** option — *None of the Above* — at the very bottom.
- You press the button next to the candidate of your choice. A short **beep** confirms your vote.
- Through a small glass window in the **VVPAT (Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trail)** machine, a **printed paper slip** flashes for seven seconds showing the candidate you voted for. This is your proof that the EVM recorded your vote correctly. The slip then drops into a sealed box.

The whole process takes a minute or two. Through every step — from the ink to the curtain to the silent EVM — the design protects the **secret ballot**: no one in the room, not the polling officer, not the candidate's agent, not your neighbour, will ever know whom you voted for. The secrecy is what makes it impossible for anyone to threaten or bribe you for your vote.

## EVMs and Why They Were a Big Deal

Before the 1990s, India used **paper ballots**. Voters were given a sheet listing all candidates and asked to stamp a mark next to their choice, then fold and drop the ballot into a sealed box. Counting was slow — sometimes lasting days. Paper ballots could be stuffed, snatched, or ruined.

The **Electronic Voting Machine** changed this. An EVM is a simple, rugged device with two units — a **Control Unit** held by the polling officer and a **Ballot Unit** kept inside the booth where the voter presses the button. It does not connect to the internet. It runs on a battery. It can record one vote every few seconds, refuses to register two votes from the same voter, and stops automatically if anyone tries to tamper with it.

EVMs were introduced gradually from the late 1990s. By the **2004 general elections**, the entire country was voting electronically. Counting that used to take days now ends in hours.

The **VVPAT** machine was added later as a transparency check. It prints a paper slip after every vote so that, if there is ever a dispute, the slips can be physically counted to verify what the EVM recorded.

India's EVM–VVPAT technology has been studied and adopted by other countries — **Bhutan** and **Namibia**, for example, have used EVMs with support from the ECI, and trainers from India have helped run elections in several neighbouring nations.

> **Key idea:** EVMs made Indian elections faster and harder to rig. VVPAT slips added a verifiable paper record on top of the electronic count.

## NOTA — A Quiet Tool

What if a voter does not want to support *any* of the candidates on the ballot? Earlier, that voter either had to vote for someone she did not believe in, or stay home, or cast an invalid ballot. None of these sent a clear message.

Since 2013, India offers a sixth (or last) option on every EVM: **NOTA — None of the Above**. Pressing NOTA records that the voter rejected every candidate offered. NOTA does not currently change who wins — the candidate with the most ordinary votes still wins. But the count of NOTA votes is published and read carefully. A high NOTA share in a constituency tells parties: *the people here want better choices next time*.

## The Model Code of Conduct

Elections can get aggressive — money is spent, speeches are made, accusations fly. To keep the contest fair, the ECI enforces the **Model Code of Conduct (MCC)**. It is a set of rules that every political party and candidate must follow once the election dates are announced.

A few of its key elements:

- **The ruling party cannot use government resources for campaigning.** No new schemes, projects or appointments that might influence voters can be announced. Government vehicles, buildings and budgets must not be turned into campaign tools.
- **All candidates must show discretion and restraint.** No hate speeches based on religion, caste or language. No personal insults that incite violence.
- **Bribing voters is punishable.** Distributing cash, sarees, household appliances or liquor in return for votes is a serious offence. The ECI regularly seizes such "freebies" in raids during election season.
- **Officials must remain neutral.** A serving collector or police officer cannot campaign for any party.

The MCC was first adopted voluntarily in **Kerala in 1960**. The ECI circulated it nationally for the 1962 general elections, and from **1991** onwards became proactive in enforcing it.

When MCC violations are reported — a candidate distributing free appliances, abusive language between rival candidates, a minister campaigning at government cost, or large cash bundles found in a car — the ECI can issue warnings, ban a leader from campaigning for a set period, file FIRs, or take stricter action.

## T. N. Seshan — The Officer Who Made Elections Fairer

For decades after 1950, the ECI existed but was relatively quiet. The Model Code was honoured more in the breaking than the keeping. Then in 1990, **T. N. Seshan** was appointed **Chief Election Commissioner**, and Indian elections changed.

Seshan insisted on enforcing the rules already on the books. He cracked down on **excessive campaign spending**, banned posters and loudspeakers from being used after fixed hours, and pushed for **photo Voter ID cards** to eliminate proxy voting. He postponed elections where conditions were not fair, and he was willing to take on powerful politicians from any party.

By the time he stepped down in 1996, the idea that the Election Commission was a strict, neutral, independent body had been planted in the public mind. Indians today take this for granted — but it was, in good measure, T. N. Seshan's gift to Indian democracy.

## The Different Elections India Runs

Universal adult franchise is the same rule everywhere — but India runs *several* kinds of elections, with different mechanisms:

### Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies — Direct Elections

For the **Lok Sabha**, the country is divided into **543 constituencies**. Voters in each constituency directly elect one Member of Parliament (MP). Out of these, **84 seats** are reserved for Scheduled Castes and **47** for Scheduled Tribes, ensuring representation for historically disadvantaged communities. The remaining 412 are general seats. The party or coalition with a majority of MPs forms the central government and its leader usually becomes the **Prime Minister**.

A similar process plays out for **State Legislative Assemblies** (often called Vidhan Sabha, Niyama Sabha, or other regional names). Voters directly elect **Members of the Legislative Assembly (MLAs)**. The leader of the majority party becomes the **Chief Minister** of that state.

India uses the **First-Past-the-Post (FPTP)** system. The candidate with the most votes in a constituency wins — even if she does not get 50 per cent. Just as in the Suryodaya School class election where Gurmat won with only 12 of 33 votes, an Indian MP can win her seat with, say, 32 per cent of the vote, as long as no other candidate gets more.

### Rajya Sabha — Indirect Elections

The **Rajya Sabha** ("Council of States") has 245 members. Of these, **233 are elected indirectly by MLAs of state legislative assemblies**, and **12 are nominated by the President of India** for their distinguished work in literature, science, art or social service. Each state's share of Rajya Sabha seats depends on its population — Uttar Pradesh sends many more than Arunachal Pradesh.

The Rajya Sabha is called the **Permanent House** because it is **never dissolved**. Each member serves a six-year term, and one-third of the House retires every two years, so there is always continuity. Voting is by a special method called the **single transferable vote**, which lets smaller states win fairer representation.

### President and Vice President — Indirect, Through an Electoral College

The **President of India** is not elected by ordinary voters. Instead, an **Electoral College** does it. This college consists of:

- The **elected MPs** of both Houses of Parliament (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha)
- The **elected MLAs** of all states, plus the union territories of Delhi and Puducherry

**Nominated members** and members of state Legislative Councils are *not* part of this college. The reasoning is simple: only people who themselves were directly elected by the public can elect the President, keeping the chain of democratic legitimacy unbroken. Voting again uses the single transferable vote, and votes from more populous states carry greater weight.

The **Vice President** is elected by the elected and nominated members of both Houses of Parliament (no MLAs). The Vice President chairs the Rajya Sabha and steps in if the President is unable to function.

## Reaching Every Last Voter

The ECI takes pride in the principle that **no eligible voter is left behind**, however remote or however vulnerable. Some of the steps it has taken:

- **Polling stations within walking distance.** Indian law requires a polling station to be no more than two kilometres from any voter's home. Election teams trek into deep forests, climb to high-altitude villages, sail to islands, and ride camels into deserts to reach voters.
- **Polling stations for single voters.** Famously, in the **Gir forest of Gujarat**, election officials set up a full polling station just for **Mahant Bharatdas Darshandas**, a priest who lives alone at a temple inside the lion sanctuary. One voter, one station, every election.
- **Home voting and postal ballots.** Since 2024, the elderly above a certain age, and people with disabilities, can vote **from their homes**. Soldiers posted on borders and Indian voters abroad use the postal ballot.
- **Accessibility for the disabled.** Polling booths now have ramps and wheelchairs, **Braille-enabled voter slips** for the visually impaired, and app-based requests for help. EVMs carry Braille markings next to candidate buttons.

Despite all this effort, about **34 per cent of eligible voters did not vote** in the 2024 general election. Voter apathy — especially in big cities — remains a real challenge.

## Challenges and the Road Ahead

India's electoral system is admired worldwide, but it is not perfect. Among the concerns frequently discussed:

- **Money power.** The cost of contesting elections has shot up. This shuts ordinary citizens out and lets a few wealthy candidates dominate.
- **Criminalisation of politics.** A worryingly large number of elected representatives have serious criminal cases pending against them. Reforms have tried to make criminal records of candidates public, but the deeper problem remains.
- **Voter apathy in cities.** Urban voters often skip elections. Yet local issues — water, traffic, schools, garbage — are decided by exactly those elections.
- **Misinformation in the digital age.** Campaign reels, deepfake videos, viral but false claims on WhatsApp and social media confuse voters and poison the debate.
- **Hate speech and polarisation.** Election speeches that pit communities against each other damage the social fabric long after voting day is over.

The solutions lie partly in **stronger enforcement**, partly in **electoral reforms** (cleaner funding, faster trials for criminal candidates), and most of all in **aware, vigilant voters** who ask the right questions, check facts, and turn up at the booth.

> **Key idea:** Universal franchise gives every adult Indian the *right* to vote. Democracy works only when each of us treats that right as a *responsibility* and uses it wisely.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Tashigang — the world's highest polling booth.** Up in the Spiti valley of Himachal Pradesh, the village of Tashigang sits at **15,256 feet** — higher than most Himalayan trekking destinations. It is also the **highest polling booth in the world**. A team of polling officers carries an EVM up through thin air and freezing winds for the handful of voters who live there. In 2024, every single registered Tashigang voter cast their vote. The image of one election team trudging up an icy slope so that thirty mountain dwellers can vote is what universal franchise means in practice.

**Bharat Singh of Banswara — voting at 110.** In Rajasthan, **Bharat Singh** of Banswara district reportedly cast his vote in the 2024 Lok Sabha election at the age of **110**. He had voted in every general election since 1951–52. When asked why he still bothered, he smiled and said the day he stops voting is the day he stops being a citizen. India is full of such elders. Their unbroken streak across seventy-plus years is the lived history of universal adult franchise.

**The polling officer who reaches one voter in the forest.** Inside the **Gir sanctuary**, where Asiatic lions roam, lives **Mahant Bharatdas Darshandas**, the sole priest at the Banej temple. Indian election law says every voter must have a booth within reach. So every general election, the Gujarat ECI team — a returning officer, polling officers, security guards and one full EVM-VVPAT set — travels into the forest. They set up an actual polling booth. They wait. One man walks up, votes in secrecy, and walks back. The booth is then sealed and carried out. One voter. One station. Every time.

### Try it at home — the voter detective project

*30–45 minutes · pen + notebook + internet · best done with a family member who has voted*

- **Pick the next election** that will be held in your area — Lok Sabha, State Assembly, municipal, or panchayat. Find out the date.
- **Visit (or virtually look up) the polling booth** nearest to your home on election day if you can — observe the indelible ink, the EVM signage, the queues. *Don't* enter the booth itself. Just stand at a respectful distance.
- **Interview a first-time 18-year-old voter** in your extended family — a cousin, neighbour, older sibling. Ask: *How did you decide whom to vote for? What did the booth look like inside? What surprised you?*
- **Go to the ECI website or indiavotes.com** and look up the **number of candidates** who contested in your local constituency in the last general election. Note how many had a winning chance, how many were independents, how many got the NOTA share.
- **Bonus:** ask a teacher or relative who was a polling officer to share what their election-day duty was like.

### What to look for

Notice that the polling officials at the booth are mostly **schoolteachers, post office staff, government clerks** — ordinary people pulled in for election duty. Notice how seriously they take the role. Notice also the **gap between voters registered and voters who actually came** — if your booth has, say, 1,200 registered voters and only 800 voted, that is a third of voices not heard. Was the gap higher among young voters or older ones?

**Show what you found:** Make a single notebook page titled "My constituency at the last election." Write down: the name of the constituency, the number of candidates, the name and party of the winner, the winner's vote percentage, the NOTA percentage, and one sentence each from your interview with the first-time voter and the polling officer. Show it to a parent or grandparent and ask them how voting in *their* first election was different from voting today.
