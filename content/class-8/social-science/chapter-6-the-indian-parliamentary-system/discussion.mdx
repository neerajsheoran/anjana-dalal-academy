## What Does Parliament Actually Do?

**Child:** Maa, every time I switch on the TV news, somebody is shouting in Parliament about something. What does Parliament actually do? Is it just a place where people argue?

**Mother:** That's a fair question — what you see on TV is the noisy 10%. Most of Parliament's work happens away from the cameras. Let me explain what it really does.

**One — it makes laws.** Every Indian law — the **Right to Education Act**, the **GST law**, the **Women's Reservation Bill, 2023** — was made in Parliament. Without Parliament, there is no law. Without law, there is no order. Even something as small as the rule about wearing helmets on a two-wheeler was first a bill, debated in Parliament or a state assembly, voted on, and signed by the President.

**Two — it controls the country's money.** Every February, the **Finance Minister** presents the **Union Budget**. Until Parliament approves the budget, **the government cannot spend a single rupee**. That is a very serious power. The Budget says how much money goes to defence, how much to railways, how much to schools, how much to villages. If Parliament refuses, the government has no money.

**Three — it watches over the government.** Every working day, MPs ask ministers questions. *"Why are railway accidents rising?" "How many schools are without teachers in Bihar?" "What is the status of the Bharatmala highways project?"* Ministers have to answer. This is **Question Hour**. It's like having to face a class test in front of 543 examiners every day.

**Four — it chooses the government.** The party with a Lok Sabha majority forms the government. The Prime Minister has to keep that majority. If she loses it, she is out — through a **no-confidence motion**.

**Child:** So Parliament is sort of the foundation of everything?

**Mother:** Exactly. People focus on the shouting because it makes for good TV. But the **shouting is just the surface**. Below it, real laws are made, real money is allocated, real ministers are questioned. The shouting is **a sign that democracy is alive** — in countries without democracy, no one is allowed to shout at the government at all.

## Why Two Houses — Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha?

**Child:** Maa, I don't get this — if Lok Sabha is already elected by the people, why do we need a Rajya Sabha at all? Isn't it just doubling up?

**Mother:** This is one of the most thoughtful design choices the Constituent Assembly made. There were big debates in 1947-49 about whether India should have **one house or two**. They went with two for several reasons.

**Reason 1 — States need a voice.** India is a **federation**. Tamil Nadu, Bihar, Manipur, Goa — every state has its own identity, language, and interests. The Lok Sabha represents **the people** based on population. So Uttar Pradesh sends 80 MPs and Sikkim sends 1. But Sikkim is still a state of India with its own concerns. The **Rajya Sabha** gives every state a steadier, separate voice. That's why it's called the **Council of States**.

**Reason 2 — A second look.** When the Lok Sabha passes a bill in a hurry — perhaps in the heat of a political moment — the Rajya Sabha can take a **calmer look**. It can suggest changes, send the bill back, force more debate. Two houses give a bill **two chances** to be examined.

**Reason 3 — Different election cycles.** The Lok Sabha can be **dissolved** before its 5-year term — there have been many early elections. But the Rajya Sabha is **permanent** — only one-third of its members retire every two years. So there is always **continuity**. Even if a Lok Sabha is dissolved tomorrow, the Rajya Sabha is still there to function.

**Reason 4 — Expertise.** Twelve Rajya Sabha members are **nominated by the President** for their distinction in literature, science, art, or social service. So you can have people like **Lata Mangeshkar**, **Sachin Tendulkar**, or **Dr M.S. Subbulakshmi** in Parliament without them having to fight an election. This brings expert voices into law-making.

**Child:** But doesn't this make things slower?

**Mother:** Yes — and that's the point. Many countries that started with one house — like New Zealand — later added a second one because they realised that **too much speed is dangerous in lawmaking**. Britain has the **House of Commons** and the **House of Lords**. The United States has the **House of Representatives** and the **Senate**. We have **Lok Sabha** and **Rajya Sabha**. Across democracies, two houses are the norm for federal countries.

When a law becomes part of a country's life, you don't want it passed in five minutes by one excited majority. You want it **examined twice**, by different people, with cooler heads in between. The Rajya Sabha is that cooler head.

## How Does a Bill Actually Become a Law?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter talked about RTE going through committees and readings and assent. Can you walk me through it one more time? It sounds like a long journey.

**Mother:** It is a long journey — and that's deliberate. Let's trace it step by step.

**Step 1 — The idea.** Somebody — usually the government — has an idea for a new law. It might come from a policy report, a Supreme Court judgment, a public demand, or a political promise. For **RTE**, the idea was that every Indian child should have **free schooling between ages 6 and 14**.

**Step 2 — Drafting.** Lawyers and ministry officials prepare a **draft law**. This draft is called a **bill**. The bill has clauses — specific rules — like *"This Act applies to children between 6 and 14 years"*, *"No fee shall be charged"*, *"Every school must have a teacher-pupil ratio of..."*.

**Step 3 — Introduction in Parliament.** The minister stands up in either Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha and asks for permission to introduce the bill. This is called the **First Reading**. Copies are distributed. RTE was first tabled in the **Rajya Sabha**.

**Step 4 — Standing Committee.** Most important bills are sent to a **Standing Committee** of MPs from different parties. They meet over weeks, study the bill, invite experts and citizens to give views, and write a report with recommendations. This is where most of the real **homework** happens.

**Step 5 — Second Reading.** The bill returns to the House. MPs debate it in general first. Then they go **clause by clause**. Amendments are moved. The House votes on each amendment. The bill that emerges may look quite different from the one introduced.

**Step 6 — Third Reading.** The full amended bill is voted on. If it passes, it moves to the other House.

**Step 7 — Repeat in the other House.** The Rajya Sabha (or Lok Sabha) goes through the same process. If both houses pass an identical version, the bill is ready. If they disagree, there can be a **joint sitting** — but that's rare.

**Step 8 — Presidential assent.** The bill is sent to the **President**. The President can sign it, hold it for some time, or send it back once for reconsideration. After assent, the bill is no longer a bill — it is an **Act**.

**Step 9 — Gazette notification.** The Act is published in the **Government Gazette**. From the date mentioned in the Act (sometimes immediately, sometimes later), it is **law**.

**Child:** That's nine steps! No wonder some bills take years.

**Mother:** Yes — and for good reason. RTE itself took **decades** from the original Constitutional idea (1950s Directive Principles) to actual law (2009). Some bills are blocked, modified, dropped, revived. The **Women's Reservation Bill** took 27 years from first being introduced to finally being passed in 2023. The process is slow because the **consequences are huge** — every law affects 140 crore Indians.

## What's the Difference Between the PM and the President?

**Child:** Maa, I always confuse these two. Both seem to be at the top of the country. Who actually runs India — the PM or the President?

**Mother:** Great question. The short answer: **the PM runs the country; the President is the constitutional head.** But let's go deeper, because the relationship is interesting.

**The President — Head of State.** The President is the **face** of the Indian Republic. When a foreign head of state visits India, they are received by the President. When new laws are signed, it's the President's signature. When the armed forces salute someone as Supreme Commander, that's the President. The President is the **symbol** of the nation — like the Queen / King in Britain.

But — and this is the key — **the President acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers** in almost all cases. The actual decisions about taxes, schools, war, peace, roads, and welfare are made by the PM and Cabinet. The President signs them.

**The Prime Minister — Head of Government.** The PM is the **engine room** of the government. The PM:
- Chairs the Cabinet meetings.
- Decides which minister gets which portfolio.
- Sets the political direction of the country.
- Represents India abroad in working terms (signing trade deals, attending G20, talking with world leaders).
- Answers to the Lok Sabha every day.

**Child:** So why have both?

**Mother:** Because of how parliamentary democracies work. We need a **head of state** — somebody who stands above party politics, who represents the **whole country**, and who provides **continuity**. The President is sworn in for **5 years** regardless of which party wins which election. Even if the Lok Sabha is dissolved, the President is still there. This is **stability**.

We also need a **head of government** — somebody who actually runs things, makes daily decisions, is answerable to the legislature. That's the PM.

**The British system has the same logic.** They have a **King** (head of state) and a **Prime Minister** (head of government). The King symbolises continuity; the PM runs the country.

There's also a third reason. In moments of crisis — say, when **no party has a majority** in the Lok Sabha after an election — somebody has to decide whom to invite to form the government. The President does this. It's a **discretionary power**. Or when a state government has collapsed and **President's Rule** has to be imposed (under Article 356), the President signs it on the advice of the Cabinet but with their own judgment of the situation.

**Child:** So the President is like the steady older relative, and the PM is the one running the household?

**Mother:** A nice way to put it. The President watches over things from above; the PM gets the actual work done. **Together** they form India's executive. They're not rivals — they're a **team** designed to balance stability with action.

## What Are 'Checks and Balances'?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter kept saying "checks and balances". I get that we have three organs — legislature, executive, judiciary — but what stops any one of them from going wrong?

**Mother:** This is the most important question in democratic design. The fear is exactly what you said — *what if one organ becomes too powerful?* So the Constitution builds in many checks. Let me show you how each one watches the other two.

**Legislature checks Executive.** Parliament makes laws — the executive has to obey them. Parliament approves the **budget** — without that, the executive cannot spend. Parliament holds **Question Hour** — ministers must answer. Parliament's **Standing Committees** and the **Public Accounts Committee** dig into every ministry's work. Parliament can pass a **no-confidence motion** and bring the whole government down. That's a lot of checks.

**Executive checks Legislature.** The executive can ask the President to **dissolve the Lok Sabha** early and call new elections. The Cabinet decides what bills come up for debate. The President can **send a bill back** once for reconsideration. The PM can call the legislative agenda. There's some pushback in this direction too.

**Judiciary checks both.** This is the powerful one. The **Supreme Court** and **High Courts** can:
- **Strike down a law** passed by Parliament if it violates the Constitution. This is called **judicial review**.
- **Strike down an executive order** if it violates fundamental rights.
- **Settle disputes** between the Centre and a state, between two states, between citizens and the government.
- **Hold the government in contempt** if it disobeys court orders.

In 1973, in the famous **Kesavananda Bharati case**, the Supreme Court ruled that even Parliament cannot amend the **basic structure** of the Constitution — features like democracy, secularism, federalism, judicial review. That ruling protects India from any government, however powerful, overturning its democratic foundations.

**Legislature and executive check the Judiciary.** Judges are **appointed** through a process involving the executive (the **collegium system**, where senior judges recommend and the executive appoints). Their **salaries** are protected — but their conduct can be questioned. A judge can be **impeached** by Parliament for proved misconduct or incapacity (this has been attempted, though never completed). And Parliament can **change a law** if the court has interpreted it in a way it didn't intend.

**Child:** So they're all watching each other?

**Mother:** Yes — and that's the genius of it. **No organ is supreme.** Not the PM, not Parliament, not even the Supreme Court. The **Constitution is supreme**, and each organ keeps the others within the Constitution.

History has tested this. During the **Emergency of 1975-77**, the executive and legislature briefly overpowered the judiciary, fundamental rights were suspended, and democracy almost broke. But the system **corrected itself** — the next election brought a new government, the judiciary reasserted independence, and the rules were tightened. India remembers that period as a warning. It's why we treasure checks and balances today.

**Child:** And what's the people's role?

**Mother:** The biggest check of all. **Every five years, the people vote.** The Lok Sabha that displeases its voters gets thrown out. The MP that didn't show up for sittings loses her seat. The party that broke its promises is replaced. **Elections are the ultimate check** — over and above the formal ones inside government.

## What Should I Take From This Chapter?

**Child:** Maa, so many institutions and rules and processes. If I had to remember just a few things, what should they be?

**Mother:** Let me give you the small list — the **essence**.

**First — India is a parliamentary democracy.** That means the people elect representatives (MPs and MLAs), and those representatives choose the government. The government **stays in power as long as it has the legislature's confidence**. Lose that confidence — and you're out. This is fundamentally different from a presidential system where a leader serves a fixed term regardless of mood.

**Second — Parliament has three parts: President, Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha.** The Lok Sabha is the people's house. The Rajya Sabha is the states' house. The President is the constitutional head. Together they make laws and supervise the executive.

**Third — Power is shared, not held by one person.** Even the **Prime Minister** — the most visible leader — does not act alone. She works with the Cabinet (collective responsibility), is answerable to the Lok Sabha (Question Hour), is bound by the Constitution (interpreted by the Supreme Court), and must face voters every five years.

**Fourth — Lawmaking is hard on purpose.** A bill goes through nine steps before becoming law. This **slowness is a feature, not a bug.** When laws affect a billion people, you want them examined, debated, and refined — not rushed.

**Fifth — The executive answers to the legislature.** Question Hour, Standing Committees, the PAC, the no-confidence motion — these are the daily, weekly, monthly tools that keep ministers honest. The PM is **not above** Parliament; she **comes from** Parliament and has to face it every day.

**Sixth — Power is also shared across the federation.** The Union handles defence, foreign policy, currency. States handle police, public health, agriculture. Education is shared. This is the **Union List–State List–Concurrent List** division.

**Seventh — The Judiciary is the umpire.** When the legislature or the executive crosses constitutional lines, the courts step in. **Checks and balances** keep all three organs in their lanes.

**Child:** And what should I do with all this?

**Mother:** Three things, beta.

**One — know your representatives.** Find out who your **MP and MLA** are. Look them up. Read what they say. Hold them in your mind as **real people** working on your behalf.

**Two — watch what Parliament actually does.** Don't just watch the shouting on TV. Read about bills being passed, the debates that happen, the committees that report. Follow PRS Legislative Research (prsindia.org) for plain-language summaries.

**Three — when you turn 18, vote.** And then vote in **every single election** — Lok Sabha, Vidhan Sabha, panchayat. Every vote tells the system: *I am watching. I am part of this.* That is the deepest check of all.

The parliamentary system is not perfect. Sessions get disrupted, important bills get rushed, productivity falls. But it is **ours** — chosen by our founders, lived through by our grandparents, and now in your hands. Whether it stays strong or weakens **depends on what citizens like you do**.

**Child:** That's a lot of responsibility, Maa.

**Mother:** It is. But it's also a lot of **power**. In a parliamentary democracy, the citizen is not a subject of the state — the **citizen is the state**. Don't ever forget that.
