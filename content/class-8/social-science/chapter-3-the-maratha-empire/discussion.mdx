## Who Was Shivaji Really?

**Child:** Maa, was Shivaji actually as great as everyone says, or is it because he won?

**Mother:** That is a sharp question, and I'm glad you asked it. Winners do get to write history. So let's strip away the legend for a minute and ask — what is *actually* impressive about Shivaji, even if you set aside all the patriotic feeling?

**1. He started with nothing.**

Think about who he was at 16. His father Shahji was a Maratha noble who served the Bijapur Sultanate. His mother Jijabai raised him at a jagir at Pune. He had no army. He had no treasury. He had no king's permission to do anything. He had a few trusted friends, a piece of his father's land, and an idea.

Forty years later, at his coronation, he was sovereign of his own kingdom, with his own navy, his own coinage, his own era of dating, and a reputation that had reached as far as London. Most people in history who build something that big inherit something. Shivaji inherited a fragmentary jagir and a vision from his mother.

**2. He fought *much* bigger powers and did not lose.**

He defeated the army of the Bijapur Sultanate (Afzal Khan). He humiliated a Mughal noble at the head of a huge army (Shaista Khan). He sacked the richest port city of the Mughal Empire (Surat) — twice. He escaped from the personal custody of Aurangzeb himself. By the standards of the time, those are absurd achievements.

**3. He governed well.**

This is the part that people who repeat his name don't always know. He was not just a warrior. He paid his officials a salary instead of giving away hereditary jagirs — which kept his nobles dependent on him rather than on local landlords. He transferred officials so none of them became local kingpins. He had a cabinet of eight ministers — a Prime Minister, a Finance Minister, a Foreign Minister, a Chief Justice, an Intelligence Chief, and so on. He paid pensions to the widows of fallen soldiers. He gave their sons jobs.

If you read his letter about the navy timber — telling his officials *not* to cut down mango and jackfruit trees because the villagers love them — you see a king who actually thought about a peasant's grief over a single tree. That is not normal.

**4. He had a vision that outlasted him.**

He called it **Swarajya**. The literal meaning is "self-rule." But for Shivaji it meant more — political independence, economic self-reliance, cultural confidence, all together. Two hundred years later, Bal Gangadhar Tilak picked up the same word and said, "Swarajya is my birthright, and I shall have it." Mahatma Gandhi picked it up after that. The Indian freedom struggle was, in a real sense, the second act of Shivaji's project.

**So why is he so beloved?**

Because all four of those things together — the underdog start, the military genius, the careful administration, the long-term vision — is a rare combination. Most great rulers in history have one or two. Shivaji had all four.

**But is he above criticism?**

No. He killed Afzal Khan with concealed weapons after inviting him to a parley — by chivalric standards of the time, this is morally complicated, even if it was self-defence. The sack of Surat hurt a lot of ordinary merchants, not just the Mughal exchequer. Later Marathas, like during their Bengal campaign, did genuine cruelty.

The honest version is: Shivaji is great because of *real* achievements, not because of fairy tales. You can love him without believing every legend.

**Child:** Did he ever lose anything?

**Mother:** Yes. He lost the **Treaty of Purandar** to Jai Singh in 1665 — he had to surrender a big chunk of his kingdom and send his son Sambhaji to Mughal service. That defeat is what eventually led him to the trap at Agra. So no, he was not undefeated. He was defeated and recovered. That is more interesting than "never lost."

The myth makes him perfect. The history makes him human. The human is more inspiring.

## Why Did the Marathas Become So Big So Fast?

**Child:** Maa, how did the Marathas go from a small Deccan kingdom under Shivaji to controlling almost the whole of India in less than a century?

**Mother:** That speed is one of the most dramatic stories in Indian history. Let me give you the main reasons.

**1. The Mughal Empire was cracking up.**

This is the most important single fact. Aurangzeb spent the last 26 years of his life — from 1681 until his death in 1707 — fighting the Marathas in the Deccan. He brought the whole Mughal court and army south with him. The treasury was drained. The army was exhausted. The provinces in the north were neglected. By the time he died, the Mughal Empire was wealthy on paper but hollow in practice.

When Aurangzeb died, his successors fought each other. **Bahadur Shah I, Jahandar Shah, Farrukhsiyar** — short reigns, palace coups, court murders. **Nadir Shah** of Persia sacked Delhi in 1739 and took the Peacock Throne and the Koh-i-Noor. The Mughal emperor became a name on coins, not a real ruler.

The Marathas were the *opposite*. While the Mughals were imploding, the Marathas were forged in the fire of the 27-year war. By 1707 they had a generation of battle-hardened commanders, a confederate structure that could move fast across long distances, and a sense of momentum.

**2. The Maratha cavalry was extraordinary.**

The Marathas, especially under Bajirao I, used **light cavalry** in a way nobody else in India did. They travelled fast. They lived off the land. They didn't depend on heavy supply trains. They could appear in central India one week and Rajasthan the next.

In Bajirao's words: *"Let us strike at the trunk of the withering tree. The branches will fall by themselves."* By "trunk" he meant Delhi. By "branches" he meant the Mughal provinces. He was right — the Maratha strategy was to bypass small Mughal garrisons and threaten the centre.

**3. Chauth and sardeshmukhi were a clever financial weapon.**

Instead of conquering and annexing every territory — which is slow and expensive — the Marathas demanded chauth (25%) and sardeshmukhi (10%) from regions they would *not* directly govern. The local ruler kept his throne; the Marathas kept his money; in return, the Marathas protected him.

This is fast expansion without administrative cost. It is also a kind of tribute system that the Mughals themselves had used for centuries — the Marathas just took over the franchise.

Over time, many tribute-paying regions just *became* Maratha. The line between "we collect tax here" and "we rule here" blurred.

**4. The confederacy structure suited a vast country.**

A single centralised empire ruled from one capital — like Shivaji's original kingdom — is fine when the territory is the size of Maharashtra. It does not work when you are governing from Peshawar to Tamil Nadu.

The Maratha confederacy solved this by having local chiefs — the **Scindias at Gwalior, Holkars at Indore, Gaekwads at Baroda, Bhonsles at Nagpur** — running their regions semi-autonomously, with the Peshwa at Pune as the chief coordinator and the Chhatrapati at Satara as the symbolic head.

This was less tidy than the Mughal system but much more flexible. Local chiefs could make local decisions without writing to Pune. They knew their territory. They could mobilise quickly.

**5. The Marathas attracted talent.**

A lot of Indians wanted what the Marathas offered: a return to Indian self-government, careers based on ability, fewer barriers. The Maratha military and administration drew **Marathas, Brahmins, Kayasthas, traders, even Muslims** willing to serve the Maratha cause. Nana Phadnavis, the great statesman, was a Chitpavan Brahmin. The Scindias, Holkars, Gaekwads were of different communities. This was a movement, not just one community in arms.

**6. They learned from their enemies.**

When the Marathas saw European disciplined infantry and artillery were superior, they didn't shrug — they hired French and Portuguese officers, raised modern battalions, bought modern guns. Mahadji Scindia's army by 1780 looked very different from Shivaji's. The Marathas were adaptable.

**So why didn't this last?**

The same things that made them strong eventually weakened them.

The confederacy structure meant the regional chiefs — Scindia, Holkar, Bhonsle, Gaekwad — could and *did* fight each other. By the early 19th century, Scindia and Holkar were openly at war. The British, very deliberately, allied with one against the other.

The light cavalry strategy that worked against Mughal feudal armies did not work against British disciplined infantry with field artillery. Battles like Assaye (1803) showed the Marathas couldn't beat the British in pitched battle.

And the loss at Panipat in 1761 had killed an entire generation of leaders in one afternoon. The recovery was real but never quite complete.

So the Marathas rose fast because of timing, structure, talent, and audacity. They fell because the same structure could not hold together against a disciplined, united, technologically superior enemy. That is the rhythm of empires.

## What Was Special About Maratha Statecraft?

**Child:** Maa, you said Shivaji built modern statecraft in 17th-century India. What does that even mean?

**Mother:** Good question. Most kingdoms before Shivaji ran on **personal loyalty** — the king gave land to a noble, the noble gave service back, and what happened on the land was the noble's problem. The king got tax revenue when the noble felt like sending it, soldiers when the noble felt like raising them, justice when the noble decided to deliver it.

Shivaji tried something different. Let me list what made him different.

**1. He paid salaries.**

Shivaji's officials were paid a salary in cash from the state treasury — like government servants today. They were *not* given a piece of land to administer for life. This sounds small, but it changes everything. A salaried official depends on the king. A landed official can become a rival.

This was rare in the Indian context. Most kingdoms — Mughal, Bijapur, Vijayanagara — distributed jagirs widely. Shivaji minimised them.

**2. He rotated officials.**

He moved his officials from place to place. So no official stayed long enough in one district to build up a private power base, marry into the local elite, and start running things for himself. This is the same principle used by modern civil services like the IAS — don't let any officer become a local fiefdom.

**3. He had a written cabinet.**

The **Ashta Pradhan Mandal** — Peshwa, Amatya, Sachiv, Mantri, Senapati, Sumant, Nyayadhish, Panditrao — was not just a list of ministers. Each had a defined portfolio. The Senapati was the army chief. The Amatya handled finance. The Sumant handled foreign affairs. There was a chief justice. There was an intelligence chief. There was even a head of religious affairs.

This is essentially the structure of a modern cabinet. Compare with how Mughal nobility worked — flexible, overlapping, often a single noble was governor, general, and judge in his province.

**4. He distinguished civilian and military authority.**

Forts had a commander. Districts had a separate civil head. Tax-collection had its own chain. This separation is a feature of modern governance — and it was unusual.

**5. He treated soldiers as state employees.**

The **bargir** category of cavalry — soldier who got his horse and equipment from the state — meant the state owned the means of war. Compare with the Mughal mansabdari, where nobles raised their own troops and the emperor depended on those nobles.

He also paid **pensions to widows** of fallen soldiers and gave their sons jobs. That is something like a modern welfare state, in the 17th century.

**6. He cared about the people.**

This is the most striking part to me. He wrote letters to his own officials warning them *not to oppress villagers*, not to grab a blade of grass, not to cut down mango and jackfruit trees because the villagers grieve. Most kings in history did not bother. Shivaji bothered. Whether he succeeded everywhere is debatable. But the intention is documented.

His finance minister Ramachandrapant Amatya later wrote the *Ajnapatra* (1715) — a kind of constitutional handbook of Maratha governance. Reading it, you see a serious political theory: forts are the backbone of the state; the king must consult ministers; foreign trade must be controlled; European merchants must be watched carefully because they are "obstinate people" who want to take over the country.

That Ajnapatra is from 1715. The British East India Company would consolidate its rule a century later. Maratha statecraft was already warning about exactly this.

**7. He invented his own symbols.**

Royal seal in Sanskrit, not Persian. Coins in Devanagari, not Persian script. His own era of dating, the Rajyabhisheka shaka. A new dictionary of Sanskrit equivalents for Persian administrative terms — the *Rajya-Vyavahara-Kosha*. He was deliberately *un-Persianising* the language of state.

This is a cultural-political act. Language matters. The language of administration shapes the elite who serve. Shivaji wanted an elite that thought in Sanskrit and Marathi, not in Persian. He wanted Indian self-confidence on the seal of the state.

**So what's the upshot?**

Shivaji ran a state that, in 1670, had features many other states only acquired in the 19th century — salaried bureaucracy, defined ministries, rotation of officials, state welfare for veterans' families, separation of civilian and military.

He didn't get all of these from anywhere. He invented and assembled them. Other Indian kingdoms had some of them, separately. Shivaji put them together.

When historians say he "modernised" the Indian state, that's what they mean.

## Why Did the Marathas Lose at Panipat?

**Child:** Maa, the Marathas were the strongest power in India in 1760. They had Delhi, they had the Mughal emperor, they had a huge army. Then a year later they lost catastrophically at Panipat. What happened?

**Mother:** Panipat in 1761 is one of the saddest days in Indian history. Let me explain what went wrong — there are many reasons, not just one.

**Background.**

By 1758, the Marathas under Nanasaheb Peshwa had pushed all the way to **Attock and Peshawar** in the northwest — territory that today is in Pakistan. This was the high water mark.

But there was a new enemy. **Ahmad Shah Abdali** — a Pashtun chief who had taken control of Afghanistan after Nadir Shah's death — had been raiding north India for years. Abdali was an extremely skilled commander with a battle-hardened army of Afghans, Persians, and Indian Muslims aligned with him.

When the Marathas pushed his garrisons out of Punjab, Abdali decided to settle the matter. He marched into India with his full army and his Indian allies — the **Rohilla Afghans of north India, Najib-ud-Daulah, and the Nawab of Awadh, Shuja-ud-Daulah**.

The Maratha response was led by **Sadashivrao Bhau**, cousin of the Peshwa, and **Vishwasrao**, the Peshwa's eldest son. They marched north with a huge army.

The two sides met at **Panipat** — north of Delhi — in late 1760. Both armies dug in. There was a long stalemate. Finally, on **14 January 1761**, the Marathas attacked. By the end of the day, the Maratha army was destroyed.

Why did they lose? Several reasons together.

**1. They were far from home, deep in winter, with bad logistics.**

The Marathas had marched over 1,500 km north from their Deccan supply base. Their grain trains were stretched thin. Abdali's allies cut their supply lines. By the time of the battle, Maratha soldiers were starving.

**2. They had too many non-combatants.**

The Maratha army marched north with families, pilgrims, civilian followers, baggage trains — perhaps 100,000 people of whom only a fraction were soldiers. This was partly a political decision (showing the Marathas were a power moving across India, not just raiders) and partly because much of the army was made up of feudal contingents that brought their families. But it slowed everyone down and made supply harder.

**3. They had few local allies.**

Abdali had assembled a coalition of Indian Muslim powers — Rohillas, Najib-ud-Daulah, Shuja-ud-Daulah of Awadh. The Marathas, in contrast, had no major local ally in north India. The Rajputs stayed neutral; the Jats were lukewarm; the Sikhs were not yet a major force. So the Marathas fought alone against a coalition.

**Why didn't they have allies?** Partly because of the way they had behaved as overlords. They had been hard taskmasters on the chiefs of north India — demanding tribute, interfering in succession disputes, sometimes plundering. The local powers were not eager to risk their forces for Maratha victory.

**4. Their cavalry strategy didn't work against Abdali's army.**

Maratha light cavalry was brilliant at raid, manoeuvre, and pursuit. But at Panipat, they were trapped in a fixed defensive position. They couldn't manoeuvre. They had to fight a set-piece battle against an enemy who had as much cavalry and better infantry.

When they finally attacked on 14 January, they did well at first — they broke the Afghan centre. But Abdali kept a strong reserve. He committed it at the right moment. The Maratha line broke. Then a cavalry charge from Maratha reserves was repulsed and turned into a rout.

**5. The leadership was killed early.**

Vishwasrao, the Peshwa's son, was killed by a stray bullet early in the battle. Sadashivrao Bhau, seeing this, dismounted from his elephant — partly out of grief, partly to lead a foot charge. With the Bhau no longer visible on his elephant, the rumour spread that he was dead. Discipline collapsed. The army broke.

**6. Sheer numbers.**

Tens of thousands of Maratha soldiers died on the battlefield. Many more were killed in the rout afterwards, or in the cold winter march back south. Almost every major Maratha family in Maharashtra lost someone at Panipat. The grief was personal across the whole region.

**What was lost?**

The Peshwa Nanasaheb died of grief a few months later. Most of the senior Maratha command was dead. The expansion to the northwest was over. The Marathas would never again advance north with such confidence.

But the Marathas were not finished.

**The recovery.**

Madhavrao I — Nanasaheb's son — became Peshwa at 17. In ten years, he stabilised the empire. He defeated rebellious tributaries. He restored Maratha control over central India. Mahadji Shinde (Scindia) — who had survived Panipat with a permanent limp — built a new disciplined Maratha army with European training. In 1771, just ten years after Panipat, Mahadji Shinde **recaptured Delhi**.

So Panipat was a disaster — but not the end. The Marathas remained a major power for another half-century. What ended them was not Panipat but the Anglo-Maratha Wars and their own internal quarrels.

**What can we learn?**

Panipat is a warning about overextension. About the difference between brilliant raid-and-retreat and the harder game of holding territory. About the danger of fighting without local allies in a far country. About what happens when the leader becomes a target.

It is also, in a strange way, a story of resilience. The Marathas were almost finished — and they came back. That tells us something about the strength of the institutions Shivaji had built. The state was bigger than any one battle.

## What's the Maratha Legacy Today?

**Child:** Maa, the Marathas lost the wars to the British. So is it still relevant today? Or is it just history?

**Mother:** It is very relevant. Let me give you concrete legacies.

**1. The idea of Swarajya.**

This is the biggest. Two centuries after Shivaji's coronation, Bal Gangadhar Tilak revived his memory deliberately. Tilak started the **Shivaji Jayanti** festival in Pune in 1895 — a public celebration of Shivaji's birth as a way of building national consciousness under British rule. He used Shivaji's example to argue: *Indians have ruled themselves before, can rule themselves now, and have a duty to demand self-rule.*

When Tilak said, "**Swarajya is my birthright and I shall have it**," he was using Shivaji's word. He was citing Shivaji's example. The Indian National Congress adopted "Purna Swaraj" — complete self-rule — as its formal goal in 1929. The freedom struggle was, in a real sense, Shivaji's project completed.

**2. The political map.**

When the Marathas dominated India in the mid-18th century, they more or less held the *outline* of what would become modern India — from the Punjab to Tamil Nadu, from Gujarat to Bengal. The British inherited this. When India became independent in 1947, the territory it inherited was substantially the territory the Marathas had unified a century earlier.

The Marathas kept the idea of *pan-Indian political space* alive between the Mughals and the British. Without them, the Mughal collapse could have left India in many disconnected kingdoms, more like the way the Ottoman collapse fragmented the Middle East.

**3. Administrative continuity.**

When the British took over Maratha territory, they kept much of the **revenue system, the village structure, the police organisation, and many of the actual officials**. The Indian Civil Service grew out of the Mughal-Maratha administrative tradition. India today still has district administration with patterns that go back through the British, through the Marathas, to Shivaji's careful design.

**4. Family names and family identity.**

We talked about this in class. **Scindia, Holkar, Gaekwad, Bhonsle, Pawar, Patil, Deshmukh, Deshpande, Kulkarni** — these surnames carry Maratha history. Famous modern Indian politicians and industrialists carry them. Some princely descendants of the Maratha houses are still active in public life — for example the Scindia family at Gwalior.

**5. The forts.**

Maharashtra alone has hundreds of preserved forts. Many are major heritage sites today. Trekking to them — **Sinhagad, Rajgad, Raigad, Pratapgad, Lohagad** — is one of the great cultural activities of Maharashtra. People grow up climbing these forts. The forts are part of daily memory.

**6. Carnatic music and Bharatanatyam.**

This may surprise you. The Marathas who ruled Thanjavur in Tamil Nadu — especially the brilliant **Serfoji II** — were great patrons of music and dance. **Modern Carnatic music** as we know it took its shape in the 18th-century Thanjavur Maratha court. The **Bharatanatyam** classical dance form also matured under their patronage. So when you watch a Bharatanatyam performance today, you are watching something that took its modern form under a Maratha king.

**7. Ahilyabai Holkar's temples.**

Ahilyabai rebuilt or supported temples all over India — **Kashi Vishwanath at Varanasi, Somnath in Gujarat, Kedarnath, Rameshwaram, Ujjain, Mathura, Ayodhya, Puri, Dwarka**. When you visit these temples today, in many cases, the structure you see is *because of her*. She also revived the **Maheshwari handloom** industry that is still active in Madhya Pradesh.

**8. The navy.**

The Indian Navy today traces some of its institutional memory to the Maratha navy. **Indian Navy Day is celebrated on 4 December**, which commemorates the 1971 Karachi strike — but the historical tradition of Indian sea power that the Navy claims includes the Marathas, especially **Kanhoji Angre**. There are Navy ships named after Maratha admirals.

**9. Indian cinema and literature.**

Marathi literature, Hindi cinema, Indian historical fiction — Shivaji, Sambhaji, Tarabai, Bajirao, Mastani, Madhavrao have all been the subjects of bestselling novels, hit films, popular TV serials. **Bajirao Mastani**, **Tanhaji**, **Panipat**, **Manikarnika** — recent Bollywood films have brought Maratha history to a new generation. *Shivaji Sawant's* Marathi novel *Chhava* on Sambhaji has been read for decades.

**10. Public memory and pride.**

Statues, road names, airports, ports, universities, currency notes — Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj appears across India. The **Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Terminus (CSMT)** in Mumbai is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The **Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj International Airport** in Mumbai handles tens of millions of passengers a year. He is one of the most universally honoured figures in modern Indian public space.

**Why does all this matter?**

A nation needs **stories of its own greatness** that are *true*. Stories that show *we have done difficult things before, we can do them again*. Shivaji and the Marathas give Indians a story like that — not borrowed, not romantic, not fabricated, but real. A boy from Pune who built a kingdom against the largest empire on earth. A woman who held off Aurangzeb when her husband died. A queen who rebuilt half the temples of India. An admiral who made the European navies pay him for the right to sail in his waters.

These are not just historical facts. They are part of the inner equipment of being an Indian today.

## What Should I Take From This?

**Child:** Maa, so what should I really *take* from this chapter, as a kid?

**Mother:** Three things, I think.

**1. Big things start small.**

Shivaji was 16 when he started. Not 16 with a kingdom; 16 with an idea and a few friends. When you think your problem is too big, or that you're too young, remember that. The first fort he captured was not a great fortress. It was a neglected hill stronghold that nobody was watching. Real things often begin with one small thing nobody else is paying attention to.

**2. Geography matters.**

He didn't fight in the open plains where Mughal cavalry was supreme. He fought in the **Western Ghats** — the mountains he knew. He didn't build a navy to fight European ships in open ocean. He built coastal forts where his knowledge of the rocks and tides gave him the advantage. He used what he had.

In life this is also true. Don't try to fight every battle in the enemy's strongest ground. Find your ghats. Find your terrain.

**3. Take care of your people.**

The letters Shivaji wrote to his officials — about not cutting villagers' mango trees, about not grabbing even a blade of grass — those are not the words of a typical conqueror. He understood that a kingdom is not its army or its treasury. It is its people. Strong kingdoms are built on the goodwill of villagers, not on the fear of nobles.

A small lesson for you: in any group, in any team, in any classroom — the people you don't see, the people whose names you don't bother to learn, the people whose effort you don't acknowledge — *they* are the reason anything works. Pay attention to them. Treat them well. That is how leaders are made, not how rulers are made.

**Child:** And the Swarajya idea — is that still alive?

**Mother:** That is the deepest question.

Political Swarajya we have — we are an independent country. That part is done. But **economic Swarajya** — making our own things, owning our own technology, not being dependent on outside money — that is still a work in progress. **Cultural Swarajya** — being confident in our own languages, our own arts, our own thinking, without needing approval from the West — that is still a work in progress. **Personal Swarajya** — being master of your own choices, your own attention, your own time — *that* is the hardest of all, and it is the daily work of a lifetime.

So yes, Swarajya is still alive. It just looks different now. In Shivaji's time it meant building a kingdom. In Tilak's time it meant winning freedom from the British. In our time it means building India. In your time, *child*, it will mean something you will have to define for yourself.

But the word is still the same. **Swarajya.** Self-rule. That you decide for yourself. That you stand on your own feet. That you do not bow to any power that does not deserve your bowing.

That is what a sixteen-year-old in Pune four hundred years ago wanted. That is what you can still want today.

Keep climbing those forts in your head, child. There are always more to capture.
