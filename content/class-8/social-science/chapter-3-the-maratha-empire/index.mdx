## A Boy from Pune Who Changed India

In the year **1630**, a son was born to a Maratha nobleman named **Shahji Bhonsle** and his wife **Jijabai**, in a small hill fort near Pune. The boy was named **Shivaji**.

At the time, the Deccan was a battlefield. The **Mughal Empire** under Shah Jahan was pushing south. Three sultanates — **Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, and Golconda** — were fighting each other for survival. Maratha chiefs served whichever sultan paid them. They had no kingdom of their own. They had no flag of their own. They were warriors-for-hire on their own soil.

Within his lifetime, this boy from Pune would:
- Build the **Maratha kingdom** out of nothing
- Defeat generals sent by the mighty Mughal Empire
- Escape from Aurangzeb's prison hidden in a basket of sweets
- Be crowned **Chhatrapati** — Lord of the Umbrella — in a sovereign Vedic ceremony
- Create one of the first Indian **navies** in centuries
- Inspire a movement that, fifty years after his death, would briefly control **Delhi itself**

This is the story of **how the Marathas rose** — and how they shaped India.

## Who Were the Marathas?

The **Marathas** are a people native to the **Deccan plateau**, specifically the region we now call **Maharashtra**. They are identified above all by their language — **Marathi** — which has a continuous **literary history since the 12th century**.

For centuries before Shivaji, Maharashtra had been ruled by others. In the **13th century**, the **Yadava dynasty** ruled most of the region, with their capital at **Devagiri** (today's Daulatabad). The Yadavas were overcome by the **Khilji Sultanate** in the early 14th century. After that, Maharashtra passed under various sultanates of the Deccan.

But while political rulers changed, **cultural traditions continued**. Between the 7th and 17th centuries, Maharashtra became one of the great homes of the **Bhakti movement** — a path of personal devotion to the divine, in songs and poetry composed in the language of the people.

The Marathi Bhakti saints became famous across India:
- **Dnyaneshwar** (13th century) — translated the Bhagavad Gita into Marathi as the *Dnyaneshwari*
- **Namdev** (13th-14th century) — wrote bhajans still sung today
- **Eknath** (16th century) — major commentator and devotional poet
- **Tukaram** (17th century) — wrote thousands of *abhangas* in simple Marathi
- **Ramdas** (17th century) — focused on social organisation and political awareness; considered a spiritual guide to Shivaji's circle

This long Bhakti tradition gave Maratha society something subtle but powerful — **a sense of shared identity, a confidence in their own language, a tradition of fearless ethical thinking**. When Shivaji rose, he was not building from nothing. He was building on a 400-year cultural foundation.

By the **early 17th century**, Maharashtra was politically chaotic. The Marathas served the Deccan sultans as soldiers and tax-collectors. Some Maratha chiefs had small **jagirs** — pieces of land granted by a king in exchange for service. But none had succeeded in carving out a kingdom of their own.

Until Shivaji.

## The Young Shivaji and the Dream of Swarajya

Shivaji's father **Shahji** served the Deccan sultanates and was usually far from home. So Shivaji grew up at **Pune**, raised by his mother **Jijabai** and a circle of trusted officials. He received the values of a sovereign king — the **Ramayana** and the **Mahabharata**, the deeds of past Indian rulers, the songs of the Bhakti saints — and a hard practical education in administration and warfare.

The Pune region around him was suffering. The Deccan sultans were endlessly at war with each other; their soldiers oppressed villagers; the common people had no protection. Jijabai is said to have told her son again and again: *this land is yours; these people are yours; rule them well*.

At **just 16 years old**, in 1646, Shivaji began his career. His first acts were small but bold — he captured **neglected and unoccupied forts** in the hills around Pune and strengthened their defences. He began to gather Maratha warriors who would fight for a cause, not for pay.

He had a name for what he was building. He called it **Swarajya** — literally, "self-rule" or "the rule of one's own." Swarajya was not just political independence. It meant **rule by Indians, for Indians, according to Indian values** — economic, cultural, and spiritual independence rolled into one. It was a vision that would echo three centuries later in the freedom movement.

## Forts, Guerrilla Warfare, and the Maratha Way of War

Shivaji's territory was not flat. It was the **Western Ghats** — a long range of steep, forested, river-cut mountains running parallel to India's west coast. This terrain shaped everything about Maratha warfare.

A large invading army moved slowly through the Ghats. Its supply lines stretched thin. Its soldiers got lost in jungles, ambushed at narrow passes, exhausted by hill-climbs. The defenders, who knew every track and stream, could appear and disappear like ghosts.

Shivaji built his strategy around this geography. The two pillars of his system were:

**1. Forts.** Shivaji eventually controlled **hundreds of forts** — built on hilltops, isolated by cliffs, supplied with their own water and grain. His finance minister **Ramachandrapant Amatya** would later write in his treatise *Ajnapatra* (1715):

> "Forts are the core of the state. In their absence, the land gets devastated in the face of an invasion. ... This kingdom was created by the late great master from forts alone. ... A great enemy like Aurangzeb invaded, conquered great empires like Bijapur and Bhaganagar — but it was due to forts that the Maratha state survived despite decades of onslaught."

Important Maratha forts included **Raigad** (the eventual capital), **Pratapgad, Purandar, Sinhagad, Rajgad, Torna**. Each was both a refuge and a launching pad.

**2. Guerrilla warfare.** When the **Bijapur Sultanate** sent the veteran general **Afzal Khan** with a large army to crush him, Shivaji did not meet the army in the open. Instead he invited Afzal Khan to a one-on-one meeting at the foot of **Pratapgad** fort, in thick forest. There, in close combat, Shivaji killed Afzal Khan — historians believe with the small claw-shaped weapon called the **wagh nakh** ("tiger's claw"). Meanwhile his troops, hidden in the mountains above, attacked the leaderless Bijapuri army from every side and routed it.

Later, when the Mughal noble **Shaista Khan** invaded with a huge army and occupied Pune itself, Shivaji slipped into Shaista Khan's own camp at night with a small force and attacked his quarters. The Khan barely escaped — losing a few fingers — and fled Maharashtra. This was a daring raid that historians compare to a modern-day **surgical strike**.

In retaliation for Shaista Khan's three years of devastation, Shivaji **sacked Surat** — the wealthy port city of the Mughal Empire in modern Gujarat — and carried off treasure worth almost **one crore of rupees**, an enormous sum. Notably, he gave strict instructions to spare religious places and the home of **Mohandas Parekh**, a known philanthropist. The story reached Europe and appeared in the *London Gazette*.

## The Sea Wall: The Maratha Navy

As Shivaji's kingdom reached India's **west coast**, he made a decision that no Indian ruler of his time had made — he built a **navy**.

This was a remarkable step. The **Bijapur Sultanate** had merchant ships but no real naval force. Even the **Mughal Empire** used the sea only in limited ways. The dominant naval powers along the Konkan coast were **European** — the Portuguese, the Dutch, the British, the French.

In **1657**, Shivaji founded the **Maratha navy**. He built coastal forts at **Sindhudurg, Vijaydurg, Suvarnadurg** and others — fortresses that rose straight out of the sea. He commissioned ships, recruited sailors from the fishing communities of the Konkan coast, and began to challenge European dominance of Indian waters.

In 1665, four of his ships even reached **Muscat** in present-day Oman. The Sultan of Oman initially seized them, but when he heard that Shivaji's navy contained "more than a hundred ships," he hurriedly retreated his fleet into the harbour. The reputation of the Maratha navy had crossed the Indian Ocean.

A generation later, in the early 18th century, the brilliant Maratha admiral **Kanhoji Angre** would lead this navy to victory after victory against European ships — turning the Marathas into one of the few Asian powers to seriously contest European sea power. The Europeans, frustrated, called Angre a "pirate." History knows better.

## Agra, the Basket of Sweets, and the Coronation

Sacking Surat had been a stinging insult to Mughal prestige. Aurangzeb responded by sending the formidable Rajput general **Jai Singh** of Amber. Jai Singh besieged the fort of **Purandar** and forced Shivaji to a treaty (1665). Shivaji had to surrender a large part of his kingdom and agree to send his elder son **Sambhaji** into Mughal service.

Jai Singh then persuaded Shivaji to visit Aurangzeb's court at **Agra**. But when Shivaji arrived, he was deliberately placed in a low rank — made to stand behind a Mughal general he had earlier defeated. Furious at the public insult, Shivaji stormed out of the court. Aurangzeb placed him under **house arrest** (1666).

Shivaji's escape became legendary. He began sending **baskets of fruits and sweets** to holy men and Mughal nobles as gifts. The guards searched them at first; over weeks, they grew careless. One day, Shivaji and Sambhaji **hid themselves inside the baskets** and were carried out of the prison. They made their way back to Maharashtra in disguise, travelling for many days through hostile country. Aurangzeb never captured Shivaji again.

In **1674**, at his great mountain fortress of **Raigad**, Shivaji was formally crowned with full **Vedic rites**. His title was **Shri Raja Shiva Chhatrapati** — Sovereign Lord of the Umbrella. Like some past Indian kings, he started his own era of dating, the **Rajyabhisheka shaka** ("coronation era"). He minted **gold and copper coins** in his own name using the **Devanagari** script, replacing the dominant Persian.

The coronation was not merely ceremonial. It was a constitutional act. After centuries of subjection to outside rulers, an **Indian king sat on an Indian throne in the Deccan**, claiming sovereignty in the language of his own people. Indians beyond Maharashtra noticed. The Bundela prince **Chhatrasal** was so inspired that he carved out an independent kingdom of **Bundelkhand**. The Hindi poet **Bhushan** travelled from north India to Maharashtra just to meet Shivaji and compose poems in his praise.

In 1677, Shivaji marched south on his famous **dakshina-digvijaya** — "conquest of the south" — extending Maratha influence into present-day northern Tamil Nadu and parts of Karnataka. This southern strategic depth would later prove crucial when the Mughals invaded the Deccan.

In **1680**, a fever ended Shivaji's life. He was fifty years old.

## After Shivaji: Sambhaji, Aurangzeb, and the 27-Year War

Shivaji had two sons — **Sambhaji** and **Rajaram**. After Shivaji's death, **Sambhaji** became Chhatrapati.

Aurangzeb now saw his opening. Determined to crush both the Marathas and the Deccan sultanates once and for all, he marched south himself with the entire Mughal army (1681). For the next **twenty-six years**, he would not return north.

In a few years, Aurangzeb crushed the **Bijapur (Adil Shahi)** and **Golconda (Qutb Shahi)** sultanates (1686–1687). Then he caught Sambhaji, tortured him brutally, and executed him in 1689. He captured Raigad, the Maratha capital. He must have believed the Maratha kingdom was finished.

He was wrong.

After Sambhaji, his younger brother **Rajaram** became Chhatrapati and fled south to the fort of **Gingee** (in present-day Tamil Nadu). The Maratha resistance scattered into hundreds of forts across the Deccan. From every fort, Maratha cavalry raided Mughal supply lines, ambushed Mughal columns, and disappeared again into the hills. Whenever the Mughals captured a fort, the Marathas seized another.

When Rajaram died in 1700, his queen **Tarabai** took charge. She organised large Maratha armies and sent them deep into **Mughal territory itself**. She is rightly called the architect of the early **northward Maratha expansion**.

In 1707, Aurangzeb finally died at Ahmednagar — old, broken, and far from his capital. He had spent the last quarter-century of his life trying to subdue the Marathas and had failed. His vast empire was financially exhausted. As the Mughal Empire began to crack apart, the Marathas were ready to inherit much of it.

## The Peshwa Era and the Pan-Indian Maratha Confederacy

After Aurangzeb's death, the Maratha state changed shape. Shivaji had run a tightly centralised kingdom. But the long war against the Mughals had created a generation of regional commanders, each fiercely loyal to the idea of Swarajya but used to acting on their own.

So the post-Aurangzeb Maratha state evolved into a **confederacy** — nominally centred on the Chhatrapati at Satara, but with effective power held by the **Peshwa** (a Persian word meaning "prime minister") at Pune, and by regional chiefs across India.

The great Peshwas were:
- **Balaji Vishwanath** (Peshwa 1713–1720) — organised and stabilised the system
- **Bajirao I** (Peshwa 1720–1740) — perhaps the greatest cavalry commander in Indian history; expanded Maratha power across central and northern India; never lost a battle
- **Balaji Bajirao / Nanasaheb** (Peshwa 1740–1761) — under whom the Marathas reached their geographic peak
- **Madhavrao I** (Peshwa 1761–1772) — recovered the empire after the disaster at Panipat

Under the Peshwas, the Marathas operated through major regional dynasties spread across India:
- The **Bhonsles of Nagpur** — central India
- The **Holkars of Indore** — central India (Malwa)
- The **Scindias of Gwalior** — north-central India
- The **Gaekwads of Baroda** — Gujarat
- The **Peshwas themselves** at Pune — the Deccan core

This was the **Maratha Confederacy**. At its peak in the mid-18th century, it controlled or exacted tribute from territory stretching from **Attock and Peshawar in the northwest** (in present-day Pakistan), through **Delhi, Lahore**, across the Indo-Gangetic plain, down to the **Deccan and Tamil Nadu**. The Marathas were the **largest pan-Indian power before the British**.

## Panipat 1761: The Great Defeat

In 1761, Maratha power suffered its greatest disaster — the **Third Battle of Panipat**.

Background: The Afghan king **Ahmad Shah Abdali** had been raiding north India for years. The Marathas, who by now considered themselves protectors of the Mughal emperor at Delhi, marched a huge army north to confront him. They met at **Panipat** — the same plain near Delhi where two earlier battles had decided India's fate.

The Maratha army was vast — perhaps 100,000 soldiers plus a huge entourage of non-combatants and pilgrims. But it was deep in unfamiliar country, far from its Deccan supply base, in winter, surrounded by Afghan, Rohilla, and Awadhi allies of Abdali. After months of stalemate and starvation, the Marathas attacked. By evening, tens of thousands of Marathas lay dead. Most of the Peshwa's own family was killed.

Panipat was a hammer blow. It killed an entire generation of Maratha leaders. The shock split the confederacy. **No Maratha army again advanced as confidently into the far north**.

And yet — Maratha power recovered. Within ten years, **Peshwa Madhavrao I** had stabilised the empire. **Mahadji Shinde (Scindia)** of Gwalior, one of the great strategists of the late 18th century, raised a modern army with European-trained officers and artillery. In **1771**, Mahadji recaptured **Delhi** and made the Mughal Emperor a Maratha pensioner. For the next thirty years, the Maratha would effectively control the Mughal court.

## The Anglo-Maratha Wars and the End

In the second half of the 18th century, the chief rivals for control of India were no longer the Mughals or the Afghans. They were the **British East India Company** and the **Marathas**.

Three **Anglo-Maratha wars** were fought:

- **First Anglo-Maratha War (1775–1782)** — ended in Maratha **victory**; the Treaty of Salbai restored the status quo. **Nana Phadnavis**, the brilliant statesman at Pune, even organised the **first pan-Indian anti-British alliance**, uniting old rivals like **Hyder Ali of Mysore** and the **Nizam of Hyderabad** against the Company.

- **Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803–1805)** — by now the Maratha chiefs were quarrelling with each other. The British defeated them piecemeal. Delhi was lost.

- **Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–1818)** — the final defeat. The Peshwa's throne was abolished. The Maratha confederacy ended as a political entity.

A famous line summarises the historical truth: **the British took India from the Marathas more than from the Mughals or any other power**. By 1818, the Marathas had been the *de facto* paramount power across much of the subcontinent. When they fell, India fell with them.

## Maratha Administration: Eight Ministers and the Care of Subjects

Shivaji's administrative innovations were as important as his military ones.

**A salaried bureaucracy.** Earlier sultans had distributed **jagirs** — land grants — to nobles and soldiers, who then collected taxes for themselves and only nominally served the king. This made nobles powerful and the king dependent. Shivaji **abolished hereditary jagirs** for most officials, and paid government servants a **salary out of the state treasury**. He also **periodically transferred** them, so no official could become a local strongman.

**The Ashta Pradhan Mandal — Council of Eight.** Shivaji established a formal cabinet of eight ministers:

- **Peshwa** — Prime Minister, head of administration
- **Amatya** — Finance Minister
- **Sachiv** — Land Revenue / Records Minister
- **Mantri** — Intelligence Minister (and royal household)
- **Senapati** — Commander-in-Chief of the army
- **Sumant** — Minister for Foreign Affairs
- **Nyayadhish** — Chief Justice
- **Panditrao** — Head of Religious Affairs

The **Chhatrapati** himself sat above the council.

**Care for soldiers and subjects.** Shivaji gave **pensions** to widows of soldiers killed in battle and offered **military posts** to their sons — a system of social security four centuries before such things were common anywhere. He issued strict orders that his officials must not mistreat villagers or grab their property — *not even a single blade of grass*. In a famous letter about timber for the navy, he wrote:

> "Wood from big trees like teak is required for the Navy. If needed, secure permission for cutting the trees from the forest and then proceed. Other trees like the mango and jackfruit are also useful, but do not touch them. Because such trees take many years to mature, and the people look after them like their children. If you cut them, will their sorrow ever end?"

**Taxation.** The Marathas levied two main taxes on territories they did not directly govern:
- **Chauth** — 25% of the revenue, paid to the Marathas in exchange for protection
- **Sardeshmukhi** — an additional 10%, an older Maratha claim of overlordship

In return, the Marathas protected the area and did not interfere in its internal administration. Over time, many such territories simply became part of the Maratha Empire.

**Army.** The Maratha army had three wings:
- **Infantry** (foot soldiers)
- **Cavalry** — divided into **bargirs** (whose horses and gear were supplied by the state) and **shiledars** (who brought their own)
- **Navy**

By the 18th century, Maratha leaders like **Mahadji Shinde** were also raising disciplined European-style infantry with modern artillery — though never quite enough of it.

**Judicial system.** Local justice was usually delivered by the **panchayat** — a gathering of local officials and elders — with appeal possible to a Maratha chief. The Marathas were notable for **rarely using the death penalty**. In big towns like Pune and Indore, a **kotwal** (city police chief) maintained law and order.

**Trade.** Shivaji and his officials owned their own merchant ships and traded directly with **Mocha in Yemen, Muscat in Oman, Malacca in Malaysia** — and beyond. The Maratha state actively built roads, ferries, and bridges across its territory. The 18th-century network of ferries in Odisha is a striking example.

## Cultural Revival: Marathi, Sanskrit, and a Confidence Recovered

The Maratha contribution to Indian culture was profound — and was *part* of, not separate from, the political project.

**Language.** Shivaji commissioned a Sanskrit treatise called **Rajya-Vyavahara-Kosha** — literally "Dictionary of Statecraft" — which gave **Sanskrit equivalents for the Persian terms** used in the diplomatic and administrative language of the day. After this, the proportion of Persian loanwords in Maratha diplomatic Marathi dropped sharply. The cursive **Modi** script, a stylish form of Devanagari, became the everyday handwriting of the Maratha state.

**Seal and symbols.** Shivaji's royal **seal** carried a Sanskrit inscription — a striking departure from the prevailing Persian-only seals of other Indian states:

> *"This seal of Chhatrapati Shivaji, son of Shahji, waxing like the new moon, revered by the world, reigns for the welfare of the people."*

His **saffron flag** (Bhagwa Dhwaj) was adopted by all Marathas.

**Religion.** Shivaji was a devout Hindu who **respected other faiths** while upholding his own. He rebuilt **desecrated temples**, promoted Sanskrit and Marathi literature, and protected religious institutions. But the historical record also notes — for example, his strict orders that the **holy books and persons of Islam were not to be insulted**, and his careful treatment of Muslim subjects of conquered territory. He famously **forbade the Dutch from trading slaves** in his southern territories — at a time when most European powers were capturing and selling Indians as slaves with no opposition from local rulers.

**The Bhonsles of Nagpur** later revived the worship of **Lord Jagannath at Puri** (Odisha), which had been disrupted during Mughal rule. The Marathas patronised **temples, ghats, music, dance, painting** across India.

## The Maratha Women: Tarabai and Ahilyabai Holkar

Two women stand out in the Maratha story.

**Tarabai** (early 18th century) — Rajaram's queen. After her husband's death, she took military command in the middle of the great Mughal-Maratha war. Recognising that Aurangzeb and most of the Mughal army were tied down in the Deccan, she sent **large Maratha armies northward** to invade Mughal territory. She is, in many ways, the **architect of the northward Maratha expansion**. Her sense of strategy and her tenacity preserved Maratha independence during a critical period.

**Ahilyabai Holkar** (1725–1795) — queen-regent of the Holkar dynasty of Indore (in central India). After losing both her husband and her only son, she ruled her state for thirty years with **wisdom, justice, and care**. She personally listened to petitions from common people. She built and restored **hundreds of temples, ghats, wells, and rest-houses** across India — from **Kedarnath in the Himalayas to Rameswaram in the south**. Most famously, she **rebuilt the Kashi Vishwanath temple at Varanasi** (destroyed by Aurangzeb) and the **Somnath temple in Gujarat** (destroyed centuries earlier by Mahmud of Ghazni). She also revived the **Maheshwar handloom industry**, which continues to thrive today. Few rulers in Indian history are remembered with such universal affection.

## In Focus: Thanjavur and the Southern Maratha Bridge

The Maratha story was not only northern. **Ekoji**, the half-brother of Shivaji, conquered **Thanjavur** in Tamil Nadu in the late 17th century, establishing a Maratha kingdom in the deep south that lasted until the 19th century.

The Thanjavur Marathas became great patrons of culture. The most remarkable was **Serfoji II** (ruled 1798–1832), who:
- Spoke many Indian and European languages
- Wrote a Marathi play called *Devendra Kuravanji* describing world geography
- Patronised musicians during whose time **modern Carnatic music** took shape
- Patronised dancers during whose time **Bharatanatyam** classical dance took its modern form
- Established the **Dhanwantari Mahal**, a centre offering **free medical treatment** in both Indian and Western medicine
- Started **the first printing press in India run by an Indian ruler**
- Inscribed the entire history of his family on the walls of the **Brihadishwara temple at Thanjavur** — one of the largest single inscriptions in India

Thanjavur shows that the Marathas were not just Maharashtrian. They created a **syncretic culture** wherever they ruled — Tamil, Telugu, and Marathi traditions interacted freely, producing innovation that influences Indian arts to this day.

## The Maratha Legacy

What did the Marathas leave India?

**Political.** They demonstrated, against the largest empire in the world, that a determined people could **govern themselves**. The idea of **Swarajya** — Indian self-rule — outlived the Maratha state. It became, two centuries later, the central rallying cry of the **Indian freedom movement**. When **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** declared "Swarajya is my birthright and I shall have it," he was speaking Shivaji's word.

**Geographic.** At their peak, the Marathas held more Indian territory than any indigenous power since the Mauryas. Without them, the Mughal collapse in the 18th century could have left India even more fragmented. They preserved a kind of pan-Indian political horizon — which the British then inherited.

**Cultural.** The Maratha revival of Sanskrit and regional languages, of temples and pilgrimage routes, of music and dance and painting, was real and lasting. Ahilyabai's temple-building reached from the Himalayas to Rameswaram. The Thanjavur Marathas helped midwife modern Carnatic music and Bharatanatyam.

**Naval.** They were the **only Indian power in centuries to seriously contest European sea power**. The names of Sindhudurg, Vijaydurg, and Kanhoji Angre belong to the global history of the Indian Ocean.

**Administrative.** Salaried officials. Periodic transfers. Pensions for widows. Strict instructions not to harm subjects. A cabinet of eight ministers. A treasury, a navy, a foreign office, a chief justice. The Marathas built modern statecraft in 17th-century India.

**Three Anglo-Maratha wars** later, the British inherited all of this. They redrew the map; they redirected the revenue; but the administrative scaffolding they used was substantially what the Marathas had built. The story of modern India begins, in many ways, in the forts of the Western Ghats.

## Apply: The Marathas Around You

The Maratha story is not buried in books. It is around us — in the names of forts, the rhythms of folk songs, the streets of our cities, the family names of people we know. Pick one and look closer.

### Story 1 — A Fort You Could Visit

Maharashtra has over **350 hill forts**. If your family is in or near the state, places like **Sinhagad, Pratapgad, Raigad, Rajgad, Lohagad, Torna** are within a weekend's drive of Pune or Mumbai. Many are still climbable. If you live further afield — central India has **Gwalior fort** (long held by the Scindias), **Jhansi fort** (associated with the Bundela line that Shivaji inspired), and Tamil Nadu has **Gingee fort** where the Maratha Chhatrapati Rajaram took refuge during the 27-year war. A weekend climb, with water in your bag and the Maratha story in your head, will make this chapter feel real in a way no textbook can.

### Story 2 — Shivaji in Your City

Almost every city in India has a **statue or street named after Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj**. In Mumbai, the international airport, the central railway terminus, and a major museum all bear his name. In Pune, look for **Shivaji Maharaj Putla** in the heart of the city. In Bengaluru, Delhi, Hyderabad, Chennai — look for **Shivaji Marg, Shivaji Park, Shivaji Statue**. Why is this one Maharashtrian king honoured in cities thousands of kilometres from his Deccan? Because his idea — Swarajya, dignified Indian self-rule — belongs to every Indian, not just to Maharashtra.

### Story 3 — A Family Name in Your Circle

If you have classmates or neighbours with surnames like **Bhonsle, Shinde / Scindia, Holkar, Gaekwad, Pawar, Patil, Deshmukh, Deshpande, Kulkarni, Joshi, Phadnavis, Apte, Bhosale, Pant**, you are looking at a family with Maratha roots — or roots in a region the Marathas governed. The **Scindias** still hold honorary positions in Gwalior. The **Holkars** are remembered in Indore. The **Gaekwads** in Baroda. Ask your friend about their family's village. The map of Maratha India is still alive in our address books.

### DIY — Pick One

- **Visit a Maratha-built or Maratha-held fort or museum.** Take photographs of the walls, the water tanks, the carvings, the views. Sketch the fort's layout in your notebook. Ask the guide which campaign the fort played a role in.
- **Draw the Maratha Confederacy at its peak (around 1759).** Use an outline map of India. Mark the Peshwa core at Pune, the Bhonsles at Nagpur, the Holkars at Indore, the Scindias at Gwalior, the Gaekwads at Baroda. Shade in the territory paying chauth. Compare with the Mughal map of the same year. What do you notice?
- **Interview an elder** in your family or community. Ask: "What stories did *you* hear about Shivaji as a child? Where did you hear them?" Write down what they say in their words, not yours. Folk memory is history too.
