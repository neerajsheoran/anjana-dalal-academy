## Weaving Patterns — Maths in Art

**Child:** Maa, at the craft exhibition I saw a woman weaving a beautiful cane basket. Is that really maths?

**Mother:** Absolutely! Weaving follows a strict rule — one strand goes over, then under, then over, then under. The next row does the opposite. If you change the rule — say 2 over, 1 under — you get a completely different texture. The weaver is creating a mathematical pattern with her hands!

**Child:** So the pattern is like a secret code?

**Mother:** Exactly. And different codes produce different designs. The famous Banarasi sarees and Patola silk from Gujarat both use specific mathematical patterns woven into the fabric. Next time you see a darri or a bamboo basket at home, look closely — you will spot the over-under rule repeating perfectly.

**Child:** Maths is hiding in the most surprising places!

## Tessellation — Covering Without Gaps

**Child:** Maa, why are the floor tiles in our house squares and not circles?

**Mother:** Try placing four round coasters on the dining table so they cover the surface completely. Can you do it?

**Child:** No! There are always gaps between the round shapes.

**Mother:** Exactly! Circles cannot tessellate — they cannot cover a surface without leaving spaces. But squares fit together perfectly, edge to edge. So do equilateral triangles and regular hexagons. In fact, only these three regular shapes can tile a floor all by themselves.

**Child:** What about the honeycomb? Bees use hexagons, right?

**Mother:** Yes! Bees discovered this millions of years before humans. Hexagons tessellate perfectly AND use the least amount of wax for the most storage space. Bees are nature's mathematicians!

**Child:** What if I want to use pentagons?

**Mother:** Pentagons leave gaps when placed together. But here is the clever part — you can combine shapes. Octagons with small squares together can tile beautifully. You may have noticed this pattern on the floors of old Indian monuments and temples.

## Types of Triangles — Every Triangle Has a Character

**Child:** Maa, are all triangles the same?

**Mother:** Not at all! Think of triangles like members of a family. An equilateral triangle is perfectly balanced — all three sides equal, all three angles equal. It is like a person who treats everyone exactly the same way.

**Child:** What about isosceles?

**Mother:** An isosceles triangle has two sides equal — like a matching pair of jhumka earrings. If you fold it down the middle along its symmetry line, the two halves fit on top of each other perfectly. The warning signs on the road — those yellow triangular boards — are often isosceles.

**Child:** And scalene?

**Mother:** A scalene triangle has all three sides different — every side is a different length, every angle is different. It is like a jigsaw piece that is completely unique.

**Child:** So if I measure a triangle and find two sides are 6 cm each and one is 9 cm, it is isosceles?

**Mother:** Right! And if all three sides are 6 cm, it is equilateral. If they are 4, 6, and 9 — all different — it is scalene. Triangles tell you their type if you just measure them!

## Quadrilaterals — The Shape Family Tree

**Child:** Maa, I get confused between rectangles, squares, rhombuses, and parallelograms. They all seem similar!

**Mother:** Think of them as a family tree. The parallelogram is the grandparent — opposite sides are equal and parallel. Now, if you make all the angles right angles in a parallelogram, you get a rectangle. If you make all sides equal in a parallelogram, you get a rhombus. And if you do BOTH — all sides equal AND all right angles — you get a square.

**Child:** So a square is both a special rectangle AND a special rhombus?

**Mother:** Exactly! Every square is a rectangle, but not every rectangle is a square. Just like every gulab jamun is a sweet, but not every sweet is a gulab jamun.

**Child:** What about a kite? Like the patang we fly on Makar Sankranti?

**Mother:** A kite has two pairs of neighbouring sides that are equal — the two shorter sides sit next to each other, and the two longer sides sit next to each other. Your Sankranti patang is a perfect maths example! When you make a kite at home, you are actually building a quadrilateral with a specific mathematical property.

## Tangram — Seven Pieces, Endless Possibilities

**Child:** Maa, I made a bird, a boat, and a running person using just seven tangram pieces! How can so many shapes come from the same pieces?

**Mother:** The tangram is an ancient Chinese puzzle — 2 large triangles, 1 medium triangle, 2 small triangles, 1 square, and 1 parallelogram. All cut from one single square. But the possibilities are endless because you can rotate and flip every piece.

**Child:** It reminds me of rearranging furniture in a room — same items, totally different look!

**Mother:** Lovely analogy! And here is a mathematical secret — the total area of all 7 pieces always equals the area of the original square. No matter what shape you create — a bird, a fish, or a house — the area stays the same. Only the outline (perimeter) changes.

**Child:** So the area stays fixed but the perimeter can change? That is fascinating!

**Mother:** You are connecting ideas beautifully, beta. That is what real learning looks like.

## 3D Shapes — The Painted Cube Puzzle

**Child:** Maa, the painted cube problem is so cool. You take 27 small cubes, glue them into a big 3 by 3 by 3 cube, paint the outside, and then count how many small cubes have paint on different numbers of faces. I figured out 8 corner cubes have 3 painted faces, but how do I count the rest?

**Mother:** Think about where each small cube sits. Corner cubes touch three outside faces — so 3 painted faces. Edge cubes sit along the edges but NOT at corners — they touch exactly 2 outside faces. A cube has 12 edges, and on a 3 by 3 by 3 cube, each edge has exactly 1 cube in the middle. So 12 edge cubes with 2 painted faces.

**Child:** And the face centres? Each face of the big cube has one cube right in the centre that touches only that one face. Six faces means 6 cubes with 1 painted face!

**Mother:** And the one buried in the very centre of the whole cube?

**Child:** Zero painted faces! It is completely surrounded by other cubes. So 8 plus 12 plus 6 plus 1 equals 27 — everything adds up!

**Mother:** That is the elegance of maths, beta. Every piece fits together like a perfect puzzle.
