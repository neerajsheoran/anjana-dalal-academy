## Why Do We Need Fractions?

**Child:** Maa, fractions are so confusing. Why can we not just use regular numbers?

**Mother:** Okay, tell me this — if I make one paratha and we need to share it equally between you, me, and Papa, how much does each person get?

**Child:** Less than one paratha...

**Mother:** But "less than one" does not tell me anything useful! Should I give you a tiny bite or a big piece? Fractions tell us **exactly** how much. If three people share equally, each gets **1/3** — one-third. Without fractions, we could never divide anything fairly!

**Child:** So each of us gets one out of three equal parts?

**Mother:** Exactly. The bottom number, 3, tells you the paratha was split into 3 equal pieces. The top number, 1, tells you that you get 1 of those pieces. Simple, right?

## Numerator and Denominator — Top and Bottom

**Child:** Maa, I always mix up which number is which.

**Mother:** Let me explain with something you love — mangoes! Imagine I cut a mango into **8 equal slices**. You eat **3 slices**. The fraction is 3/8.

**Child:** Okay...

**Mother:** The **bottom number** (8) is the **denominator** — it tells you the total number of equal pieces. Think of it as the "total cuts." The **top number** (3) is the **numerator** — it tells you how many pieces you actually took. Think of it as "your share."

**Child:** So denominator = how many pieces total, numerator = how many I got?

**Mother:** Perfect! If Dadi cuts a cake into 6 slices and you take 2, that is 2/6. Denominator 6, numerator 2.

**Child:** That is actually easy when I think of food!

## Equivalent Fractions — Same Amount, Different Look

**Child:** Maa, my teacher says 1/2 and 3/6 are equal. But they look so different!

**Mother:** Let me show you with rotis. Take one roti and tear it exactly in half. You have 2 pieces, and one piece is **1/2**. Now take another roti of the same size and cut it into 6 equal parts. Pick up 3 of those parts — that is **3/6**. Place them next to the half roti. Same amount?

**Child:** Yes! Both cover the same area!

**Mother:** That is what equivalent fractions are — **same quantity, different way of writing it**. It is like saying "Bengaluru" and "Bangalore" — same city, different names.

**Child:** How do I find equivalent fractions without cutting rotis every time?

**Mother:** Multiply both the top and bottom by the same number. Start with 1/2 — multiply both by 2, you get 2/4. Multiply both by 3, you get 3/6. By 4, you get 4/8. All of them equal one-half!

**Child:** So 1/2 = 2/4 = 3/6 = 4/8 = 5/10... this goes on forever?

**Mother:** Forever! But every single one represents the same amount of roti.

## Comparing Fractions — Which Is Bigger?

**Child:** Maa, which is more — 5/8 of a cake or 3/8 of a cake?

**Mother:** Easy one! Both have the same denominator — 8. Same-sized slices. So just count the slices.

**Child:** 5 slices versus 3 slices. So 5/8 is more!

**Mother:** Good. Now try a tricky one — which is more, 1/4 or 1/6?

**Child:** Hmm... 6 is bigger than 4, so... 1/6?

**Mother:** Think again! You and your cousin both have identical rotis. You cut yours into **4 pieces** and eat one. Your cousin cuts hers into **6 pieces** and eats one. Whose single piece is bigger?

**Child:** Mine! Because I made only 4 pieces, so each one is fatter. She made 6, so each one is thinner!

**Mother:** Exactly! **More cuts means smaller pieces.** So 1/4 > 1/6. When the numerator is the same, the fraction with the **smaller denominator** is actually bigger. It feels backwards, but think of rotis and it makes sense!

**Child:** Rotis make everything clear!

## Fractions Greater Than 1 — More Than a Whole

**Child:** Maa, my friend wrote 7/4 on the board. How can you have 7 pieces when the whole has only 4?

**Mother:** Wonderful question! Think of idlis. Each plate has 4 idlis. You are very hungry and you eat 7 idlis. That means you ate one full plate (4/4 = 1) plus 3 more from the next plate (3/4). So 7/4 = 1 and 3/4, written as **1 and 3/4**.

**Child:** Oh! So I needed parts of a second plate!

**Mother:** Yes! When the top number is bigger than the bottom, you have crossed the "one whole" mark. We call it a **mixed number** because it mixes a whole number with a fraction. Like when Nani says she drank **2 and 1/2 glasses** of water — that is 2 full glasses plus half a glass, which is 5/2 in fraction form.

**Child:** And 5/2 means 5 halves? Two full glasses use 4 halves, and one half is left over?

**Mother:** Exactly right, beta!

## The Halfway Trick

**Child:** Maa, teacher asked me to compare 5/9 and 3/8. They have different tops and different bottoms. I am stuck!

**Mother:** Here is a clever shortcut. Check whether each fraction is above or below half.

**Child:** How do I check?

**Mother:** For 5/9 — what is half of 9? It is 4.5. Is 5 more than 4.5? Yes! So 5/9 is **above half**. For 3/8 — what is half of 8? It is 4. Is 3 more than 4? No! So 3/8 is **below half**.

**Child:** So 5/9 is bigger because it is above the halfway mark and 3/8 is below?

**Mother:** Correct! It is like a race — if one runner has crossed the halfway point and the other has not, you already know who is ahead without measuring the exact distance.

**Child:** That is such a smart shortcut, Maa!

**Mother:** Maths is full of shortcuts, beta. You just need to keep your eyes open for patterns.
