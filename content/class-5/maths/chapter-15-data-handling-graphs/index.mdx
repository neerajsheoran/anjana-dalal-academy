## What Is Data?

**Data** is information gathered about something. It can be numbers, facts, or observations.

**Examples of data you might collect:**
- How many books each student in your class reads per month
- Number of auto-rickshaws passing your house in an hour
- How you spend your time on a Sunday
- Amount of water used by your family each day

> Before data becomes useful, you must **collect** it, **organise** it, and **present** it clearly.

---

## Collecting and Organising Data

### Using a Table

The simplest way to organise data is in a **table** (also called a frequency table).

**Example:** Time spent reading by 40 students each day:

| Reading Time | Number of Students |
|------------------|--------------------|
| Less than 15 minutes | 6 |
| 15 to 30 minutes | 14 |
| 30 to 45 minutes | 10 |
| 45 to 60 minutes | 7 |
| More than 1 hour | 3 |

From this table you can quickly answer:
- How many students read for more than 30 minutes? → 10 + 7 + 3 = **20**
- How many read for less than 45 minutes? → 6 + 14 + 10 = **30**

## Pictographs

![Sample pictograph showing toy store data with icons representing 5 items each](pictograph-example.png)

A **pictograph** uses **small pictures (icons)** to show data. Each icon stands for a certain number of items.

### What is a Scale?

When numbers are large, drawing one icon for each item would be impractical. Instead, we use a **scale** — one icon represents several items.

**Example:** Sharma Uncle's stationery shop:
- Scale: 1 icon = 4 items
- Pencils: 8 icons = **32 pencils**
- Erasers: 5 icons = **20 erasers**
- Sharpeners: 6 icons = **24 sharpeners**

### Reading a Pictograph:

**Example:** Cycles passing Rohan's house (scale: 1 icon = 4 cycles):

| Day | Icons | Count |
|-----|-------|-------|
| Monday | 7 icons | 7 × 4 = **28** |
| Wednesday | 4 icons | 4 × 4 = **16** |
| Friday | 6 icons | 6 × 4 = **24** |

- Busiest day: **Monday** (28 cycles)
- Fewer on Wednesday than Monday: 28 − 16 = **12 fewer**
- Total over three days: 28 + 16 + 24 = **68**

> You can pick **any scale** that suits the numbers — the same data can be displayed with different scales!

## Bar Graphs

![Bar graph showing daily routine with bars of different heights for sleeping, school, studying, eating, and other activities](bar-graph-daily-routine.png)

A **bar graph** uses **rectangular bars** to display data. The height (or length) of each bar shows the value.

### Parts of a Bar Graph:
- **Horizontal axis** (bottom) — shows the **categories** (what is being compared)
- **Vertical axis** (side) — shows the **numbers/values**
- **Bars** — one bar for each category, height matching its value
- **Title** — tells what the graph is about

### Example: Sita's Daily Routine

| Activity | Hours |
|----------|-------|
| Sleeping | 9 |
| School | 6 |
| Studying at home | 2 |
| Playing and eating | 4 |
| Other activities | 3 |

In the bar graph, the "Sleeping" bar is tallest (9), and "Studying" is shortest (2).

### How Bar Graphs Differ from Pictographs:
- **Pictographs** use pictures or icons
- **Bar graphs** use rectangular bars
- Bar graphs make comparisons easier because differences in bar heights are immediately visible

## Reading a Bar Graph

![Bar graph showing food wastage in school canteen across weekdays with Thursday having the tallest bar](bar-graph-food-wastage.png)

### Example: Water Usage at School (litres per day)

| Day | Activity | Litres Used |
|-----|-----------|-------------|
| Monday | Regular day | 150 |
| Tuesday | Sports day | 250 |
| Wednesday | Regular day | 140 |
| Thursday | Science fair | 200 |
| Friday | Half day | 90 |

From the bar graph:
- **Highest usage:** Tuesday (Sports day) — 250 litres
- **Lowest usage:** Friday (Half day) — 90 litres
- **Total usage:** 150 + 250 + 140 + 200 + 90 = **830 litres**
- If each student drinks about 2 litres per day and there are 100 students: 200 litres for drinking alone. The rest goes to cleaning, cooking, and other uses.

> **Think about it:** How can we reduce water wastage at school? Fix leaking taps, use buckets instead of running hoses, and reuse water for watering plants.

## Making Your Own Bar Graph

To make a bar graph:

1. **Gather your data** — collect the numbers
2. **Pick a scale** — decide what each mark on the vertical axis stands for
3. **Draw the axes** — horizontal for categories, vertical for values
4. **Draw the bars** — one bar per category, with the correct height
5. **Label everything** — both axes, every bar, and give it a title

### A Fun Activity:
Measure how far each person in your family can jump from a standing position. Record the distances and draw a bar graph. Who jumps the farthest?

## Comparing Pictographs and Bar Graphs

| Feature | Pictograph | Bar Graph |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| Uses | Pictures or icons | Rectangular bars |
| Scale | 1 icon = some number | Height of bar = value |
| Best for | Small, simple data sets | Larger data, easy visual comparison |
| How to read | Count icons × scale | Read bar height off the axis |

## Key Words to Remember

| Word | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Data** | Information collected about something — numbers, facts, observations |
| **Pictograph** | A graph that uses pictures to represent data |
| **Bar graph** | A graph that uses rectangular bars to represent data |
| **Scale** | How many items each picture or unit on the axis represents |
| **Horizontal axis** | The bottom line of a graph — usually shows categories |
| **Vertical axis** | The side line of a graph — usually shows numbers |
| **Frequency** | How many times something occurs |
