## Collecting and Organising Data

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says data is information. But why do we need to collect information in maths?

**Mother:** Let me ask you something. How many books do you think children in your class read each month — more than 3, or fewer?

**Child:** I think most of my friends read about 2 books. But I am not really sure.

**Mother:** That is exactly the problem! Without data, you are guessing. But if you actually ask all 40 students in your class and write down their answers, you have real data. And data turns guesses into knowledge. Maybe 14 students read for 15 to 30 minutes daily, 10 read for 30 to 45 minutes. Now you KNOW, instead of just assuming.

**Child:** So data is like proof!

**Mother:** Exactly. Scientists collect data before drawing conclusions. Doctors collect patient data before prescribing treatment. Even the Indian cricket team studies data about opposing batsmen — which bowlers trouble them most — to plan their strategy.

**Child:** Virat Kohli's batting average is data!

**Mother:** A perfect example.

## Pictographs

**Child:** Maa, what is a pictograph? The chapter shows little icons standing for items.

**Mother:** A pictograph uses small pictures to display data. Each picture represents a fixed number of items — that number is called the scale. For example, if one pencil icon stands for 4 pencils sold, then 8 icons mean 32 pencils sold.

**Child:** Why not just draw 32 tiny pencil icons?

**Mother:** Because that would take too much space! Imagine a shop that sold 200 pencils — you would need 200 icons. With a scale of 1 icon equals 4, you only draw 50. The scale keeps the pictograph manageable.

**Child:** What if the number does not divide evenly? Like 26 pencils with a scale of 4?

**Mother:** Smart question! 26 divided by 4 is 6 with remainder 2. So you draw 6 full icons and half an icon (since 2 is half of 4). It is not perfectly precise, but it gives a clear picture at a glance.

**Child:** So pictographs trade a little accuracy for a lot of clarity?

**Mother:** Exactly right. That is the trade-off — simplicity over perfect precision.

## Bar Graphs

**Child:** Maa, how is a bar graph different from a pictograph?

**Mother:** A bar graph uses rectangular bars instead of pictures. The height of each bar tells you the value. Let me use an example from your school. Suppose the school tracked water usage: Monday — 150 litres, Tuesday — 250, Wednesday — 140, Thursday — 200, Friday — 90.

**Child:** So Tuesday's bar would be the tallest?

**Mother:** Yes! And Friday's would be the shortest. The beauty of a bar graph is that you can SEE the comparison immediately — which day used the most water, which used the least, and by how much.

**Child:** I can also see that Tuesday's bar is nearly 3 times taller than Friday's bar. So Tuesday used almost 3 times as much water!

**Mother:** Excellent observation! That kind of visual insight is much harder to get from a plain table of numbers. That is why bar graphs appear in newspapers, weather reports, and cricket match summaries.

**Child:** The IPL uses bar graphs to show run rates and scores, right?

**Mother:** Exactly! And the wagon wheel showing where a batsman scored runs is another type of data visualisation.

## Reading a Bar Graph Carefully

**Child:** Maa, the chapter shows Sita's daily routine as a bar graph. Sleeping is 9 hours, school is 6, studying is 2, playing and eating is 4, other activities is 3. How do I check if this data makes sense?

**Mother:** Add up all the hours — they should total 24, because a day has exactly 24 hours. 9 plus 6 plus 2 plus 4 plus 3 equals 24. It checks out!

**Child:** What if someone showed me a bar graph where the total was only 20 hours?

**Mother:** Then either some activities are missing from the graph, or the data has mistakes. Always add up the totals. This is called data validation — making sure the data is reasonable.

**Child:** Can I make a bar graph of my own daily routine?

**Mother:** Absolutely! Track everything you do for one full day, round to the nearest half hour, and draw a bar graph. You might be surprised — many children discover they spend more time watching screens than they thought, and less time playing outdoors.

**Child:** That sounds like a useful experiment!

**Mother:** And that is the power of data — it shows you the reality of your own life, not just what you imagine is happening.

## Choosing the Right Scale

**Child:** Maa, how do I decide which scale to use for a pictograph or bar graph?

**Mother:** Look at your numbers. If they are small — like 3, 5, 8, 4 — use a scale of 1. Each icon or grid line represents 1 item. But if the numbers are large — like 80, 150, 200, 120 — a scale of 1 would need 200 icons! So you pick a scale of 10 or 20.

**Child:** So the scale depends on how big the numbers are?

**Mother:** Exactly. The goal is to keep the graph easy to read. Too many icons or squished bars make it confusing. Too few make small differences invisible. A good scale means the tallest bar is about 10 to 15 grid squares high.

**Child:** In the water usage example, the numbers are 150, 250, 140, 200, 90. Those are large, so a scale of maybe 50 litres per grid line would work?

**Mother:** Perfect — each grid square on the vertical axis would represent 50 litres. The tallest bar (Tuesday, 250) would be 5 squares high. That gives a clean, readable graph.

**Child:** I think I could make bar graphs for anything now — cricket scores, test marks, even the number of parrots I see each morning!

**Mother:** And that is exactly what data analysts do — they collect information about something they care about, then use graphs to spot patterns and tell stories hidden in the numbers.

## Using Data to Make a Difference

**Child:** Maa, I noticed our school wastes a lot of water. Can data help fix that?

**Mother:** Absolutely! If you measure water usage each day for a week and show the results on a bar graph, everyone can see which days waste the most. Maybe sports day uses extra water for the field. Maybe the canteen wastes water during cooking. Once people see the numbers, they are motivated to act.

**Child:** We could put the bar graph on the notice board!

**Mother:** Now you are using data for social change. This is exactly what the government does with campaigns like Jal Shakti Abhiyan — they collect data about water usage and display it to encourage conservation. Data is not just maths — it is a tool for making the world better.

**Child:** One bar graph at a time!

**Mother:** I could not be more proud of that thinking, beta.
