## Perimeter — The Boundary Length

**Child:** Maa, I keep mixing up perimeter and area. What exactly is perimeter?

**Mother:** Picture a tiny ant walking along the edge of your textbook. The total distance the ant walks to go all the way around — that is the perimeter. It is simply the boundary length.

**Child:** So if my textbook is 28 cm long and 20 cm wide, the perimeter is 28 plus 20 plus 28 plus 20?

**Mother:** Yes! That gives 96 cm. Or use the formula — 2 times length plus 2 times breadth, which is 2 times 28 plus 2 times 20 equals 56 plus 40 equals 96 cm. For a square, it is even simpler — 4 times the side length.

**Child:** Like the carrom board at school! If one side is 74 cm, the perimeter is 4 times 74 equals 296 cm!

**Mother:** Perfect. Think about the rope that marks the boundary of the cricket ground. That rope is basically the perimeter of the playing field. When the commentator says the boundary is 65 metres away, that is the distance from the pitch to the rope — but the full perimeter is the entire length of the rope going around.

**Child:** So perimeter is the length of fence you would need to go around something?

**Mother:** That is the simplest way to think about it in real life.

## Area — The Space Inside

**Child:** And area is the space enclosed inside the boundary?

**Mother:** Yes. If perimeter is the fence around a garden, area is the amount of grass growing inside. For a rectangle, the formula is length times breadth. So if your textbook is 28 cm by 20 cm, its area is 28 times 20 equals 560 square centimetres.

**Child:** Why do we say "square" centimetres?

**Mother:** Because area is measured in square units. Imagine covering your textbook with tiny stickers, each 1 cm by 1 cm. You would need 560 stickers — that is 560 square cm. Each sticker is one square unit.

**Child:** What about a square floor tile that is 25 cm on each side?

**Mother:** Area equals 25 times 25 equals 625 square cm. To tile Nani's 4 metre by 3 metre kitchen, we need the kitchen area — 4 times 3 equals 12 square metres, which is 1,20,000 square cm. Divide by 625 square cm per tile — that is 192 tiles.

**Child:** Maths even helps plan house renovation!

## Important Discovery: Same Area ≠ Same Perimeter

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says two rectangles can have the SAME area but DIFFERENT perimeters. How is that possible?

**Mother:** Let me show you with Nani's quilt patches. She has 18 square patches to arrange in a rectangle. She could make a 9 by 2 arrangement — area is 18, perimeter is 2 times 9 plus 2 times 2 equals 22. Or she could arrange them as 6 by 3 — area is still 18, but perimeter is 2 times 6 plus 2 times 3 equals 18. Or even 18 by 1 — area is still 18, but perimeter shoots up to 2 times 18 plus 2 times 1 equals 38!

**Child:** All three have 18 patches but the perimeters are 22, 18, and 38. That is a huge difference!

**Mother:** And it works the other way too. Rectangles with the same perimeter can have different areas. If you have 20 metres of fence for a garden, you could make a 9 by 1 rectangle with area 9, or a 7 by 3 with area 21, or a 5 by 5 square with area 25!

**Child:** The square gives the largest area! Is that always the case?

**Mother:** Always! For any given perimeter, a square packs in the maximum area. That is why many storage boxes and containers are square-shaped — they hold the most for a given amount of material.

## Finding Missing Dimensions

**Child:** Maa, if I know the area is 72 square metres and the length is 18 metres, how do I find the breadth?

**Mother:** Just reverse the area formula. Area equals length times breadth, so breadth equals area divided by length. That is 72 divided by 18 equals 4 metres.

**Child:** Like undoing a multiplication with division!

**Mother:** Exactly — just like the Coconut Farm chapter taught us. Here is a practical example: Papa wants to paint one wall of the living room. The wall is 6 metres long and 3 metres high. Area is 6 times 3 equals 18 square metres. If one tin of paint covers 36 square metres, one tin is enough for two walls of that size.

**Child:** What if the wall has a window?

**Mother:** Good thinking! If the window is 1 metre by 1.5 metres, its area is 1.5 square metres. So the actual area to paint is 18 minus 1.5 equals 16.5 square metres. Builders always subtract doors and windows when they calculate paint needs.

## Estimating Area of Irregular Shapes

**Child:** Maa, not everything is a perfect rectangle. How do I find the area of my palm or a mango leaf?

**Mother:** Place your hand flat on centimetre grid paper — the kind in your maths notebook. Trace around your hand with a pencil. Then count the squares inside the outline.

**Child:** But some squares are only partly covered!

**Mother:** Here is the rule — if more than half the square falls inside, count it as 1. If less than half is inside, count it as 0. Add all the ones and you get an approximate area.

**Child:** I tried it and my hand covers about 90 square centimetres!

**Mother:** Now try a banana leaf next time we get one. Geographers use the same idea on maps — they place a grid over the map of a state and count squares to estimate its area. India's total area of about 32,87,000 square kilometres was calculated using advanced versions of this technique.

## Why This Chapter Matters

**Child:** Maa, why do we learn both area and perimeter? When would I actually use these?

**Mother:** Think about it — a farmer needs the area of her field to buy the right amount of seeds, and the perimeter to build a fence. An architect calculates the area of each room for flooring, and the perimeter for skirting tiles. A painter measures wall area. A gardener measures perimeter for border plants. Even you — when you cover your textbooks with brown paper at the start of term, you are estimating area!

**Child:** And during Diwali, when we string lights around the balcony railing?

**Mother:** That is perimeter! The length of light string you need equals the perimeter of the balcony. You already use these ideas without even realising it.

**Child:** Now I will notice, and that makes it even more interesting!

**Mother:** That is the whole point of studying maths, beta. Not just to solve textbook problems, but to understand the world around you a little better.
