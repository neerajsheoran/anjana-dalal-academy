## Factors and Multiples

**Child:** Maa, what exactly is a factor? I keep getting confused between factors and multiples.

**Mother:** Think of factors as the building blocks of a number. If you can arrange 18 rangoli dots into 3 rows of 6, then both 3 and 6 are factors of 18. Factors are numbers that divide evenly into a number with nothing left over.

**Child:** So I need to find all the ways to split 18 into equal rows?

**Mother:** Yes! 1 times 18, 2 times 9, 3 times 6 — those give you all the factors: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, and 18. Every number always has 1 and itself as factors. And here is the important difference — factors are limited. You can list every single one. But multiples never end.

**Child:** Like the multiples of 4 are 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 and they go on forever?

**Mother:** Exactly! A simple way to remember: factors divide INTO a number, multiples are what you GET when you multiply. Factors look inward, multiples look outward.

**Child:** So factors are the ingredients, and multiples are the dishes you can cook!

## Prime Numbers

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says some numbers have only two factors — 1 and themselves. What makes them special?

**Mother:** They are called prime numbers, and they are the basic building blocks of all numbers. Just like all words are made from letters, every number can be broken down into primes. Take 18 — it is 2 times 3 times 3. Take 35 — it is 5 times 7. But 7 by itself? You cannot break it down further. It is 1 times 7, and nothing else.

**Child:** So 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 are all prime?

**Mother:** Yes! And here is something surprising — 2 is the only even prime number. Every other even number can be divided by 2, so they all have at least three factors and cannot be prime.

**Child:** What about 1? It only has one factor — itself. Is it prime?

**Mother:** Wonderful question! By agreement among mathematicians, 1 is NOT considered prime because a prime must have exactly two factors. Since 1 has only one factor, it is neither prime nor composite — it stands in its own special category.

**Child:** Like how zero is neither positive nor negative!

**Mother:** Exactly that kind of exception.

## Common Multiples

**Child:** Maa, the chapter shows a deer jumping by 4 and a squirrel jumping by 5 on a number line. They land on the same spot at 20, 40, 60. Why does that happen?

**Mother:** Because 20 is a multiple of BOTH 4 and 5. The deer reaches 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24... The squirrel reaches 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30... The spots where both land are the common multiples: 20, 40, 60, and so on.

**Child:** And the smallest one is 20 — the LCM?

**Mother:** Right! LCM means Least Common Multiple. Here is a real-life example. The Janpath bus comes every 4 minutes, and the Nehru Place bus comes every 5 minutes. If both arrived just now, when will both arrive together again?

**Child:** In 20 minutes! Because LCM of 4 and 5 is 20.

**Mother:** Now think about this. What if two buses come every 4 and every 8 minutes?

**Child:** Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16... Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24... They first meet at 8. So LCM of 4 and 8 is just 8!

**Mother:** Because 8 is already a multiple of 4. When one number is a multiple of the other, the bigger number IS the LCM. That is a handy shortcut.

## Common Factors and HCF

**Child:** Maa, what about common factors? The chapter finds common factors of 30 and 42.

**Mother:** Factors of 30: 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30. Factors of 42: 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 14, 21, 42. The numbers appearing in BOTH lists are common factors: 1, 2, 3, 6. The biggest one — 6 — is the HCF, the Highest Common Factor.

**Child:** When would I use HCF in everyday life?

**Mother:** Suppose I have 30 gulab jamuns and 42 rasgullas and I want to make gift plates where each plate has the same number of gulab jamuns and the same number of rasgullas, with nothing left over. The maximum number of plates is the HCF of 30 and 42, which is 6. So I can make 6 plates, each with 5 gulab jamuns and 7 rasgullas!

**Child:** Oh! HCF tells you the most equal groups you can make!

**Mother:** That is a perfect way to put it.

## Divisibility Rules

**Child:** Maa, is there a shortcut to check if a number is divisible by 2 or 5 or 10 without actually doing the division?

**Mother:** Yes! These are divisibility rules, and they are like magic tricks. For 2 — just check if the last digit is even (0, 2, 4, 6, or 8). For 5 — check if the last digit is 0 or 5. For 10 — the last digit must be 0.

**Child:** So 4,586 is divisible by 2 because it ends in 6, but not by 5 because it does not end in 0 or 5?

**Mother:** Spot on! Now think about cricket scores. India scored 345 runs. Is that divisible by 5?

**Child:** Ends in 5 — yes! 345 divided by 5 is 69.

**Mother:** Is it divisible by 2?

**Child:** Ends in 5, which is odd — no!

**Mother:** Divisible by 10?

**Child:** Does not end in 0 — no! These rules are so quick!

**Mother:** When you learn the rules for 3, 4, 6, 8, and 9 in higher classes, you will be able to test any number almost instantly.

## Real-Life Problem — Raja the Tiger and Meera the Leopard

**Child:** Maa, the problem about Raja visiting the watering hole every 4 days and Meera every 6 days is fun. They meet together every 12 days because LCM of 4 and 6 is 12.

**Mother:** Right! And let me extend it. What if a monkey also comes to the watering hole every 3 days, Raja every 4 days, and Meera every 6 days? When do all three arrive on the same day?

**Child:** I need the LCM of 3, 4, and 6. Multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12... Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12... Multiples of 6: 6, 12... The first number in all three lists is... 12!

**Mother:** Excellent! LCM of 3, 4, and 6 is 12. All three animals gather at the watering hole every 12 days. This kind of problem shows up in real life too — if three school bells ring at intervals of 10, 15, and 20 minutes, the LCM tells you when they all ring at the same time.

**Child:** So LCM is like a meeting point for repeating events!

**Mother:** That is a beautiful way to describe it, beta. I could not have said it better.
