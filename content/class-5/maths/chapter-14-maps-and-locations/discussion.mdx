## The Four Directions

**Child:** Maa, I always mix up East and West. Is there an easy way to remember?

**Mother:** Here is a simple trick. Every morning, face the sunrise — that direction is East. Your left hand points North, your right hand points South, and your back faces West. You can also remember the clockwise order: N-E-S-W. Some children remember it as "Nani Eats Sweet Watermelon"!

**Child:** Ha ha! I love that! But what if it is cloudy and I cannot see the sun?

**Mother:** That is when a compass is useful. The compass needle always swings towards North because of the Earth's magnetic field. Once you know North, you know all four directions. Indian sailors used compasses hundreds of years ago to navigate the seas.

**Child:** And birds — they fly thousands of kilometres without any compass at all!

**Mother:** Yes, migratory birds have a natural navigation system. Scientists believe they sense the Earth's magnetic field, use the sun's position, and even follow the stars. Nature truly has its own GPS!

## Reading a Street Map

**Child:** Maa, when I look at a map, how do I figure out which direction is which?

**Mother:** Most maps follow one simple rule — North is at the top. That means the right side is East, the left side is West, and the bottom is South. Look for a small compass arrow on the map to confirm.

**Child:** So if the map shows my school to the right of our house, the school is to the East?

**Mother:** Exactly! And if you need to walk from home to school, you would walk East. Now, if the playground is shown below the school on the map, which direction is that?

**Child:** South! Because down on the map means South.

**Mother:** Perfect. Here is a tricky one — if you are walking East and you turn right, which direction do you now face?

**Child:** Facing East, turn right... I would now face South!

**Mother:** Right turns are clockwise: North to East, East to South, South to West, West to North. Left turns go the other way. This is how bus routes work — "go North on Gandhi Road, turn right onto Market Street" means you are now heading East on Market Street.

## Reading a Metro Map

**Child:** Maa, I love looking at the Kolkata Metro map. All those coloured lines! But how do I plan a trip if my station and my destination are on different lines?

**Mother:** Good question. Say you want to go from Park Street on the Blue Line to Salt Lake on the Green Line. First check if they share any line — they do not. So you need to find an interchange station where both lines cross.

**Child:** Like Sealdah? That might be on both lines!

**Mother:** Exactly! You take the Blue Line to Sealdah, switch to the Green Line, and continue to Salt Lake. But there might be another route with fewer stops — always look for the option with the fewest interchanges.

**Child:** It is like a puzzle — finding the quickest path from start to finish!

**Mother:** And believe it or not, this is actually a real branch of mathematics called graph theory. Metro maps, Google Maps directions, and even internet data routing all use the same mathematical ideas to find the best path.

## Grid Maps and Coordinates

**Child:** Maa, what are coordinates? The chapter says they are like an address on a grid.

**Mother:** Think of a chess board. If I say "move to column D, row 4," you know exactly which square I mean. Coordinates work the same way — two numbers that give the exact position: first the horizontal number (column), then the vertical number (row).

**Child:** So (3, 2) means go 3 steps across and 2 steps up?

**Mother:** Exactly! Always remember: across first, then up. I use this trick — "walk along the corridor, then climb the stairs." Corridor is horizontal, stairs are vertical.

**Child:** Like in the zoo map — the elephant is at (2, 1) and the parrot is at (5, 3). They are in different rows AND different columns!

**Mother:** Right. If a peacock is at (2, 3), it would be in the same column as the elephant but higher up. Coordinates remove all confusion about location.

**Child:** Is this how GPS works on Maa's phone?

**Mother:** Yes! GPS uses latitude and longitude, which are coordinates on the globe. Our house might be at something like 22.5 degrees North, 88.3 degrees East. Same idea — two numbers pinpoint a spot on Earth.

## Giving Directions on a Grid

**Child:** Maa, the chapter shows an ant on a grid trying to reach sugar. It can only crawl along the lines. How do I find the best path?

**Mother:** Say the sugar is 4 cm East and 2 cm North from the ant. The ant could go 4 East first, then 2 North. Or 2 North first, then 4 East. Or even zigzag — 2 East, 1 North, 2 East, 1 North. All paths reach the sugar!

**Child:** But which is the shortest?

**Mother:** Here is the interesting part — as long as the ant does not go backwards (never moves West or South), every path has the same total length: 4 plus 2 equals 6 cm. This is called the grid distance.

**Child:** So on a grid, many different paths can all be equally short!

**Mother:** Right. In the real world with diagonal roads and shortcuts, the shortest path would be a straight line — about 4.5 cm. You will learn to calculate that exact distance using the Pythagorean theorem in a few years.

**Child:** Now I want to learn that too!

**Mother:** Patience, beta — enjoy this chapter first!

## Making Your Own Map

**Child:** Maa, the chapter asks us to draw a map of our neighbourhood. Where do I begin?

**Mother:** First, decide on North and draw a compass arrow pointing upward. Then think about the main landmarks: our house, the park, your school, the temple, the medical shop. Place them in roughly the right directions — school is to the East, the park is to the North, the market is to the South.

**Child:** Do I need to measure exact distances?

**Mother:** Not exactly, but keep things proportional. If school is about twice as far as the park, show it roughly twice as far on your map. Use a simple legend — a rectangle for buildings, a tree symbol for the park, a cross for the hospital.

**Child:** Can I use a grid to make it more accurate?

**Mother:** Definitely! Draw a grid first and assign coordinates to each landmark. Then anyone can find any place on your map using just two numbers. That is exactly what professional map-makers, called cartographers, do. You could write (4, 3) for school and (1, 2) for home!

**Child:** My own coordinate system for our area. That is so cool!

**Mother:** And that, beta, is the power of mathematical thinking — turning the complicated real world into something neat and organised.
