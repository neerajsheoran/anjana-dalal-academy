## Choosing the Right Unit for Weight

**Child:** Maa, why do we have milligrams, grams, kilograms, quintals, AND tonnes? That is five units just for weight!

**Mother:** Imagine telling the chemist that your crocin tablet weighs 0.0005 kilograms. Or telling the truck driver his cargo is 80,00,000 grams. Sounds ridiculous, right?

**Child:** Ha ha, yes! The chemist would say 500 milligrams and the driver would say 8 tonnes.

**Mother:** Exactly. Just like you use different utensils in the kitchen — a tiny spoon for turmeric, a regular spoon for sugar, and a big ladle for sambar — you use different units for different amounts of weight. Milligrams for medicines and spices, grams for small items, kilograms for groceries, quintals for grain sacks, and tonnes for vehicles and elephants.

**Child:** How heavy is an elephant?

**Mother:** An Indian elephant weighs around 4 to 5 tonnes — that is 4,000 to 5,000 kilograms! Now try expressing that in milligrams.

**Child:** That would be... 5,000 times 1,000 times 1,000... five hundred crore milligrams! Definitely need the right unit!

## The Tricky Conversions

**Child:** Maa, I made an error on my test. I wrote 6 kg 75 g as 6,750 g instead of 6,075 g. Why did I get confused?

**Mother:** This is a very common trap. Think of it like money. If I say ₹6 and 75 paise, how many paise total?

**Child:** ₹6 is 600 paise, plus 75 paise, so 675 paise.

**Mother:** Now what about ₹6 and 7 paise?

**Child:** That is 600 plus 7 equals 607 paise — not 67!

**Mother:** Same with weight. 6 kg 75 g means 6,000 g plus 75 g equals 6,075 g. The 75 does not magically become 750 just because there are three zeros in a kilogram. Always convert the kg part fully to grams first, then add.

**Child:** So the trick is to always write out the full conversion — 6 kg = 6,000 g — before adding?

**Mother:** That is the safest approach. Write it out step by step, and you will avoid that trap every time.

## Weight at the Sabzi Mandi

**Child:** Maa, today at the vegetable market, you bought 3 kg 200 g of potatoes and 2 kg 650 g of tomatoes. The vendor said the total is 5 kg 850 g. Is that correct?

**Mother:** Let us verify! Add the kg parts first: 3 plus 2 equals 5 kg. Now the grams: 200 plus 650 equals 850 g. Since 850 g is less than 1,000 g, we do not need to convert. The total is 5 kg 850 g. The vendor was right!

**Child:** What about subtraction? If we already had 550 g of onions at home and we bought 3 kg 100 g more?

**Mother:** Convert everything to grams. 3 kg 100 g equals 3,100 g. Add the 550 g at home — total is 3,650 g, which is 3 kg 650 g. Or if you want just the difference of what was bought versus what was home: 3,100 minus 550 equals 2,550 g equals 2 kg 550 g.

**Child:** Doing maths at the mandi is surprisingly fun!

## Capacity — How Much Can It Hold?

**Child:** Maa, what is the difference between weight and capacity exactly?

**Mother:** Weight tells you how heavy something is. Capacity tells you how much liquid a container can hold. Your water bottle has some weight even when it is empty, but its capacity is 750 ml — that is how much water fits inside it.

**Child:** So my school bottle holds 750 ml. What about the matka at Nani's house?

**Mother:** A matka holds about 12 to 15 litres. A household bucket holds about 15 to 20 litres. And the water tanker that comes to fill the overhead tank? About 8,000 litres!

**Child:** 8,000 litres! That is 8,000 times 1,000 ml — 80 lakh millilitres!

**Mother:** Now you are converting automatically. And the same tricky rule applies here too — 4 litres 6 ml is 4,006 ml, NOT 4,600 ml. The 6 ml stays as 6, not 600.

**Child:** Same trap as the weight conversion! I will be extra careful.

## Reading the Weighing Scale

**Child:** Maa, at the kirana store, the digital scale showed 1.650 kg for the chana dal. But the old balance scale at the village shop uses metal weights. How does that one work?

**Mother:** The balance scale is a brilliant invention. You place the dal on one pan and known metal weights on the other pan. When both sides are perfectly level — like a see-saw with equal load on both ends — you know the dal weighs exactly as much as the metal weights.

**Child:** What if the dal is between two standard weights?

**Mother:** That is why there are smaller weights too — 500 g, 200 g, 100 g, 50 g, 20 g, 10 g, and even 5 g. You keep adding smaller weights until the pans balance. It is like making exact change with coins!

**Child:** And on a spring scale with a dial, what do the small lines mean?

**Mother:** Always check two things first — the maximum the scale can weigh, and what each mark represents. If the scale goes up to 5 kg and has 10 marks between each kg, each mark is 100 g. But if it has 20 marks, each mark is 50 g. Read the markings before you read the weight!

## The Maharaja's Donation — A Multiplication Problem

**Child:** Maa, the chapter mentions a maharaja who donates rice equal to 12 times his weight. If he donated 960 kg, his weight must be 80 kg. But what if he weighed 75 kg instead?

**Mother:** Then the donation would be 75 times 12. Let us use the break and multiply trick: 75 times 12 = 75 times 10 plus 75 times 2 = 750 + 150 = 900 kg. That is 9 quintals of rice!

**Child:** What if every child in my class of 35 donated rice equal to their own weight? If the average weight is about 32 kg, that would be 35 times 32. Let me try — 35 times 30 = 1,050 and 35 times 2 = 70. Total is 1,120 kg!

**Mother:** That is 11 quintals and 20 kg of rice — more than 1 tonne! Enough to feed hundreds of families during a flood relief camp. This is actually how community donations work across India — small individual contributions add up to something huge.

**Child:** Maths helps me see how small efforts can make a big difference.

**Mother:** That is a lesson worth more than any textbook, beta.
