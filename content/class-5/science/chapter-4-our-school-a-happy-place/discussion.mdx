## What Makes School a Happy Place?

**Child:** Maa, two students in the chapter -- Priya and Arjun -- want to make their school a "green school." What does "green" mean here? They are not painting the school green, are they?

**Mother:** Ha ha, no beta! A green school means a school where everyone works together to take care of the environment. It is about building daily habits -- separating waste into the right bins, turning off fans and lights when a room is empty, saving water, and planting more trees. It is about making the school better for people AND for nature.

**Child:** That sounds like a lot of work!

**Mother:** It sounds like a lot, but each action by itself is tiny. Flipping a switch when you leave a room takes one second. Putting a banana peel in the green bin instead of the blue bin takes two seconds. Reporting a leaky tap takes thirty seconds. When 500 students each do these small things every day, the school transforms completely.

**Child:** Our school also has a "Green Committee." We take turns checking if classrooms are clean.

**Mother:** That is exactly the spirit! The Swachh Bharat Swachh Vidyalaya programme encourages students to become caretakers of their own school environment. Remember the village of Mawlynnong in Meghalaya -- it is called Asia's Cleanest Village because every single person there, from children to grandparents, takes responsibility for keeping it spotless. If a whole village can do it, a school certainly can!

## Waste Management -- Why Two Bins Matter

**Child:** Maa, why do we need a green bin and a blue bin? Why not just throw everything into one big bin?

**Mother:** Because wet waste and dry waste have completely different futures, beta. Wet waste -- banana peels, leftover rice, vegetable scraps -- is organic. If you put it in a compost pit, microbes will break it down into rich dark soil that is like a superfood for plants. But if you mix a plastic wrapper or a glass bottle with those banana peels, the plastic contaminates everything and neither can be processed properly.

**Child:** And dry waste can be recycled?

**Mother:** Exactly! Paper, cardboard, plastic bottles, and metal cans can all be collected and sent to recycling units. The kabadiwala who comes to our colony collects these materials and sends them to factories where they become new products. An old plastic bottle might become the fabric of a school bag! An old newspaper could become a brand new notebook.

**Child:** But Maa, I see people throwing everything into one pile on the street. Does separating actually make a difference?

**Mother:** It makes an enormous difference! When waste is mixed up, most of it ends up in giant landfills -- those massive garbage mountains that you can sometimes smell from kilometres away. They pollute the soil, the water, and the air. But when waste is separated at the source -- right there in your classroom or kitchen -- each type goes to the right place. Wet waste becomes compost. Dry waste gets recycled. And the amount going to landfills drops dramatically. It is also a matter of respect for the sanitation workers who handle our waste every day.

## Saving Water at School

**Child:** Maa, a dripping tap is just a few drops. How much water can it really waste?

**Mother:** Let us work out the maths together. A dripping tap loses roughly one litre every hour. That is 24 litres in a day. In a month, that is about 720 litres. Over a full year, one dripping tap wastes nearly 8,700 litres! That is enough drinking water for a family of four for almost a month.

**Child:** That is shocking! And if a school has several dripping taps?

**Mother:** Now you see the problem! If three taps are leaking, that is over 26,000 litres wasted in a year. Fixing a tap costs almost nothing, but the water it saves is worth a fortune. That is why the "Water Watchers" team in the chapter is so important -- students who walk around the school checking for leaks and reporting them.

**Child:** There is a tap near our school library that has been dripping for weeks. I am going to report it tomorrow morning!

**Mother:** That is real leadership, beta -- noticing a problem and taking action instead of walking past it. And here is another idea: if your school collects rainwater from its big rooftop during the monsoon, that water can be stored and used for the garden throughout the year, or directed into the ground to recharge groundwater beneath the school.

## Keeping Cool the Smart Way

**Child:** Maa, why is our classroom on the ground floor always cooler than the one on the top floor, even without a fan?

**Mother:** Several reasons! The ground floor classroom has big windows on two sides, so a nice breeze flows through -- that is called cross-ventilation. There is a huge neem tree right outside that shades the windows. And the thick walls keep heat from getting in. The top floor classroom, on the other hand, gets direct sunlight on its roof all day, heating it up like a tawa.

**Child:** The chapter says white roofs are cooler than dark ones. Is that why houses in Rajasthan are often white?

**Mother:** Exactly right! In Rajasthan, Jodhpur is called the "Blue City" because many houses are painted light blue, which reflects sunlight and keeps them cool in the scorching desert heat. It is the same principle as wearing a white cotton kurta in summer instead of a dark polyester shirt. Light colours bounce the sun's rays away, while dark colours absorb them and heat up.

**Child:** And matkas cool water without electricity -- that is so clever!

**Mother:** Traditional Indian architecture and tools are full of genius. Matkas use evaporation to cool water naturally. Old houses were built with thick walls and internal courtyards -- the courtyard allowed hot air to rise and escape, keeping the rooms around it cool. In Ladakh during winter, some families keep livestock on the ground floor, and the animals' body heat rises to warm the rooms above. Nature provides heating and cooling if we are smart enough to use its principles!

## Fire Safety and Traffic Safety

**Child:** Maa, we had a fire drill at school last Thursday, and I was confused about what to do. Some children were screaming and running. Can you explain the correct steps?

**Mother:** Fire drills exist so that when a real emergency happens, everyone already knows the plan. Rule number one: do not panic. Walk briskly toward the nearest exit -- do not run, because running can cause people to trip and fall. Rule two: if there is smoke, get low. Crouch down or crawl, because smoke rises and the air near the floor is cleaner. Cover your nose and mouth with your dupatta or handkerchief. Rule three: never hide in cupboards or bathrooms -- rescuers will not find you there. Rule four: once you are outside, go straight to the assembly point and stay with your class. Never go back into the building for any reason.

**Child:** What if a younger child is scared and crying?

**Mother:** Hold their hand, speak calmly, and guide them toward the exit. But never put yourself in danger. And always tell a teacher immediately if you notice someone is missing. The most important goal is that everyone gets out safely.

**Child:** And what about road safety near school?

**Mother:** The school gate area is one of the busiest and most dangerous spots. Always use the zebra crossing. Look left, right, and left again before stepping onto the road. Never run across. If you ride a bicycle, wearing a helmet is not a choice -- it is a necessity. A helmet can mean the difference between walking away from a fall and a very serious injury.

## Being Kind -- The Heart of a Happy School

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says a happy school is about more than just clean buildings and green gardens. It says it is about how we treat each other. Why would that be in a science chapter?

**Mother:** Because well-being is a science, beta! Research shows that students learn better and perform better when they feel safe, included, and respected. When someone is bullied or teased, their brain actually gets stressed -- they cannot concentrate, they feel anxious, and their grades can slip. A kind word, on the other hand, can light up someone's entire day.

**Child:** Last week, a new girl joined our class and she was sitting alone during lunch. I asked her to sit with my group, and now she is one of my closest friends!

**Mother:** That single act of kindness changed her entire experience at a new school. That is exactly what this chapter is about. Waiting your turn in the lunch line, using polite words, not making fun of someone's accent or appearance, keeping shared spaces clean -- these are not just good manners. They are the foundation of a school where everyone feels they belong.

**Child:** So being kind is not just about being nice -- it actually helps everyone learn better?

**Mother:** Absolutely. And here is the wonderful thing about kindness -- it spreads. When you are kind to one person, they feel good and are more likely to be kind to someone else. Like ripples from a stone thrown into a pond, one act of kindness travels outward and touches many lives.
