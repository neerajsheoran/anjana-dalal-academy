## Why Water Matters

**Child:** Maa, our teacher said that even though Earth looks blue from space because of all the water, we still have a water problem. How is that possible?

**Mother:** Think of it this way, beta. Imagine I give you a plate of 100 biscuits, but I tell you that only 3 of them are safe to eat. And out of those 3, one and a half are locked inside a freezer you cannot open. So you are really left with only about one and a half biscuits from the whole plate!

**Child:** That is so little! So most of Earth's water is salty ocean water?

**Mother:** Exactly. About 97 percent of all the water on our planet is in the oceans -- far too salty for drinking or farming. The remaining 3 percent is freshwater, but most of that is trapped in glaciers and ice caps at the North and South Poles. The tiny fraction that flows in rivers, fills lakes, and sits underground is what 800 crore people on Earth must share.

**Child:** No wonder Maa always tells me not to waste water while brushing!

**Mother:** And rightly so! A running tap pours out about 6 litres of water every minute. If you leave it running for the 2 minutes you brush, that is 12 litres gone. Do that twice a day, and in one year you waste over 8,000 litres -- enough to fill a small tanker!

## Three Forms of Water

**Child:** Maa, I understand that water can be ice, liquid, or steam. But why does the same water change form?

**Mother:** Imagine a group of your classmates in the assembly ground. When the principal says "stand in line," everyone stands still, close together, holding position -- that is like ice. When the bell rings for break, everyone starts walking around the corridor, moving freely but staying nearby -- that is liquid water. And when school gets over, everyone runs in different directions, far apart -- that is like steam!

**Child:** So it is all about how much energy the water particles have?

**Mother:** Spot on! When you heat water, you give its tiny particles more energy, so they move faster and farther apart. When you cool water down, the particles slow down and stick closer together. That is the whole secret behind water changing its form.

**Child:** Maa, why does ice float in my nimbu paani? Should not a solid sink?

**Mother:** That is a wonderful question! Most solids do sink in their own liquid -- like a piece of iron sinks in melted iron. But water is special. When water freezes, its particles arrange themselves in a pattern that actually takes up MORE space. So ice is lighter than the same amount of liquid water, and it floats. This is a gift of nature -- if ice sank, ponds and lakes would freeze from the bottom up, and all the fish would die!

## The Water Cycle

**Child:** Maa, where exactly does rain come from? Does someone pour water from the sky?

**Mother:** Ha ha, no beta! Nature has its own brilliant recycling system. The sun heats up water in rivers, oceans, and even puddles on the road. This warm water slowly turns into an invisible gas called water vapour and rises up into the sky. Way up high, where the air is cold, this vapour cools down and turns back into tiny droplets that clump together and form clouds.

**Child:** And when the clouds get too heavy, the water falls as rain?

**Mother:** Exactly! It is like a sponge -- you keep adding water to it, and at some point it cannot hold any more and starts dripping. The water that falls as rain flows into rivers, soaks into the ground, or reaches the oceans, and then the whole cycle repeats. It has been going on for millions and millions of years.

**Child:** So the glass of water I drank today could have been rain falling over Jaipur last month?

**Mother:** It could have been part of a river in Kerala, a snowflake in Ladakh, or even water that a fish swam through in the ocean. Water is never created new or destroyed -- it just keeps travelling through this endless cycle. That is why keeping our rivers and oceans clean matters so much. Whatever dirt we put in, nature sends right back to us.

## Groundwater

**Child:** Maa, when we go to Naani's village, they get water from a borewell. Where is that water hiding?

**Mother:** When it rains on open land, some water runs off into streams, but a lot of it slowly trickles down through the soil. It passes through layers of sand and rock, going deeper and deeper, until it reaches a hard rocky layer it cannot pass through. The water pools there, underground, like a hidden lake. That is groundwater.

**Child:** But Maa, in our city there is cement and tar everywhere. Can rain soak through that?

**Mother:** That is exactly the problem cities face! In Naani's village, there are open fields and trees, so rainwater easily seeps into the soil and refills the groundwater. But in cities like Delhi or Mumbai, roads, footpaths, and parking lots are covered with concrete. Rain cannot get through, so it just flows away into drains. Meanwhile, people keep pumping groundwater out with borewells. The result? Groundwater levels keep dropping year after year.

**Child:** Is there anything we can do?

**Mother:** Yes! Many cities are now encouraging **rainwater harvesting** -- collecting rain from rooftops and directing it into special pits that let the water seep underground. In Rajasthan, villagers have built earthen structures called **Johads** for centuries to collect rainwater and recharge groundwater. And the village of Hiware Bazar in Maharashtra turned itself from a dry, water-scarce place into a green, water-rich village by harvesting every drop of rain that fell. Our ancestors knew how precious water was!

## Life in Water

**Child:** Maa, I saw a bright blue kingfisher near the park pond today! It suddenly dived into the water and came out with a fish in its beak. Do many creatures live in ponds?

**Mother:** Oh yes! A pond is like a small city with many different residents. Fish swim in the deeper water. Frogs sit on lily pads or hop along the edges -- they can live both in water and on land. Dragonflies zoom above the surface, and their babies actually grow up underwater for months before they come out and fly!

**Child:** And the lotus? Its roots are stuck in muddy water, but the flower looks so clean.

**Mother:** That is because lotus leaves have a special waxy coating. Water and mud simply slide off the surface like drops rolling off an umbrella. Scientists were so impressed by this that they studied lotus leaves and invented self-cleaning paints and fabrics! Nature is the best inventor.

**Child:** The chapter talks about the Fishing Cat. A cat that catches fish? That sounds so strange!

**Mother:** It is real! The Fishing Cat lives in India's wetlands and mangrove forests. Its paws are partly webbed -- like a duck's feet -- which helps it paddle through water. It actually dives underwater to catch fish with its claws. Most cats hate water, but this one is a champion swimmer!

## Why We Must Save Water

**Child:** Maa, I am just one kid. Can I really make a difference by saving water?

**Mother:** Let me show you the power of numbers. If you close the tap while brushing and save 12 litres a day, that is 4,380 litres in a year. Now, your school has about 500 students. If every student does this, that is 500 times 4,380 -- which is nearly 22 lakh litres saved in just one year! All from one simple habit.

**Child:** 22 lakh litres! That could fill a whole water tanker many times over!

**Mother:** And there are so many other ways too. When Maa washes dal or vegetables, the leftover water goes straight to our tulsi plant. The water from rinsing rice is actually nutritious for plants. Even taking a shorter bath by just 2 minutes saves about 20 litres each time.

**Child:** Starting tomorrow, I will make sure nobody leaves taps running at home. And I will tell my friends at school too!

**Mother:** That is the spirit, beta! One person's actions inspire others. And remember -- the water we save today is the water someone else gets to drink tomorrow. Every single drop counts.
