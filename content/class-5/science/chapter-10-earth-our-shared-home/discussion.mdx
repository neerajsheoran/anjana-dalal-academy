## Seeing Earth from Space

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says that from space, no borders between countries are visible. Does India really just look like one piece of a big blue ball?

**Mother:** Yes, beta! Astronauts say that seeing Earth from space is one of the most powerful experiences of their lives. There are no painted lines, no fences, no walls between nations -- just blue oceans, green forests, brown deserts, and beautiful white clouds swirling everywhere. Group Captain Shubhanshu Shukla, the first Indian to reach the International Space Station, said the Earth looks like one single home. And Rakesh Sharma, our first astronaut, replied "Saare Jahaan Se Achcha" when asked how India looked from above!

**Child:** If nature has no borders, why do we have countries?

**Mother:** Countries are something humans created to organise ourselves -- to have governments, laws, and order. But nature does not recognise any of that. The wind blows from one country to another without stopping. Rivers flow across national borders. Birds migrate across entire continents. Even pollution does not stay within one country's boundaries -- it spreads everywhere. That is why protecting the environment has to be a global effort.

**Child:** So looking after the Earth is everyone's responsibility, not just one country's?

**Mother:** Exactly. Problems like climate change, ocean pollution, and deforestation affect the whole planet. The ancient Indian saying "Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam" -- the world is one family -- has never been more relevant than it is right now.

## Things India Gave the World

**Child:** Maa, I was so surprised to learn that sugar, yoga, chess, and even the concept of zero all came from India! What else did we give the world?

**Mother:** So much, beta! India was the first place where people figured out how to press sugarcane and make jaggery and sugar from its juice. This knowledge travelled through Arab traders to Europe and eventually to the whole world. Yoga, which began in India over 3,000 years ago, is now practised in nearly every country -- the United Nations even declared 21 June as International Day of Yoga!

**Child:** And zero? Indians really invented zero?

**Mother:** Indian mathematicians were the first to develop zero as a real number and use it in calculations. Without zero, there would be no modern maths, no computers, no smartphones! The game of chess was also born in India -- it was originally called Chaturanga. Even the board game Snakes and Ladders started here as a moral teaching game about good and bad deeds.

**Child:** India has shared so many incredible things. Did anything come to us from other places?

**Mother:** Plenty! Chillies, tomatoes, potatoes, peanuts, and cashew nuts all came from the Americas, carried to India by Portuguese sailors about 400 to 500 years ago. Can you imagine making sabzi without any of those?

**Child:** No chillies? No potatoes? No tomatoes? Our food would be completely different!

**Mother:** Completely! Before chillies arrived, Indians used black pepper -- kali mirch -- for spiciness. And that gorgeous marigold flower that decorates every wedding and temple? It originally came from Mexico!

## The Travelling Rosy Starling

**Child:** Maa, the chapter tells the story of Rosy Starlings -- birds that fly from Russia to India every winter. Why do they travel so far?

**Mother:** Rosy Starlings breed in the grasslands of southern Russia and Mongolia during summer. But when winter hits, those places become bitterly cold and food becomes very hard to find. So the birds fly thousands of kilometres southward to India, where it is warm, sunny, and full of insects to eat.

**Child:** What kind of insects do they eat here?

**Mother:** Locusts and grasshoppers -- the very insects that destroy farmers' crops! Indian farmers actually welcome Rosy Starlings because they act as free, natural pest controllers. A single flock can gobble up millions of crop-eating insects.

**Child:** So a bird from Russia helps Indian farmers protect their wheat and rice? That is teamwork across countries!

**Mother:** Nature's own version of international cooperation, beta! The birds get food and warmth in India, and Indian farmers get pest control in return. Everyone benefits. This is why protecting the wetlands and resting spots along their migration route is so important -- without safe places to rest during the journey, many birds would not survive the trip.

## Indian Cows in Brazil

**Child:** Maa, the story about our Indian cows becoming famous in Brazil was really surprising! How did that happen?

**Mother:** About 200 years ago, Portuguese traders loaded some Indian cows onto ships and took them to Brazil in South America. Three breeds in particular -- Gir and Kankrej from Gujarat, and Ongole from Andhra Pradesh -- adapted wonderfully to Brazil's hot tropical climate because the weather there is quite similar to India's. These cows were strong, gave rich milk, and resisted diseases better than European breeds.

**Child:** And now most of Brazil's milk comes from Indian breeds?

**Mother:** More than three-fourths of it! These Indian cattle became so important to Brazil's dairy industry that their images were printed on Brazilian postage stamps and coins. It is a reminder that India's contributions to the world go far beyond just ideas and spices -- even our animals have made a global impact!

**Child:** Just like yoga and sugar and chess -- our cows also went around the world!

**Mother:** And our spices too! India was called the "Land of Spices." Traders from Europe and Arabia sailed dangerous oceans for months just to buy Indian pepper, cardamom, and cinnamon. The Portuguese explorer Vasco da Gama crossed entire oceans to reach India's Malabar Coast for black pepper -- which was so valuable back then that people called it "black gold."

## Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam — The World Is One Family

**Child:** Maa, the chapter talks about "Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam." What does it really mean for us in our everyday life?

**Mother:** It is a Sanskrit phrase from ancient Indian texts, beta, and it means "the entire world is one family." It teaches us to see every person -- regardless of which country they come from, what language they speak, or what they look like -- as a member of our own family. Just as you would share food with your brother or help your cousin with homework, we should care about people and nature everywhere.

**Child:** But practically, in my daily life, how can I follow this idea?

**Mother:** In many small but meaningful ways! When you save water at home, you are helping farmers further down the river who need that water for their crops. When you plant a sapling, you are creating oxygen that every breathing creature on the planet shares. When you refuse a plastic bag at the shop, you are protecting oceans where turtles and dolphins swim. Every thoughtful action connects you to the larger world.

**Child:** And DIGIPIN -- the digital address -- connects every small village to the rest of India too, right?

**Mother:** Yes! DIGIPIN assigns every location in India a unique 10-character code. Even a tiny hamlet with no proper road name can now be found by a delivery person, an ambulance, or a postman. It is technology bringing every Indian closer to every other Indian -- digital Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam!

## Caring for Our Shared Home

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says Earth is the only planet we know that has life. What happens if we do not take care of it?

**Mother:** That is the most important question of our time, beta. For billions of years, Earth has given us everything -- clean air, fresh water, fertile soil, and shelter for every creature. But human activities like burning too much coal and petrol, cutting down forests, dumping waste into rivers, and piling up mountains of plastic are putting enormous strain on our planet.

**Child:** But I am just one person. What difference can I make?

**Mother:** More than you think! Save water by closing the tap while brushing your teeth. Plant a tree on your birthday -- it will clean the air for decades. Carry a cloth bag when you go shopping instead of taking a plastic one. Walk or cycle to nearby places instead of asking for a ride. Eat local, seasonal fruits and vegetables instead of packaged food. And most importantly -- talk about these things with your friends and classmates. When many people make small changes, the impact is huge.

**Child:** The chapter says Earth is a gift we must protect. I think of it more like a home -- and you should never mess up your own home.

**Mother:** And this home does not belong only to us, beta. It belongs to the elephants in the jungle, the fish in the rivers, the sparrows on our windowsill, and every child who will be born in the years to come. Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam means the Earth family includes every living being -- not just humans.

**Child:** Then I want to be a responsible member of this family.

**Mother:** You already are, beta. By learning about these things, by caring, and by acting -- you are already making the Earth a little bit better every day.
