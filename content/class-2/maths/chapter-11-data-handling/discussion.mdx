## What is Data?

**Child:** Maa, teacher said we are going to learn about "data". What is data?

**Mother:** Data is just **information**, beta. When you collect answers to a question and write them down, that is data!

**Child:** Like what kind of question?

**Mother:** Any question! For example, you can ask your friends, "What is your favourite ice cream flavour?" Then you write down each friend's answer. All those answers together are your data.

**Child:** Oh! So if Riya says chocolate, Arjun says mango, and Sana says vanilla — that is data?

**Mother:** Exactly! But if you ask 20 friends, it becomes a long messy list. You cannot tell which flavour is the most popular just by looking at a long list. That is why we **organise** the data.

**Child:** How do we organise it?

**Mother:** We make a **table**! Count how many friends said chocolate, how many said mango, how many said vanilla, and so on. Then put it in neat rows. Now you can easily see which flavour has the biggest number — that is the winner!

## Reading Tables and Comparing

**Child:** Maa, I made a table today! I asked 10 friends which sabzi they like most. Aloo got 4, matar got 3, gobhi got 2, and palak got 1.

**Mother:** Wonderful! So which sabzi is the most popular among your friends?

**Child:** Aloo! It got the highest number — 4!

**Mother:** And which is the least popular?

**Child:** Palak, with only 1. Poor palak!

**Mother:** Ha ha! Now tell me — how many more friends like aloo than gobhi?

**Child:** Aloo is 4, gobhi is 2. So 4 minus 2 = 2 more friends like aloo.

**Mother:** See how useful a table is? Without it, you would have to read through the whole list again and again. With a table, you can answer questions quickly. You can compare groups using words like "more than", "less than", "most", and "least".

**Child:** Maa, can I collect data about our building? Like how many families have a dog?

**Mother:** That is a brilliant idea! Go door to door, ask politely, write down the answers, and make a table. You will be a real data collector!

## Pictographs — Pictures Tell the Story

**Child:** Maa, teacher drew a picture chart today. She drew little stars for each child. She called it a pictograph. What is that?

**Mother:** A **pictograph** shows data using small pictures instead of just numbers. Each picture stands for one thing — or sometimes more.

**Child:** So instead of writing "Cricket = 9 children", I draw 9 tiny stars next to cricket?

**Mother:** Exactly! And then you draw 4 stars next to kho-kho, 6 stars next to kabaddi, and so on. When you look at the chart, the game with the **longest row of stars** is the most popular.

**Child:** Oh, so I do not even need to read the numbers! I can just see which row is longest!

**Mother:** That is the magic of pictographs — they show information **visually**. Even someone who does not know the exact numbers can see which group is the biggest just by looking at the lengths.

**Child:** Can I make a pictograph about favourite fruits in our family?

**Mother:** Sure! Ask Dadi, Papa, me, your Didi, and your cousins. Use small fruit drawings — a tiny mango for each person who likes mango, a tiny apple for apple lovers. Then see which fruit has the most drawings!

**Child:** Mango will definitely win, Maa! Everyone in our family loves mango!

**Mother:** Ha ha, let the data speak, beta. You might be surprised! That is the beauty of collecting data — it shows us what is really true, not just what we guess.
