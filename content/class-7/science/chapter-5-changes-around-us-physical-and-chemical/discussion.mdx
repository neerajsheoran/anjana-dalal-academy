## Sorting Changes into Two Camps

**Meera:** Arjun, I watched mummy melt ghee on the tawa this morning and got confused. Is that a physical change or a chemical change?

**Arjun:** Physical, yaar. The solid ghee just turns into liquid ghee. Same stuff. Put it in the fridge and it goes solid again.

**Meera:** OK, but then she fried a puri in that same ghee. The puri puffed up, turned golden-brown, smelled completely different. That can't be physical.

**Arjun:** No, that's chemical. Heat changes the starch in the dough, proteins twist around — totally new substances. You can't un-fry a puri.

**Meera:** So the real question is whether something new is formed.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Grinding spices, freezing kulfi, dissolving mishri in chai — all physical. The material is still itself.

**Meera:** And burning agarbatti, milk setting into dahi, a nail rusting in the verandah, a kachcha aam ripening?

**Arjun:** All chemical. They usually give themselves away — new colour, new smell, bubbles, sometimes heat. The material has actually become something else.

## Understanding the Fire Triangle

**Meera:** Arjun, at puja last night I noticed something. When daadi placed a steel katori over a diya flame, it died in seconds. Why?

**Arjun:** Oxygen got cut off. Every flame needs three things — fuel that can burn, oxygen from the air, and enough heat to reach its ignition temperature. Take away any one of the three and the fire dies.

**Meera:** That's called the fire triangle, right? So when firefighters spray water, they're removing heat?

**Arjun:** Yes. Water absorbs heat and pulls the burning material below its ignition temperature. Sand smothers the fire by blocking oxygen. Carbon dioxide extinguishers do the same thing — push the oxygen away.

**Meera:** Three different attacks on the same triangle. What's combustible, though?

**Arjun:** Dry wood, paper, cotton, ghee, kerosene, LPG, the cow dung cakes we still see in villages — all combustible. Steel, glass, bricks — not under normal conditions.

**Meera:** Daadi told me one safety thing that stuck with me. If someone's clothes catch fire, wrap them in a thick cotton blanket. Never a synthetic dupatta or saree.

**Arjun:** Right, synthetic fabrics melt at high heat. They stick to skin and make burns far worse. Cotton smothers the flame instead.

## A Diya Burns — Both Changes at Once

**Meera:** Arjun, here's something that's been bothering me. The brass diya at evening aarti — when it burns, is that physical or chemical?

**Arjun:** Hmm. Trick question?

**Meera:** Actually I think it's both. Watch carefully next time. The oil near the flame gets warm and stays liquid — physical. The liquid oil climbs up the cotton wick through tiny channels — also physical.

**Arjun:** And then at the tip?

**Meera:** At the tip the oil vapour meets oxygen and burns. Carbon dioxide, water vapour, a little soot. New substances. Chemical change.

**Arjun:** So one diya is doing both at the same time. That's actually cool. Same thing must be true of cooking rice, no? Water evaporating is physical, but the starch grains changing structure when heated must be chemical.

**Meera:** Exactly. Most real-world things are a blend. One more question — can all physical changes be reversed?

**Arjun:** Many can — freeze water back to ice, let a stretched rubber band snap back. But not all. If mummy chops bhindi, that's physical (no new substance) but you can't stick the pieces back together.

**Meera:** And most chemical changes are one-way.

**Arjun:** Right. You can't turn ash back into paper, or dahi back into milk. The new substances have completely different properties.

## Rocks Becoming Soil

**Meera:** Arjun, the pebbles in the river near my nani's village in Konkan are all smooth and round. How did they get that shape? They must have started as jagged rock.

**Arjun:** Erosion, yaar. The river water tumbled them against each other and against the riverbed for thousands of years. All the sharp edges got worn down.

**Meera:** And weathering is different from erosion?

**Arjun:** Yes. Weathering is the breaking down of rock where it stands. Imagine a boulder on a hillside. During the day, the sun heats it and it expands a little. At night, it cools and contracts. Years of this and small cracks form.

**Meera:** And then?

**Arjun:** Seeds lodge in the cracks. They sprout. The growing roots push the crack wider. Rainwater seeps in. In colder places, it freezes and the ice expands and splits the rock further. Even chemical reactions between rainwater and the minerals weaken it.

**Meera:** So weathering breaks the rock apart, and erosion carries the pieces away?

**Arjun:** Perfectly stated. And over millions of years, the broken rock mixes with decomposed plants and animals to become soil. The laterite soil of Kerala, the black soil of Vidarbha — both formed this way.

**Meera:** Time is the secret ingredient.

## Good Changes and Bad Changes

**Meera:** Arjun, here's something I keep wondering. Are some changes always good and others always bad?

**Arjun:** Depends entirely on the situation. Take decomposition. When your leftover sabzi goes bad in the fridge — bad change. But the same exact process in a compost pit turns kitchen scraps into rich manure for nani's vegetable patch.

**Meera:** Same change, different contexts. What about rusting?

**Arjun:** Rust weakens iron bridges, eats up railway tracks, ruins your cycle chain — clearly undesirable. But the iron oxide in rust is actually used as a red pigment in paints and pottery glazes. And weathering, which uses similar reactions, creates the very soil that grows our food.

**Meera:** Even the "bad" changes have their place.

**Arjun:** And the same is true the other way. LPG burning cooks our food — wonderful. But the same combustion in vehicles releases carbon dioxide and pollutants that choke Delhi in winter.

**Meera:** So understanding which is which gives us power?

**Arjun:** Exactly. We can encourage the helpful changes and prevent the harmful ones. That's the practical reason to study this chapter. Not just for exams, yaar.
