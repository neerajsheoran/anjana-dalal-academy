## Sorting Changes into Two Camps

**Child:** Maa, when you melt ghee on the tawa, is that a physical change or a chemical change?

**Mother:** Physical change. The solid ghee becomes liquid ghee, but the substance itself does not change -- it is still ghee. If you put the melted ghee in the fridge, it will solidify again. No new substance was created at any point.

**Child:** And when you fry a puri in that same ghee?

**Mother:** That is a chemical change. The heat causes chemical reactions in the wheat dough -- the starch breaks down, proteins change structure, and the puri puffs up and turns golden-brown. You cannot un-fry a puri and get raw dough back. New substances with different taste, colour, and texture have been created.

**Child:** So the test is whether something new is formed?

**Mother:** Exactly. Grinding spices, freezing kulfi, dissolving mishri in water, folding clothes -- all physical changes because the material stays the same. But burning incense, souring milk into dahi, rusting a nail, ripening a mango -- all chemical changes because entirely new substances appear. Chemical changes often give themselves away through colour changes, new smells, gas bubbles, or heat.

## Understanding the Fire Triangle

**Child:** Maa, why does the diya flame go out when you place an inverted steel bowl over it?

**Mother:** Because you cut off the oxygen supply. Every flame needs three things to survive: a combustible fuel, oxygen from the air, and enough heat to reach the ignition temperature. Take away any one of these three, and the fire dies. This is called the fire triangle.

**Child:** So when firefighters spray water, they are removing heat?

**Mother:** Yes! Water absorbs heat and cools the burning material below its ignition temperature. Sand works differently -- it smothers the fire by blocking oxygen. Carbon dioxide extinguishers also displace oxygen. Each method targets a different side of the fire triangle.

**Child:** What materials are combustible?

**Mother:** Dry wood, paper, cotton, ghee, kerosene, LPG gas, dried cow dung cakes -- all combustible. But steel, glass, and bricks are not combustible under normal conditions. And here is an important safety point: if someone's clothes catch fire, wrap them in a thick cotton blanket to cut off air. Never use a synthetic dupatta or saree -- synthetic fabrics melt and can stick to skin, making burns far worse.

## A Diya Burns -- Both Changes at Once

**Child:** Maa, when our brass diya is lit during evening puja, is the burning physical or chemical?

**Mother:** This is a wonderful question because the answer is both! Watch carefully next time. The oil near the flame gets warm and liquid -- that is a physical change. The liquid oil travels up the cotton wick through tiny channels -- still physical. But at the tip of the wick, the oil vapour meets oxygen and burns, producing carbon dioxide, water vapour, and a little soot. Those are entirely new substances, so the actual burning is a chemical change.

**Child:** So physical and chemical changes can happen at the same time in the same process?

**Mother:** Absolutely. A diya, a candle, even cooking rice -- the water evaporating is physical, but the starch in rice changing its molecular structure when heated is chemical. Many real-world processes are a blend of both types of change.

**Child:** Can all physical changes be reversed?

**Mother:** Many can -- freeze water back to ice, let stretched rubber snap back to shape. But not all. Chopping bhindi into pieces is a physical change (no new substance), but you cannot stick the pieces back together. And most chemical changes are permanent -- you cannot turn ash back into paper, or dahi back into milk.

## Rocks Becoming Soil

**Child:** Maa, the pebbles in the river near Nani's village are so smooth and round. How did they get that shape?

**Mother:** Those pebbles started as jagged, rough chunks of rock. Over thousands of years, flowing river water tumbled them against each other and against the riverbed. This constant grinding -- called erosion -- wore away all the sharp edges and smoothed their surfaces.

**Child:** And weathering is different from erosion?

**Mother:** Yes. Weathering is the breaking down of rock where it stands. Imagine a boulder on a hillside. During the day, the sun heats it and it expands slightly. At night, it cools and contracts. After years of this expansion and contraction, cracks form. Seeds lodge in the cracks, sprout, and the growing roots push the crack wider. Rainwater seeps in, and if it freezes in winter, the ice expands and splits the rock further. Chemical reactions between minerals in the rock and rainwater also weaken it.

**Child:** So weathering breaks rock apart, and erosion carries the pieces away?

**Mother:** Perfectly stated. And over immense stretches of time, the broken rock mixes with decomposed plant and animal matter to become soil. The laterite soil of Kerala and the black soil of Vidarbha both formed this way -- through millions of years of weathering and erosion.

## Good Changes and Bad Changes

**Child:** Maa, are some changes always good and others always bad?

**Mother:** It depends entirely on the situation! Take decomposition -- when your leftover sabzi goes bad in the fridge, that is an undesirable change. But the exact same process in a compost pit transforms kitchen waste into rich manure for Nani's vegetable garden. Same change, different contexts.

**Child:** What about rusting? Is that ever useful?

**Mother:** Rust weakens iron bridges, corrodes tools, and damages railway tracks -- clearly undesirable. But the iron oxide in rust is actually used as a red pigment in paints and pottery glazes. And weathering, which involves similar chemical reactions, creates the very soil that feeds us. So even seemingly harmful chemical changes have their place.

**Child:** And burning fuel?

**Mother:** Burning LPG cooks our food -- very useful. But the same combustion in vehicle engines releases carbon dioxide and pollutants that worsen air quality and contribute to climate change. Understanding these changes gives us the power to encourage the helpful ones and prevent or minimise the harmful ones. That is the practical value of studying physical and chemical changes.
