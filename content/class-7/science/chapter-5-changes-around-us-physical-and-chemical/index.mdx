## Introduction

From the moment you wake up, changes surround you. The alarm clock's battery slowly drains. Frost on the window pane disappears as the room warms up. Amma heats milk on the stove, and it transforms into steaming chai. Bread slices turn golden-brown in the toaster.

But are all these changes alike? Some merely alter how something looks, while others create entirely new substances that did not exist before. In this chapter, you will learn to sort changes into two fundamental categories -- **physical changes** and **chemical changes** -- and understand the important differences between them.

> **Key idea:** Changes are happening around us constantly. Some only modify the appearance or form of a substance, while others produce entirely new materials with different properties.

---

## Physical Changes

### Understanding Physical Changes

A **physical change** alters the outward characteristics of a substance -- its shape, size, state, or appearance -- but **does not create any new substance**. The original material remains exactly what it was.

**Everyday examples of physical changes:**

- **Tearing a sheet of paper** into small pieces -- Each piece is still paper. No new substance appears.
- **Stretching a rubber band** -- When you release it, it returns to its original shape. It is still rubber.
- **Grinding wheat into flour (atta)** -- The grains become powder, but the powder is still wheat. No new substance forms.
- **Melting ghee** -- Solid ghee becomes liquid when warmed, but it is still ghee. Cool it down and it solidifies again.
- **Evaporating water** -- Liquid water becomes invisible water vapour (steam), but it is still H2O. Cool the steam and water droplets form again.
- **Hanging wet clothes to dry** -- The water evaporates from the fabric, but both the clothes and the water remain unchanged.
- **Stirring salt into warm water** -- The salt dissolves and becomes invisible, but it is still present. Boil away the water and the salt crystals reappear.

> **Key idea:** In a **physical change**, only outward properties like shape, size, or state are altered. The substance itself remains the same. Many physical changes can be undone.

## Chemical Changes

### Understanding Chemical Changes

A **chemical change** occurs when one or more **entirely new substances** are produced. The starting material transforms into something fundamentally different through a process called a **chemical reaction**.

### Activity: Breathing into Lime Water

Perform this simple experiment to witness a chemical change:

1. Fill one-quarter of a glass tumbler with **ordinary water** and another with **chuna (lime) water**.
2. Using separate straws, blow your breath (exhale) into each tumbler.
3. **Ordinary water:** You see bubbles, but the water stays clear. No new substance forms.
4. **Lime water:** The clear liquid gradually becomes **cloudy and white**! A white powdery substance settles at the bottom.

What happened? The **carbon dioxide** in your exhaled breath reacted with the lime water (calcium hydroxide) and produced **calcium carbonate** -- a white, insoluble substance. This new substance made the liquid appear milky.

**Chemical equation:**
Calcium hydroxide + Carbon dioxide --> Calcium carbonate + Water

Because a new substance (calcium carbonate) appeared, this is a **chemical change**.

> **Key idea:** If lime water turns milky when a gas passes through it, the gas is **carbon dioxide**. This is a classic laboratory test.

### Activity: Lemon Juice and Baking Soda

Here is another chemical change you can observe at home:

1. Pour a tablespoon of **nimbu (lemon) juice** into a small glass or test tube.
2. Add half a teaspoon of **baking soda** (meetha soda / sodium bicarbonate).
3. Watch the vigorous **fizzing and bubbling**! A gas escapes rapidly.
4. If you capture this gas and pass it through lime water, the lime water turns milky -- confirming the gas is **carbon dioxide**.

**Chemical equation:**
Lemon juice + Baking soda --> Carbon dioxide + Other products

A new gaseous substance appeared, so this qualifies as a **chemical change**.

> **Key idea:** In a **chemical change**, new substances with different properties are created through a chemical reaction. Common signals include colour shifts, gas bubbles, heat generation, or a change in odour.

## Notable Examples of Chemical Changes

### Rusting

When iron items are left exposed to damp air, they develop a **reddish-brown crust** called rust. Rust is **iron oxide** -- a substance entirely different from iron itself. Because a new substance forms, rusting is a **chemical change**.

### Combustion (Burning)

**Combustion** is a chemical reaction where a substance combines with **oxygen** and releases **heat and/or light**.

**Example -- Setting a magnesium ribbon alight:**
- Magnesium + Oxygen --> Magnesium oxide + Heat + Light
- The original shiny metal ribbon is consumed, and a white powdery residue (magnesium oxide) remains. Since a new substance appears, this is a chemical change.

Materials that can undergo combustion are called **combustible substances**. Common examples: newspaper, dry leaves, cotton, cooking oil, kerosene, charcoal, and LPG cylinder gas.

### The Three Pillars of Fire (Fire Triangle)

![Fire triangle diagram showing the three requirements for combustion: fuel, oxygen, and heat](fire-triangle.png)

Every fire requires exactly three ingredients:

1. **A combustible substance** (fuel) -- something that can burn, such as wood or kerosene
2. **Oxygen** (from the surrounding air) -- without it, no fuel can ignite
3. **Sufficient heat** -- the fuel must be heated to its **ignition temperature**, the minimum temperature at which it catches fire

**Demonstration:** Light a small candle, then invert a glass tumbler over it. Within seconds, the flame dies out because the glass traps only a limited amount of air, and once the oxygen is used up, combustion cannot continue.

> **Key idea:** Fire needs three things: fuel, oxygen, and heat reaching the ignition temperature. Eliminate any one of these three, and the fire is extinguished. This principle is called the **fire triangle**.

### Smothering a Fire Safely

If someone's clothing catches fire, wrapping a thick cotton blanket tightly around them blocks the air supply, suffocating the flames. **Never use synthetic fabric** for this purpose -- synthetic materials can melt at high temperatures and fuse to the skin, causing far worse injuries.

## When Both Changes Happen Simultaneously

A **burning diya (oil lamp)** beautifully demonstrates both types of change occurring together:

![Burning candle showing both physical changes (melting wax) and chemical changes (burning wax vapour)](burning-candle-physical-chemical.png)

- The **oil heats up** and is drawn up the cotton wick by capillary action -- **physical change** (no new substance)
- Some oil may drip and pool -- **physical change** (liquid oil is still oil)
- At the tip of the wick, the oil vapour **ignites** and reacts with oxygen, producing carbon dioxide, water vapour, and soot -- **chemical change** (new substances are created)

So a single burning diya involves **both physical and chemical changes** happening at the same moment!

> **Key idea:** Certain everyday processes involve both physical and chemical changes occurring side by side.

## Reversible or Irreversible?

Some changes can be **undone** (reversed), while others are **permanent** (irreversible).

| Change | Can it be reversed? | Type of change |
|--------|-------------------|----------------|
| Freezing juice into an ice lolly | Yes (let it melt) | Physical |
| Heating water to make steam | Yes (cool the steam to get water) | Physical |
| Folding a bedsheet | Yes (unfold it) | Physical |
| Mixing salt into water | Yes (evaporate the water) | Physical |
| Slicing onions | No (cannot reassemble them) | Physical |
| Frying a papad | No (cannot unfry it) | Chemical |
| Burning a matchstick | No (ash cannot become a matchstick) | Chemical |
| Rusting of a bicycle chain | No (rust cannot revert to iron) | Chemical |
| Setting dahi from milk | No (dahi cannot become milk again) | Chemical |
| A banana turning brown | No (cannot make it fresh again) | Chemical |

Most **chemical changes are irreversible** because the new substances formed have completely different properties. Some **physical changes** can be reversed, though not all -- slicing vegetables permanently changes their shape even though no new substance is created.

> **Key idea:** **Reversible changes** can be undone to recover the original substance. **Irreversible changes** are permanent. Most chemical changes fall into the irreversible category.

## Helpful Changes and Harmful Changes

### Changes We Welcome

Many changes are beneficial and even essential:
- Cooking rajma in a pressure cooker (makes it safe and tasty to eat)
- Fermenting dosa batter overnight (makes dosas soft and fluffy)
- Fruits ripening on the tree (makes them sweet and nutritious)
- Milk setting into dahi (creates a nutritious food)

### Changes We Wish to Prevent

Some changes cause damage or loss:
- Rusting of iron bridges (weakens infrastructure)
- Food going stale or mouldy (waste of food)
- Forest fires (destroy ecosystems and habitats)

### Context Matters!

The same change can be beneficial in one situation and harmful in another:
- **Decomposition of kitchen scraps** is unwanted inside your refrigerator, but it is exactly what we want in a **compost pit** where waste turns into rich fertiliser for the garden.

### Environmental Consequences

Some human-caused changes have long-lasting effects:
- Burning petrol and diesel in vehicles releases **carbon dioxide** and other pollutants, contributing to **global warming**.
- Industrial smokestacks emit particles that reduce air quality and cause **respiratory problems** in cities like Delhi and Kanpur.

## Slow Changes Beneath Our Feet

### Weathering -- Rocks Crumbling Over Centuries

Have you ever seen loose stones and gravel at the base of a hillside? These fragments were once part of the massive rock above. Over thousands of years, natural forces slowly broke them apart.

![Diagram showing the weathering process: temperature changes, root growth, water freezing in cracks breaking rocks into soil](weathering-process.png)

**Forces that cause weathering:**

- **Day-night temperature swings** -- Rocks expand in daytime heat and contract at night. Repeated expansion and contraction creates tiny cracks that grow over time.
- **Tree roots** -- Seeds lodge in rock crevices, sprout, and as roots grow thicker, they prise the rock apart.
- **Freezing water** -- Rainwater seeps into cracks. In cold regions, this water freezes and expands (ice occupies more volume than liquid water), splitting the rock further.
- **Chemical reactions** -- Minerals in rock react slowly with rainwater and dissolved gases, weakening the rock's structure.

A striking example: the Deccan Plateau's basalt rock (dark, iron-rich) develops reddish patches after prolonged exposure to rain and air. The red colour comes from **iron oxide** -- the same chemical process as rusting.

These combined physical and chemical processes are called **weathering**, and over immense timescales, they transform solid rock into **soil**.

> **Key idea:** **Weathering** is the gradual breakdown of rocks through physical and chemical processes. It is one of nature's primary methods of creating soil.

### Erosion -- Moving the Broken Pieces

Once rocks have been broken into fragments by weathering, natural forces like **flowing rivers** and **blowing winds** carry these fragments to new locations. This transport process is called **erosion**.

- River pebbles are rounded and smooth because constant tumbling in flowing water wears down their rough edges over centuries.
- When water or wind slows down, the fragments settle (this settling is called **deposition**).
- Over geological timescales, deposited layers compact and harden into new sedimentary rock.
- These processes span thousands to millions of years and are essentially irreversible.

## Summary

- A **physical change** alters shape, size, or state without creating new substances.
- A **chemical change** produces one or more new substances through a chemical reaction.
- **Combustion** is a chemical reaction involving a substance reacting with oxygen to release heat and/or light.
- The **fire triangle** states that combustion requires fuel, oxygen, and sufficient heat (ignition temperature). Remove any one to extinguish the fire.
- Some processes, like a burning diya, involve both physical and chemical changes simultaneously.
- **Reversible changes** can be undone (like melting ice); **irreversible changes** cannot (like burning paper).
- Changes may be **desirable** (cooking food) or **undesirable** (rusting of iron), and sometimes the same change is both depending on context.
- **Weathering** breaks rocks into fragments through physical and chemical means, eventually forming soil.
- **Erosion** is the transportation of these fragments by wind and water to new locations.
