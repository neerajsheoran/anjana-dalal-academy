## Is the Sun Really Moving?

**Meera:** Arjun, every morning I watch the Sun climb up in the east and slide down in the west by evening. Is it really crossing the whole sky?

**Arjun:** Looks that way, yaar. But no — you are standing on a spinning planet. Remember last year's mela carousel?

**Meera:** The wobbly one? Yes.

**Arjun:** As the carousel turned, the chaat stalls and trees around us seemed to sweep past in the opposite direction. They weren't moving — we were. Same with the Sun. Earth spins from west to east. We spin with it. So the Sun looks like it travels east to west across the sky.

**Meera:** So sunrise hits eastern India before western India? Because Arunachal turns to face the Sun before Gujarat does?

**Arjun:** Exactly. Dawn in eastern India can be almost two hours earlier than in Rajasthan. And get this — Aryabhata figured all this out over 1500 years ago. Without a telescope.

**Meera:** No telescope, no satellite, no internet?

**Arjun:** Just observations and brilliant maths. He even compared it to a person sitting in a boat — as the boat glides along the river, the trees on the bank seem to drift backward. He calculated the Earth's spin time as 23 hours, 56 minutes, 4 seconds. Modern atomic clocks confirmed he was almost perfectly right.

**Meera:** Yaar, the next time someone says "Indian scientists" I am definitely starting with Aryabhata.

## Day, Night, and the Steadfast Pole Star

**Meera:** Arjun, why is one side of the Earth always in darkness while the other has daylight?

**Arjun:** Because the Earth is a sphere. Sunlight can only hit one half of a ball at a time. The half facing the Sun gets daytime, the other half gets night. Earth keeps spinning, so every spot cycles through day and night roughly every 24 hours.

**Meera:** OK that makes sense. But here is something Dadu told me last week. He said the Pole Star — Dhruva Tara — never moves in the sky. All the other stars seem to swing across, but Dhruva just sits there. Is that true?

**Arjun:** Yes, and it is one of my favourite things. Picture a spinning top. The very tip at the top stays still while everything around it whirls. Earth's axis of rotation — its imaginary spinning rod — points almost directly at the Pole Star. So as Earth spins around this axis, the Pole Star appears frozen while all other stars trace circles around it.

**Meera:** So if I watch the Saptarishi at 8 PM and then again at midnight, I would see it has shifted position around Dhruva Tara?

**Arjun:** Exactly. The Saptarishi — the Big Dipper — circles slowly through the night. Dhruva Tara is like the hub of a wheel, all other stars are on the spinning rim.

**Meera:** I am dragging Dadu to the terrace tonight to check. Old sailors used Dhruva Tara to navigate, na?

**Arjun:** And travellers across deserts and oceans. A single still star saved thousands of lives across centuries.

## The Tilt That Creates Seasons

**Meera:** Arjun, here is a question I keep getting wrong. Why is June so brutally hot in our mohalla but December freezing? Is Earth closer to the Sun in summer?

**Arjun:** That is the most common myth, yaar. Actually Earth is CLOSEST to the Sun in early January — the heart of our winter!

**Meera:** Hain? Then why is January cold?

**Arjun:** Because seasons are not about distance. They are about the tilt of Earth's axis. The axis is tilted at about 23.5 degrees, and this tilt stays constant as Earth orbits the Sun the whole year.

**Meera:** How does a tilt make summer and winter?

**Arjun:** In June, our half — the Northern Hemisphere where India sits — tilts toward the Sun. Sunlight strikes more directly, concentrating heat over a smaller area. Days are longer too. That is summer. In December, we tilt away from the Sun. Sunlight arrives at a slanted angle, spreading thin over a bigger area. Less warmth. Shorter days. That is winter.

**Meera:** So when we have summer here, Australia has winter?

**Arjun:** Exactly. Southern Hemisphere tilts away when we tilt toward. And around 21 June — our longest day — the North Pole has 24 hours of continuous daylight. Sun does not set at all. Then around 22 December, the North Pole is in total darkness for months.

**Meera:** No sunrise for MONTHS. I cannot even imagine. My cousin in Punjab complains when the December sun goes down at 5:30 PM.

**Arjun:** That is your tilted axis making winter feel personal.

## When the Sun Disappears and the Moon Turns Red

**Meera:** Arjun, what exactly is a solar eclipse? Daadi covered all the windows during the last one and would not let us step outside.

**Arjun:** A solar eclipse happens when the Moon slips between the Sun and Earth, casting its shadow on us and blocking sunlight temporarily. Even though the Moon is much tinier than the Sun, it is also way closer to us. By a remarkable coincidence, both appear almost exactly the same size in our sky. So the Moon can cover the Sun almost perfectly.

**Meera:** Can I watch the next one? It sounds incredible.

**Arjun:** It is — but extremely dangerous to look at directly. Never look at the Sun during a solar eclipse. Not with bare eyes, not through sunglasses, not even through dark glass or X-ray film. The Sun's rays can permanently damage your eyes and cause blindness.

**Meera:** Then how do people see it safely?

**Arjun:** Go to a planetarium event or astronomy club where they hand out certified solar filters. Or make a pinhole projector — project the eclipse onto white paper. Daadi was just being smart and safe.

**Meera:** OK, and a lunar eclipse? That is the one I am allowed to watch, right?

**Arjun:** Yes, totally safe. A lunar eclipse is when Earth comes between the Sun and the Moon, so Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. You can watch it with your bare eyes, no problem. During a total lunar eclipse the Moon does not vanish — it glows a deep reddish-copper colour.

**Meera:** Wait, why red?

**Arjun:** Earth's atmosphere bends some sunlight around the planet's edge, and red light bends the most. The Moon gets bathed in this faint reddish glow. People call it a "blood moon."

**Meera:** A blood moon. Yaar, that sounds straight out of a Tinkle comic.

## India's Gift to Astronomy

**Meera:** Arjun, did ancient Indians actually know about eclipses? Or did they just say "graha is unhappy, stay inside"?

**Arjun:** Way more than that. Indian astronomical texts like the Surya Siddhanta contain detailed mathematical formulas for predicting exactly when and where eclipses would occur. Eclipses are called "grahan" in Sanskrit. Indian scholars worked out the orbits of the Sun, Moon, and Earth using observation and maths alone — centuries before telescopes were invented.

**Meera:** They could predict FUTURE eclipses?

**Arjun:** With remarkable accuracy. The British East India Company folks in the 1700s were genuinely shocked to find Indian pandits predicting eclipse times more precisely than their imported instruments could.

**Meera:** That is incredible.

**Arjun:** Think about what it required — knowing how fast Earth rotates, how the Moon orbits Earth, how Earth orbits the Sun. All the principles in our chapter — rotation, revolution, alignment — Indian astronomers had figured out and were using to make calendars and astronomical tables.

**Meera:** And now Chandrayaan-3 landed at the Moon's south pole in 2023. From Surya Siddhanta to ISRO. Same questions, just better instruments.

**Arjun:** Exactly. From a child noticing that shadows change length through the day, to an astronomer realising Earth must be spinning, to a mathematician predicting an eclipse years in advance, to Vikram lander touching down softly — same path. Same curiosity.

**Meera:** Yaar, I want to go to Jantar Mantar in Jaipur during our summer holidays.

**Arjun:** Done. We will take notebooks and figure out how those huge stone sundials work. And then write to ISRO asking when they need their next intern.
