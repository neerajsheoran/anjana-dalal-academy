## Introduction

Step outside early in the morning and watch the Sun climb from the eastern horizon. By noon, it blazes overhead. By evening, it sinks below the western skyline. It certainly looks like the Sun is racing across the sky. But is it really moving, or are we the ones in motion?

In this chapter, we will uncover the truth behind the Earth's spinning and orbiting motions, understand why we experience day and night, why seasons change, and what causes the spectacular events known as eclipses.

> **Key idea:** The Earth **rotates** on its axis (causing day and night) and **revolves** around the Sun (causing seasons and the changing night sky).

---

## The Earth Spins on Its Axis

### The Carousel Analogy

![The Earth spins on its axis — the carousel analogy](the-earth-spins-on-its-axis-the-carousel-analogy.png)

Have you ever ridden a carousel at a mela? As the carousel spins, the stalls and people standing around it seem to sweep past you in the opposite direction. But it is you who is moving, not them.

Something identical happens with the Sun. We are passengers on a giant spinning ball -- the Earth. As the Earth rotates, the Sun appears to glide across the sky from east to west. In reality, the **Sun is stationary** relative to us -- it is the **Earth that is spinning**.

### Details of Earth's Rotation

![The Earth spins on its axis — details of Earth's rotation](the-earth-spins-on-its-axis-details-of-earths-rotation.png)

Think of the Earth as a giant ball spinning smoothly on an invisible rod. This imaginary rod is called the **axis of rotation**, and it runs from the **North Pole** straight through the centre to the **South Pole**.

![The Earth spins on its axis — details of Earth's rotation (key facts)](the-earth-spins-on-its-axis-details-of-earths-rotation-key-facts.png)

Key facts:
- The Earth takes approximately **24 hours** to complete one full rotation
- When viewed from a point above the North Pole, the Earth spins in the **anti-clockwise direction** -- that is, from **west to east**
- This west-to-east spin is why the Sun appears to travel from east to west across our sky

> **Key idea:** The Earth rotates on its axis from **west to east**, completing one spin in about **24 hours**. This rotation creates the illusion that the Sun moves from east to west.

## Day and Night

### Why Half the World is Always Dark

![Day and night — why half the world is always dark](day-and-night-why-half-the-world-is-always-dark.png)

The Earth is a sphere. At any given moment, sunlight illuminates only **one half** of this sphere:
- The half facing the Sun is bathed in light -- people there experience **daytime**
- The half turned away from the Sun is in shadow -- people there experience **nighttime**

As the Earth rotates continuously:
- Your location gradually turns to face the Sun -- that moment is **sunrise**, and your day begins
- Hours later, your location turns away from the Sun -- that moment is **sunset**, and night descends

This is why **dawn arrives first in India's eastern states** like Arunachal Pradesh and Meghalaya, and reaches western states like Gujarat and Rajasthan later. The Earth's west-to-east rotation brings eastern locations into sunlight before western ones.

### The Sun's Apparent Journey

![Day and night — the Sun's apparent journey](day-and-night-the-suns-apparent-journey.png)

If you stand on the Equator and face northward:
1. **Morning:** The Sun appears to rise on your **right** (east)
2. **Midday:** The Sun climbs to nearly overhead
3. **Evening:** The Sun descends on your **left** (west)

This daily journey is not the Sun moving -- it is the Earth carrying you in a slow, constant spin.

> **Key idea:** Day and night result from the **rotation of the Earth**. The sunlit half has day; the dark half has night. As the Earth spins, every location cycles through day and night.

## Stars and the Night Sky

### Stars Appear to Drift Too

![Stars and the night sky — stars appear to drift too](stars-and-the-night-sky-stars-appear-to-drift-too-1.png)

![Stars and the night sky — stars circling the Pole Star (Dhruva Tara)](stars-and-the-night-sky-stars-appear-to-drift-too-2.png)

The Earth's rotation affects not just the Sun's apparent position but also the stars at night. As the Earth spins, constellations appear to move slowly across the sky from east to west, mirroring the Sun's apparent daytime motion.

However, one star seems to stand perfectly still -- the **Pole Star** (known in India as **Dhruva Tara**). It barely moves because the Earth's axis of rotation points almost directly at it. Every other star appears to trace circular arcs around the Pole Star as the night progresses.

Observe the **Saptarishi** (Big Dipper) constellation at 8 PM and again at midnight. You will notice it has shifted position, appearing to have rotated around the stationary Pole Star.

### Aryabhata's Brilliant Insight

Over 1500 years ago, the great Indian mathematician-astronomer **Aryabhata** grasped the true nature of this apparent motion. In his masterwork **Aryabhatiya** (written in 499 CE), he compared the situation to a traveller on a moving boat: as the boat glides forward, trees and hills on the riverbank appear to move backward. Similarly, stars appear to move because we are on the spinning Earth.

Aryabhata calculated the Earth's rotation period as approximately **23 hours, 56 minutes, and 4.1 seconds** -- astonishingly close to the value confirmed by modern instruments!

## The Earth Orbits the Sun

### Rotation vs Revolution

![The Earth orbits the Sun — rotation vs revolution](the-earth-orbits-the-sun-rotation-vs-revolution-1.png)

![The Earth orbits the Sun — rotation and revolution illustrated](the-earth-orbits-the-sun-rotation-vs-revolution-2.png)

While spinning on its axis, the Earth simultaneously **travels around the Sun** along a nearly circular path. These are two distinct motions happening at the same time:

- **Rotation** = the Earth spinning on its own axis (like a top twirling)
- **Revolution** = the Earth moving along its orbit around the Sun (like a runner going around a track)

Key facts about revolution:
- The Earth follows a **nearly circular orbit** around the Sun
- One complete orbit takes approximately **365 days and 6 hours** (roughly one calendar year)
- The extra 6 hours accumulate over four years into an extra day, giving us a **leap year** with 366 days

### The Changing Night Sky

Because the Earth is slowly orbiting the Sun, the background of stars visible at night shifts gradually over the months. The constellation Orion (Mriga), prominent in January skies over India, is nowhere to be seen in July. Different constellations become visible in different seasons as the Earth moves to different positions in its orbit.

> **Key idea:** The Earth revolves around the Sun in about **365 days and 6 hours**. This revolution causes the night sky to change throughout the year and gives rise to the seasons.

## Why Seasons Change

### The Real Reason for Seasons

You may have noticed that summer days are long and hot while winter days are short and cold. But why?

The answer comes from two facts:
1. The Earth's **axis is tilted** at an angle (about 23.5 degrees from vertical)
2. This tilt remains **constant** as the Earth orbits the Sun -- the axis always points in the same direction in space

![Why seasons change — the real reason for seasons (tilted axis and Earth's orbit)](why-seasons-change-the-real-reason-for-seasons.png)

### How the Tilt Creates Seasons

**June (Summer in India and the Northern Hemisphere):**
- The **Northern Hemisphere** is tilted **towards** the Sun
- Sunlight strikes the northern half more directly, concentrating energy over a **smaller area** -- making it hotter
- The Northern Hemisphere gets sunlight for **more than 12 hours** each day -- longer days, shorter nights
- Result: **Summer** in India, and simultaneously **winter** in Australia

**December (Winter in India and the Northern Hemisphere):**
- The **Northern Hemisphere** is tilted **away** from the Sun
- Sunlight hits at a shallow angle, spreading over a **larger area** -- providing less warmth
- The Northern Hemisphere gets sunlight for **fewer than 12 hours** per day -- shorter days, longer nights
- Result: **Winter** in India, and simultaneously **summer** in Australia

> **Key idea:** Seasons exist because of the **tilt of Earth's axis**. Whichever hemisphere tilts towards the Sun gets more direct sunlight and longer days -- experiencing summer. The opposite hemisphere experiences winter.

### Key Calendar Dates

| Event | Approximate Date | What Happens |
|-------|-----------------|-------------|
| **Summer Solstice** | 21 June | Longest day in the Northern Hemisphere |
| **Winter Solstice** | 22 December | Shortest day in the Northern Hemisphere |
| **Spring Equinox** | 21 March | Day and night are equal (12 hours each) worldwide |
| **Autumn Equinox** | 23 September | Day and night are equal (12 hours each) worldwide |

### Extreme Cases

![Why seasons change — extreme cases at the poles and equator](why-seasons-change-extreme-cases.png)

- At the **North Pole**, the Sun rises around 21 March and remains continuously above the horizon for about **six months**, setting around 22 September. Then follows six months of unbroken darkness. The South Pole experiences the reverse.
- Along the **Equator**, day and night are nearly equal (about 12 hours each) throughout the year. This is why southern Indian states like Kerala and Tamil Nadu experience milder seasonal temperature changes than northern states like Punjab and Uttar Pradesh.

### Busting a Common Myth

Some people believe seasons change because the Earth moves closer to or farther from the Sun during its orbit. This is incorrect for two reasons:
- If distance were the cause, the entire planet would experience the same season simultaneously. But when it is summer in India, it is winter in New Zealand.
- The Earth is actually **nearest** to the Sun in early **January** -- right in the middle of the Northern Hemisphere's winter!

## Eclipses

### Solar Eclipse

A **solar eclipse** occurs when the **Moon passes between the Sun and the Earth**, casting its shadow on a part of the Earth's surface and temporarily blocking sunlight.

![Eclipses — solar eclipse (Moon between Sun and Earth)](eclipses-solar-eclipses.png)

**How can the tiny Moon block the enormous Sun?** The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon, but it is also roughly 400 times farther away. This cosmic coincidence makes the **apparent size** of both objects nearly equal when viewed from Earth. The Moon can therefore appear to cover the Sun almost perfectly.

You can understand this with your thumb: stretch your arm out and hold your thumb up. Your small thumb can easily cover a large building far away because your thumb is much closer to your eyes.

**Types of solar eclipse:**

![Eclipses — types of solar eclipses (total and partial)](eclipses-types-of-solar-eclipses.png)

| Type | What is Observed |
|------|-----------------|
| **Total solar eclipse** | The Moon completely covers the Sun's disc. The sky darkens dramatically for a few minutes. Visible from a narrow strip on Earth. |
| **Partial solar eclipse** | The Moon covers only a portion of the Sun. Some sunlight still reaches the Earth. Visible from a wider area. |

During a total solar eclipse, a stunning **"diamond ring"** effect appears as sunlight peeks past the Moon's edge just before or after totality.

**Critical safety warning:** **Never look directly at the Sun during a solar eclipse** -- not with bare eyes, sunglasses, binoculars, or an unfiltered telescope. The Sun's intense radiation can cause **permanent blindness**. To observe safely, visit organised viewing events at planetaria or astronomy clubs where certified solar filters are provided.

> **Key idea:** A **solar eclipse** happens when the Moon comes between the Sun and the Earth, blocking sunlight. Never view a solar eclipse with unprotected eyes.

### Lunar Eclipse

![Eclipses — lunar eclipse (Earth between Sun and Moon)](eclipses-lunar-eclipses.png)

A **lunar eclipse** occurs when the **Earth positions itself between the Sun and the Moon**, preventing sunlight from reaching the Moon. The Moon enters the Earth's shadow.

**Types of lunar eclipse:**

![Eclipses — types of lunar eclipses (total and partial)](eclipses-types-of-lunar-eclipses.png)

| Type | What is Observed |
|------|-----------------|
| **Total lunar eclipse** | The Moon passes entirely into the Earth's shadow. It glows with a deep **reddish-copper** colour. |
| **Partial lunar eclipse** | Only a portion of the Moon enters the shadow. Part remains normally bright. |

During a total lunar eclipse, the Moon does not vanish completely. Earth's atmosphere bends some sunlight around the planet's edge, and the reddish wavelengths reach the Moon, giving it an eerie copper glow. This is sometimes called a **"blood moon"**.

Unlike solar eclipses, lunar eclipses are perfectly **safe to watch** with your bare eyes. They are also visible from a much larger portion of the Earth.

> **Key idea:** A **lunar eclipse** occurs when the Earth comes between the Sun and the Moon, casting its shadow on the Moon. During totality, the Moon turns a deep reddish hue. It can be safely observed without any protection.

### India's Ancient Eclipse Knowledge

Eclipses have been observed and recorded in India since antiquity. They are called **grahan** in Sanskrit. Ancient texts like the **Surya Siddhanta** contain mathematical methods to predict when and where eclipses would occur. Indian astronomers developed these calculations centuries before telescopes were invented, using careful observations and sophisticated mathematics. Today, scientists and astronomy enthusiasts worldwide travel to the best viewing locations to study eclipses, which offer rare opportunities to examine phenomena not visible at other times.

## Summary

- The Earth **rotates** on its axis from west to east, completing one spin in about **24 hours**. This rotation causes **day and night** and the apparent east-to-west motion of the Sun and stars.
- The **Pole Star (Dhruva Tara)** appears nearly motionless because the Earth's axis points towards it.
- Aryabhata accurately calculated the Earth's rotation period over 1500 years ago.
- The Earth **revolves** around the Sun in about **365 days and 6 hours**, causing the night sky to change through the year and contributing to seasons.
- **Seasons** are caused by the **tilt of Earth's axis** (23.5 degrees), not by the Earth's distance from the Sun. The hemisphere tilted toward the Sun gets summer; the one tilted away gets winter.
- The **summer solstice** (around 21 June) is the longest day in the Northern Hemisphere; the **winter solstice** (around 22 December) is the shortest.
- A **solar eclipse** occurs when the Moon blocks sunlight from reaching the Earth. **Never look directly** at the Sun during an eclipse.
- A **lunar eclipse** occurs when the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. It is safe to view with the naked eye. The Moon may appear reddish during totality.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Aryabhata, working without a telescope**: In 499 CE, the mathematician Aryabhata wrote that Earth rotates on its own axis, and that this rotation — not the Sun's movement — is what makes the heavens appear to swing east-to-west. He calculated Earth's rotation period as 23 hours, 56 minutes, 4.1 seconds — a number modern atomic clocks have confirmed almost exactly. All with naked-eye observations and brilliant maths, over a thousand years before telescopes. India's first satellite in 1975 was named after him as a tribute.

**Chandrayaan-3 lands on the Moon's south pole**: On 23 August 2023, ISRO's Vikram lander touched down softly near the Moon's south pole — making India the fourth country ever to soft-land on the Moon, and the first to reach its south pole. Every trajectory calculation depended on the same Earth-Moon-Sun geometry you just studied, only with millimetre precision. The launch cost less than many Hollywood films. Same physics, different precision tools.

**Eclipses, observed from India for thousands of years**: Solar and lunar eclipses are called grahan in Sanskrit. Ancient texts like the Surya Siddhanta contained formulas to predict eclipses years in advance — British officials in the 1700s were stunned that Indian pandits forecasted eclipse times more accurately than imported instruments. Today, daadi still tells you to stay indoors during a solar grahan. The science is the same: a Moon's shadow sweeping across India, just for a few minutes.

### Try it at home -- The Shadow Calendar

*⏱ 5 min a day for 7 days · 🧹 zero mess · 👨‍👩‍👧 no adult help needed*

**What you'll need:**

- A stick about 30-50 cm long (a broom handle, a pencil pushed into the ground, or a wooden ruler)
- A clear flat open space outdoors (a roof, courtyard, or patch of garden that gets sunlight all day)
- Chalk or small stones to mark positions on the ground
- A wristwatch or phone for time
- Your wonder notebook
- One week of cooperative weather

**Setup:**

1. Push the stick firmly into the ground (or stand it in a flowerpot) so that it casts a clear shadow on a flat surface.
2. Choose a fixed time each day for your observations -- **noon (12 PM) is best** because the Sun is highest then.

**Steps -- track the shadow for 7 days:**

1. At your chosen time each day, mark the **tip of the shadow** with a small stone or a chalk dot.
2. Also note the **length of the shadow** in your notebook.
3. Note the **direction** the shadow points.
4. Repeat for 7 consecutive days at exactly the same time.

**Bonus observation -- one full day:**

Pick one bright sunny day and mark the shadow tip every hour from 8 AM to 5 PM. By evening you will have a curve of dots tracing the Sun's apparent journey.

**What to look for:**

- **In one day**: The shadow moves from **west to east** across the ground while pointing in opposite-of-Sun directions. The shadow is **shortest at noon** (when the Sun is highest) and **longer in morning and evening**.
- **Across one week**: At the same noon time each day, the shadow position changes only slightly -- a small drift, not a sudden jump. This is the Earth's slow revolution around the Sun showing up over days.
- **The shadow direction at noon** points roughly north (in India, since we are north of the equator) -- a free compass made of sunlight.

**Try this**: If you keep this experiment up for a whole year (just one mark each Sunday at noon), the dots will trace a curved figure-of-eight pattern called an **analemma**. The shape is direct evidence that the Earth is both rotating and revolving on a tilted axis. Scientists call this one of the most elegant proofs of orbital mechanics you can make with no equipment at all.

**Show what you found:** Photograph your row of chalk marks at the end of the week. Sketch the shadow's path in your wonder notebook. Show your dadu or daadi the marks and ask them which direction the shadow points at noon — they will probably guess north before you tell them why. Explain that you have built, in your own courtyard, the world's simplest sundial — the same kind the astronomers at Jantar Mantar in Jaipur were using 300 years ago.

**A question to keep asking all year:** *If everything around me — sunrise, sunset, the changing seasons, the Moon's phases — is actually a clue about how Earth is spinning and moving, what other "obvious" daily things are secretly telling me about the cosmos?*
