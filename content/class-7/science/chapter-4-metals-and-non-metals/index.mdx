## Introduction

Step into any Indian kitchen and observe the objects around you. The steel pressure cooker, the aluminium kadhai, the iron tawa, the copper-bottomed vessel -- these are all made from metals. Now look at the coal in the angithi, the wooden rolling pin, and the rubber grip on a ladle -- these are non-metals.

Why do we choose specific materials for specific jobs? Why is the railway track made of iron and not rubber? Why is the wire inside your earphones copper and not wood? The answer lies in the unique properties of **metals** and **non-metals**.

In this chapter, you will investigate these properties, understand how metals and non-metals behave with air and water, and appreciate why both categories are indispensable in our lives.

> **Key idea:** All materials around us can be broadly sorted into **metals** and **non-metals** based on how they look, feel, and behave in different conditions.

---

## What Makes Metals Special?

### Lustre -- The Signature Shine

![What makes metals special — lustre, the signature shine](what-makes-metals-special-lustre-the-signature-shine.png)

Metals possess a characteristic shine called **metallic lustre**. A freshly polished brass lamp, a new stainless steel thali, or a gold chain all display this gleaming quality.

Non-metals like charcoal, sulfur powder, and wood lack this shine -- they appear **dull** or **non-lustrous**.

### Malleability -- Hammered Without Breaking

![What makes metals special — malleability, hammered without breaking](what-makes-metals-special-malleability-hammered-without-breaking.png)

Place an iron nail on a hard surface and strike it firmly with a hammer. It flattens out but does not shatter. This ability of metals to be **hammered or pressed into thin sheets** without cracking is called **malleability**.

- **Gold** and **silver** top the list of malleable metals.
- The thin aluminium foil you use to wrap rotis is a perfect example of malleability.
- The delicate silver leaf (chandi ka vark) placed on mithai during festivals demonstrates extreme malleability.

Non-metals like charcoal and sulfur are **brittle** -- strike them with a hammer and they crumble into fragments.

> **Key idea:** **Malleability** is the ability to be beaten into thin sheets. Metals are malleable; non-metals are brittle and break apart under force.

### Ductility -- Stretched into Wires

![What makes metals special — ductility, stretched into wires](what-makes-metals-special-ductility-stretched-into-wires.png)

Observe the copper wiring in your home or the thin metal strings on a sitar. These wires exist because metals can be **pulled or drawn into long, thin wires**. This property is called **ductility**.

- A single gram of **gold** is so ductile that it can be stretched into a wire spanning 2 kilometres!
- Copper, aluminium, and iron are also highly ductile metals.

Non-metals cannot be drawn into wires -- they snap under tension.

### Sonority -- The Ringing Quality

![What makes metals special — sonority, the ringing quality](what-makes-metals-special-sonority-the-ringing-quality.png)

Tap a stainless steel plate with a spoon. Now tap a wooden chopping board. The difference is unmistakable.

Metals produce a clear **ringing sound** when struck. This musical quality is called **sonority**. Metals are said to be **sonorous**.

This property explains why:
- Temple bells and church bells are cast from metal
- Ghungroos worn by classical dancers are tiny metal bells
- Manjiras and jhanjh (hand cymbals) used in bhajan-kirtan are made from brass or bronze

### Heat Conduction -- Passing Warmth Along

![What makes metals special — heat conduction, passing warmth along](what-makes-metals-special-heat-conduction-passing-warmth-along.png)

**Try this at home:** Place a stainless steel spoon and a wooden ladle in a cup of hot chai. After two minutes, carefully touch the top end of each. The steel spoon will feel noticeably warm; the wooden ladle will feel the same as before.

Metals are **excellent conductors of heat** -- they transfer thermal energy rapidly from one end to the other. Non-metals like wood and plastic are **poor heat conductors**.

This is why:
- **Tawas and kadhais** are made of metal (to transfer flame heat to food efficiently)
- **Handles** on these vessels are made of wood, plastic, or bakelite (to protect your hands from burns)

### Electrical Conductivity

As you learned in the previous chapter, metals allow electric current to flow through them easily, making them **good electrical conductors**. Non-metals block current -- they are **insulators**.

| Material tested | Does current flow? | Metal or Non-metal? |
|----------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Copper strip | Yes | Metal (conductor) |
| Iron bolt | Yes | Metal (conductor) |
| Aluminium can | Yes | Metal (conductor) |
| Lump of sulfur | No | Non-metal (insulator) |
| Piece of charcoal | No | Non-metal (insulator) |
| Wooden block | No | Non-metal (insulator) |
| Rubber sole | No | Non-metal (insulator) |

This is why electricians' tools have **plastic or rubber handles** and why electricians wear **rubber-soled chappals** -- these insulating materials shield them from electric shock.

> **Key idea:** Metals are typically hard, shiny, malleable, ductile, sonorous, and excellent conductors of both heat and electricity. Non-metals generally lack all these properties.

## When Metals Break the Rules

Not every metal fits the typical mould:

- **Sodium** and **potassium** are metals so soft that a kitchen knife can slice through them easily.
- **Mercury** is unique -- it is the only metal that remains **liquid** at room temperature. Older thermometers contained mercury inside their glass tubes.
- **Wood** is an interesting case in non-metals -- it neither flattens like a malleable metal nor shatters like a brittle non-metal when struck.

## What Happens When Metals Meet Air and Water?

### The Problem of Rusting

![What happens when metals meet air and water — the problem of rusting](what-happens-when-metals-meet-air-and-water-the-problem-of-rusting.png)

Have you noticed the reddish-brown patches on old iron railings, discarded tin cans, or the iron nails in a damp wall? This coating is called **rust**, and the process of its formation is called **rusting**.

![Experiment setup showing three glass bottles with iron nails in different conditions: dry air only, water only, and air plus water](what-happens-when-metals-meet-air-and-water-an-experiment-with-three-sealed-bottles-reveals-the-truth.png)

**An experiment with three sealed bottles reveals the truth:**

| Bottle | What is inside | What happens to the iron nail |
|--------|---------------|------------------------------|
| X | Iron nail + dry air only (moisture absorbed by silica gel) | No rust appears |
| Y | Iron nail + boiled water covered with oil (air blocked) | No rust appears |
| Z | Iron nail + ordinary water with air access | Rust forms on the nail |

**The verdict:** Rusting requires the presence of **both air (oxygen) and moisture (water)**. Dry air alone is harmless. Water without air is also harmless. Only when iron is exposed to **moist air** does rust form.

> **Key idea:** Iron rusts only when both **oxygen** and **water vapour** are present together. Removing either one prevents rust.

### Corrosion Beyond Iron

![Corrosion beyond iron — greenish layer on copper](an-experiment-with-three-sealed-bottles-reveals-the-truth-corrosion-beyond-iron-copper.png)

![Corrosion beyond iron — dark black tarnish on silver](an-experiment-with-three-sealed-bottles-reveals-the-truth-corrosion-beyond-iron-silver.png)

Rusting is just one form of a broader phenomenon:

- **Copper** vessels and statues gradually develop a **greenish layer**
- **Silver** jewellery and utensils slowly acquire a **dark black tarnish**

This gradual deterioration of metal surfaces due to reactions with air, moisture, or other chemicals is called **corrosion**.

### Keeping Rust at Bay

Rust causes massive financial losses across India each year -- bridges weaken, agricultural tools decay, and vehicles deteriorate. Prevention methods include:

- **Painting** -- creates a barrier between iron and moist air
- **Applying oil or grease** -- coats the surface and repels water
- **Galvanisation** -- depositing a thin protective layer of **zinc** onto iron, which prevents direct contact with air and water

### A Marvel of Ancient Metallurgy

The **Iron Pillar** standing near Qutub Minar in Delhi was crafted over **1600 years ago** during the reign of Chandragupta II. Standing roughly 8 metres tall and weighing more than 6000 kilograms, this pillar has withstood centuries of monsoon rains, scorching summers, and cold winters with almost **no rust**. It stands as proof of the extraordinary metallurgical knowledge possessed by ancient Indian craftspeople.

## How Metals Respond to Oxygen

When metals combine with oxygen in the air, they produce **metal oxides**. These oxides are **basic** in nature.

**Demonstration -- Igniting Magnesium:**

![How metals respond to oxygen — demonstration of igniting magnesium](how-metals-respond-to-oxygen-demonstration-igniting-magnesium.png)

1. Clean a strip of magnesium ribbon with sandpaper to remove any oxidation layer.
2. Hold it with metal tongs and light one end using a spirit lamp.
3. The ribbon catches fire and burns with a **brilliant, blinding white flame**.
4. A white powdery residue remains -- this is **magnesium oxide**.
5. Dissolve this powder in water and test with litmus paper -- **red litmus turns blue**.
6. This confirms that magnesium oxide is **basic**.

> **Key idea:** Metals burning in oxygen produce **metal oxides** that are **basic**. This is a fundamental difference between metals and non-metals.

**Special storage:** Sodium reacts so violently with both air and water that it must be stored immersed in **kerosene oil** to keep it safely away from oxygen and moisture.

## How Non-Metals Respond to Oxygen and Water

### Burning Sulfur

![How non-metals respond to oxygen and water — burning sulfur](how-non-metals-respond-to-oxygen-and-water-burning-sulfur.png)

When sulfur (a yellow non-metal) is ignited in air:
1. It burns with a **pale blue flame** and releases **sulfur dioxide gas**, which has a sharp, choking smell.
2. When sulfur dioxide dissolves in water, it forms **sulfurous acid**.
3. Testing with litmus paper: **blue litmus turns red** -- the solution is **acidic**.

> **Key idea:** Non-metals burning in oxygen produce **non-metal oxides** that are **acidic**. This is the opposite of what metals produce.

### Non-Metals and Water

If you drop sulfur powder into a beaker of water, nothing happens. Generally, non-metals **do not react with water**.

**Special storage:** Phosphorus is so reactive with air that it ignites spontaneously. To prevent this, phosphorus is stored **under water**.

## Metals vs. Non-Metals: A Side-by-Side View

| Property | Metals | Non-metals |
|----------|--------|------------|
| Appearance | Lustrous (shiny) | Dull (non-lustrous) |
| Malleability | Can be beaten into sheets | Brittle -- break under force |
| Ductility | Can be drawn into wires | Cannot be drawn into wires |
| Sound when struck | Sonorous (ringing) | Dull thud |
| Heat conduction | Excellent conductors | Poor conductors |
| Electrical conduction | Good conductors | Poor conductors (insulators) |
| General hardness | Usually hard | Often soft or powdery |
| Physical state | Almost all solid (mercury is an exception) | Can be solid, liquid, or gas |
| Nature of oxides | Basic | Acidic |
| Reaction with water | Some metals react | Mostly do not react |

## Why Non-Metals Are Irreplaceable

Despite lacking the strength and shine of metals, non-metals are absolutely vital for existence:

- **Oxygen** -- Every breath you take depends on this non-metal. It also supports combustion.
- **Carbon** -- The backbone of every living molecule. Your muscles, blood, hair, and even DNA contain carbon.
- **Nitrogen** -- The key ingredient in fertilisers like urea that nourish India's farmlands.
- **Chlorine** -- Added to municipal water supplies across India to kill harmful bacteria and make water safe to drink.
- **Iodine** -- Applied as an antiseptic solution (tincture of iodine) on cuts and wounds to prevent infection.

> **Key idea:** Metals give us strength and structure, but non-metals like oxygen, carbon, and nitrogen make life itself possible. Both are equally essential.

---

## Elements, Metals, and Everyday Materials

An important distinction: **metals** and **non-metals** are categories of **elements** -- pure substances that cannot be broken down into anything simpler by chemical means.

Materials like **plastic, glass, rubber, wood, and paper** are not elements. They are manufactured from combinations of multiple elements and therefore cannot be labelled as metals or non-metals.

As of today, **118 elements** have been identified. Some exist naturally in the earth's crust, oceans, and atmosphere, while others have been created artificially in advanced laboratories.

---

## Summary

- **Metals** are generally lustrous, malleable, ductile, sonorous, and excellent conductors of heat and electricity.
- **Non-metals** are typically dull, brittle, not ductile, not sonorous, and poor conductors.
- Metal oxides are **basic**; non-metal oxides are **acidic**.
- **Rusting** of iron occurs only when iron contacts both air and moisture simultaneously. It can be prevented through painting, oiling, greasing, or galvanisation.
- The general degradation of metal surfaces by environmental factors is called **corrosion**.
- Non-metals like oxygen, carbon, nitrogen, chlorine, and iodine play roles that are critical for sustaining life.
- Metals and non-metals are types of elements; composite materials like plastic and glass are not classified as either.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Delhi's Iron Pillar still puzzles scientists**: Materials engineers at **IIT Kanpur** have spent decades studying why the **1600-year-old Iron Pillar** near Qutub Minar has barely rusted. They found that ancient Indian metallurgists had unknowingly created a thin protective layer of iron hydrogen phosphate on its surface, blocking moist air from the iron below. Modern stainless steel does something similar using chromium. Walk past Mehrauli and you walk past one of metallurgy's greatest mysteries.

**Copper in every metro line**: The **Delhi Metro**, **Mumbai Metro**, and **Chennai Metro** together use thousands of kilometres of copper wire to feed power to the trains. Copper was picked because it conducts brilliantly, can be drawn into long thin wires (ductile), and bends through tunnel layouts without cracking (malleable). The overhead lines use aluminium instead — much lighter than copper. Every metro ride sits on a trade-off engineers think about constantly.

**Galvanised roofs in coastal Kerala**: Houses near Kochi and Mangaluru face salty sea air that eats ordinary iron in months. Builders use **galvanised iron sheets** — iron coated with a thin layer of zinc. The zinc reacts with air and moisture instead of the iron, sacrificing itself to protect the metal below. Your own school's classroom roof may be galvanised for exactly the same reason.

### Try it at home -- The Rust Race

*⏱ 4 days (10 min setup, 5 min daily check) · 🧹 low mess · 👨‍👩‍👧 no adult help needed (but boiling water needs adult supervision)*

**What you'll need:**

- 4 iron nails (any size -- the long ones used in carpentry work well)
- 4 small glass jars or transparent plastic containers with lids
- Water, cooking oil, salt, kitchen tissue paper
- A pen and your wonder notebook

**Setup -- 4 conditions, 4 days:**

1. **Jar A (dry air)**: Place a nail and a small piece of dry tissue. Seal the jar tightly.
2. **Jar B (water only, no air)**: Fill the jar to the brim with **boiled water that has cooled** (boiling drives out dissolved air). Drop the nail in. Add a thin layer of cooking oil on top of the water (the oil seals out fresh air). Seal.
3. **Jar C (water + air)**: Half-fill the jar with ordinary tap water. Drop the nail in. Leave open.
4. **Jar D (salt water + air)**: Half-fill with water, dissolve a teaspoon of salt, drop the nail in. Leave open.

**Steps:**

1. Set up all four jars on the same day. Label them clearly.
2. Each day for 4 days, observe each nail and write down what you see.
3. On day 4, line up all four nails side by side and compare.

**What to look for:**

- **Jar A** (dry air only) -- the nail should look almost the same as day 1.
- **Jar B** (water without air) -- still mostly clean. This proves water alone does not cause rust.
- **Jar C** (water + air) -- a reddish-brown coating starts forming. **Both** ingredients matter.
- **Jar D** (salt water + air) -- the most dramatic rusting. This is exactly why iron things rust faster near the sea.

**Show what you found:** Photograph all four nails side by side on day 4. Sketch the line-up in your notebook with labels. Show your daadi (or dadu, mummy, or older cousin) and tell them one new thing you understood — for instance, why Jar D rusted the fastest, or why grease on Bauji's bicycle chain matters. Ask them whether they have ever lost an iron tool to rust at home.

> **A question to keep asking all year:** *Every time I spot something rusting, tarnishing, or staying perfectly shiny — what is that material made of, and which of its element properties is showing up right now?*
