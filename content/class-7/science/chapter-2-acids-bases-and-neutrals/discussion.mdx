## The Curious Case of the Colour-Changing Stain

**Meera:** Arjun, yaar, the weirdest thing happened in our kitchen. I spilled haldi on my white dupatta, tried to wash it off with soap, and the stain turned **reddish-brown**. Worse than before!

**Arjun:** Reddish-brown? You sure you didn't just rub it deeper?

**Meera:** No, I literally watched it change colour the moment the soap touched it. Yellow one second, brown the next.

**Arjun:** Hmm, that's actually strange. Soap shouldn't change a colour like that. Unless… the soap is doing something chemical to the haldi?

**Meera:** That's what I thought too. So I asked my daadi and she said haldi is a *natural indicator*. Stays yellow normally, but turns reddish-brown when it touches anything basic. Soap is basic.

**Arjun:** So basically your dupatta accidentally did a chemistry test on the soap.

**Meera:** Exactly! But here's the catch — haldi only reacts with bases. If something is acidic, or neutral, haldi just stays yellow. So it can't tell you everything.

**Arjun:** So you need another indicator to be sure. Like litmus?

**Meera:** Right. Blue litmus turns red in acid, red litmus turns blue in base. Use both haldi and litmus together and you can identify anything.

## Sour, Slippery, or Neither

**Meera:** Arjun, are all sour things acidic? Like imli, lemon, kachcha aam?

**Arjun:** I think so. Imli ki chutney is sour because of tartaric acid. Aam panna gets its tang from citric acid in raw mango. Even dahi — that mild sourness is lactic acid forming as the milk sets.

**Meera:** So sourness is basically nature's "acid alert"?

**Arjun:** Pretty much. What I don't get is — how do bases feel? You can't really taste them safely.

**Meera:** Daadi said basic things feel **slippery** or soapy. Like how soap squishes between your fingers. Some are bitter, like baking soda. But she warned me — never taste a random substance. Strong acids and bases can burn your mouth.

**Arjun:** Good rule. So acids = sour, bases = slippery and bitter. What about the boring ones in between?

**Meera:** Neutrals are the *calm* ones. Plain water, salt water, honey in water — neither sour nor slippery, and they don't change litmus paper at all.

**Arjun:** Sour, slippery, or nothing. That's actually a useful way to sort things.

## Making Indicators from Kitchen Ingredients

**Meera:** Arjun, our chemistry kit at school has fancy bottles. But can we make indicators at home, without buying anything?

**Arjun:** I think yes. My cousin once mashed red gudhal flowers with warm water, strained it, and used it as an indicator. Red juice. Dip it in nimbu paani — stays red. Dip it in soap water — turns greenish.

**Meera:** Whoa. What other things from the kitchen would work?

**Arjun:** Beetroot juice. Jamun juice. Basically anything strongly coloured by plant pigments. They all react to acids and bases.

**Meera:** What about haldi strips? Our teacher mentioned those.

**Arjun:** Easy. Mix haldi powder with a little water into a paste, soak paper strips in it, let them dry. Now dip one in soap water — reddish-brown. Dip one in nimbu paani — stays yellow. Homemade testing kit.

**Meera:** That's so cool. One more thing — are there indicators that work through *smell* instead of colour? For people who can't see colours well?

**Arjun:** Yes! Onion paste. Or vanilla essence. You soak cloth in them, and the smell changes when an acid or base is added. They're called *olfactory* indicators.

**Meera:** Indicators by smell. The kitchen really is a full chemistry lab.

## When Acids and Bases Come Together

**Meera:** Arjun, what happens if you mix an acid and a base? Do they fight?

**Arjun:** Opposite — they cancel each other out. Acid + base = salt + water + a bit of heat. It's called **neutralisation**.

**Meera:** Like the extreme qualities just… disappear?

**Arjun:** Exactly. And it happens around us constantly. Bee sting? The bee injects formic acid. Putting baking soda paste — which is basic — neutralises it and stops the burning.

**Meera:** Oh! That's why people apply that white paste.

**Arjun:** Same with soil. Farmers in some districts have very acidic soil. They add **chuna** (lime) — a base — so bajra and jowar can actually grow. And factories treat acidic waste with bases before letting it out, otherwise rivers would die.

**Meera:** And antacids work the same way, na? When my dadu eats too much spicy food and gets acidity, he takes Eno.

**Arjun:** Eno is literally a base that neutralises the extra acid in his stomach. The fizz is carbon dioxide. The chemistry is the same whether it's a bee sting, a wheat field, or your dadu's tummy.

**Meera:** Yaar, neutralisation is doing so much work without anyone noticing.

## The Secret Message Trick

**Meera:** Arjun, at the school science exhibition, a senior wrote an *invisible* message on white paper, sprayed something, and the words appeared in reddish-brown. Everyone was amazed.

**Arjun:** I think I know that one. Did the spray smell yellow-ish, kind of earthy?

**Meera:** Yes — like haldi water!

**Arjun:** Then here's the trick. She wrote the message using **soap solution**, which is basic and dries clear. The spray was diluted turmeric water. Wherever soap had been, the haldi turned reddish-brown. Where it hadn't, the paper stayed pale yellow.

**Meera:** So the "magic" was just an indicator reacting with a base.

**Arjun:** Exactly. We could do this at home easily. Use a thin brush dipped in baking soda solution to write a secret message. Let it dry. Spray haldi water. Message appears.

**Meera:** I'm going to use this for my project! But this time, I'll wear an old dupatta. I am not staining another white one.

**Arjun:** Smart. Curious enough to try, careful enough to plan ahead. That's the real scientist mindset.
