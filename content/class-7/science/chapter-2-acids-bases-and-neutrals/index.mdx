## Introduction

![Introduction — classifying everyday substances as acidic, basic, or neutral](introduction.png)

Picture this: Meera is helping her mother make paneer at home. Her mother squeezes lemon juice into warm milk, and within moments, the milk begins to curdle -- the solid paneer separates from the liquid whey. "Why does lemon juice do this?" Meera wonders. "Would water do the same thing?"

The answer lies in the nature of the substances involved. Lemon juice contains an **acid**, which reacts with proteins in milk and causes them to clump together. Water, on the other hand, is **neutral** and would not have the same effect.

In this chapter, you will discover how to classify substances as **acidic**, **basic**, or **neutral** and learn to identify them using clever tools called **indicators**.

> **Key idea:** The substances we encounter daily can be grouped into three categories based on their chemical nature -- **acidic**, **basic**, or **neutral**. Special substances called **indicators** help us identify which category a substance belongs to.

## Discovering Indicators

### What is Litmus?

**Litmus** is a natural dye extracted from tiny organisms called **lichens** (a partnership between a fungus and an alga that grow together). Litmus is available as a liquid solution or as small paper strips. The paper strips come in two varieties -- **blue** and **red**.

Litmus is known as an **acid-base indicator** because it displays different colours depending on whether it is placed in an acidic or basic solution.

![Litmus paper test showing blue litmus turning red in acid and red litmus turning blue in base](discovering-indicators-what-is-litmus.png)

**How litmus responds:**

![How litmus responds — acid turns blue litmus red; base turns red litmus blue](discovering-indicators-how-litmus-responds.png)
- **Acidic substances** cause **blue litmus paper to turn red**
- **Basic substances** cause **red litmus paper to turn blue**
- **Neutral substances** leave both litmus papers **unchanged**

> **Key idea:** An indicator is a substance whose colour shifts depending on whether it contacts an acid or a base. Litmus is the most widely used acid-base indicator.

- **Group C (Neutral):** Plain water, honey solution, salt solution

## Acidic Substances

Substances with a **sour taste** usually contain acids and are classified as **acidic**.

**Safety warning:** Never taste any unknown substance! Only taste items that you are certain are safe for consumption.

Some common edible acidic substances and the acids present in them:

- **Lemon and orange** -- Citric acid
- **Amla (Indian gooseberry)** -- Ascorbic acid (Vitamin C)
- **Imli (tamarind)** -- Tartaric acid
- **Vinegar** -- Acetic acid
- **Dahi (curd)** -- Lactic acid
- **Raw mango** -- Citric acid and Oxalic acid

> **Key idea:** Acidic substances have a sour taste and cause blue litmus paper to change to red.

---

## Basic Substances

**Basic** substances typically feel **slippery or soapy** when touched. Many of them have a **bitter taste**, though not everything bitter is basic. For instance, neem leaves taste bitter but are not strongly basic.

Common basic substances include:
- Soap and detergent solutions
- Baking soda dissolved in water
- Chuna (lime) dissolved in water
- Toothpaste

> **Key idea:** Basic substances feel slippery, may taste bitter, and cause red litmus paper to change to blue.

---

## Neutral Substances

Substances that show neither acidic nor basic behaviour are called **neutral**. They leave both blue and red litmus papers unchanged.

Examples: Drinking water, salt dissolved in water, honey dissolved in water.

## Nature's Own Indicators

When litmus paper is unavailable, nature provides excellent alternatives. Many flowers, vegetables, and spices can serve as acid-base indicators!

### Red Hibiscus (Gudhal) as an Indicator

![Red hibiscus (Gudhal) as a natural indicator — staying red in acid, turning green in base](natures-own-indicators-red-hibiscus-gudhal-as-an-indicator.png)

You can prepare an indicator from red hibiscus flowers:

1. Collect a few fallen red hibiscus (gudhal) petals -- do not pluck from the plant.
2. Wash the petals gently and grind them using a mortar and pestle.
3. Place the crushed petals in a glass and pour warm water over them.
4. Cover and wait for about 10 minutes until the water absorbs the colour.
5. Strain the liquid. This filtrate is your **hibiscus indicator**.

**How hibiscus indicator behaves:**
- In **acidic solutions**, the extract stays **red or turns deeper red**
- In **basic solutions**, the extract turns **green or bluish-green**
- In **neutral solutions**, the colour stays the **same**

### Turmeric (Haldi) as an Indicator

Turmeric is a spice found in virtually every Indian kitchen. You can create **turmeric indicator strips** easily:

1. Mix a spoonful of haldi powder with a small quantity of water to form a thick paste.
2. Soak strips of blotting paper or filter paper in this paste until they turn bright yellow.
3. Remove the strips and let them dry completely.
4. Cut them into small pieces -- your **turmeric indicator strips** are ready.

![Turmeric paper strips showing yellow in acidic and neutral solutions and red in basic solutions](natures-own-indicators-turmeric-haldi-as-an-indicator.png)

**How turmeric strips respond:**
- In **basic solutions**, the yellow strip turns **reddish-brown**
- In **acidic solutions**, the strip **stays yellow** (no change)
- In **neutral solutions**, the strip **stays yellow** (no change)

> **Key idea:** Turmeric is useful for detecting basic substances. However, it cannot distinguish between acidic and neutral substances since it remains yellow in both cases.

This explains a common kitchen observation: a haldi stain on your white kurta turns reddish when you scrub it with soap, because soap is basic!

### More Natural Indicators

You can experiment with indicators made from:
- **Beetroot juice**
- **Red cabbage extract**
- **Jamun (Indian blackberry) juice**
- **Red rose petal extract**

### Olfactory Indicators

Certain substances reveal the presence of acids or bases through a change in **smell** rather than colour. These are called **olfactory indicators**.

For example, if you soak small pieces of cloth in crushed garlic or onion paste overnight, the fabric absorbs the strong smell. When drops of a basic solution are added to these strips, the smell may diminish or change. This technique can help identify substances through scent.

## Neutralisation: Acids and Bases Cancel Each Other

### What Occurs When an Acid Meets a Base?

When an acidic substance and a basic substance are combined in suitable proportions, they **counteract** each other. The mixture that results is neither acidic nor basic -- it becomes **neutral**.

This process is known as a **neutralisation reaction**.

**The neutralisation equation:**

![Diagram showing a neutralisation reaction: acid plus base producing salt, water, and heat](neutralisation-acids-and-bases-cancel-each-other-what-occurs-when-an-acid-meets-a-base.png)

**Acid + Base --> Salt + Water + Heat**

For instance, if you add a few drops of vinegar (acidic) to a solution of baking soda (basic), the mixture fizzes. After the reaction is complete, the resulting solution tests neutral with litmus paper. A new substance -- a salt -- has been formed, along with water and some warmth.

> **Key idea:** During neutralisation, an acid and a base react together to produce **salt** and **water**, and **heat** is released. The product is neutral.

## Neutralisation in Everyday Situations

### Bee Stings and Wasp Stings

![Neutralisation in everyday situations — bee stings and wasp stings](neutralisation-in-everyday-situations-bee-stings-and-wasp-stings.png)

A **bee sting** releases an acidic substance into the skin, causing pain and swelling. Applying a paste of **baking soda** (which is basic) helps neutralise the acid and reduces discomfort.

Interestingly, a **wasp sting** is basic in nature, so applying **vinegar** (acidic) can provide relief -- the opposite treatment!

### Correcting Soil for Farming

![Neutralisation in everyday situations — correcting soil for farming](neutralisation-in-everyday-situations-correcting-soil-for-farming.png)

Overuse of certain fertilisers can make farmland **too acidic**, stunting crop growth. Farmers correct this by mixing **powdered lime** (a basic substance) into the soil, which neutralises excess acidity.

Conversely, if the soil becomes **excessively basic**, adding **compost** or **decomposed leaf matter** helps, as these materials slowly release mild acids that restore balance.

### Treating Factory Waste

![Neutralisation in everyday situations — treating factory waste before release](neutralisation-in-everyday-situations-treating-factory-waste.png)

Industrial discharge into ponds and rivers can be strongly acidic, endangering fish and aquatic plants. Factories are required to **neutralise** this waste by adding appropriate basic substances before releasing it, safeguarding water ecosystems.

> **Key idea:** Neutralisation has wide-ranging practical uses -- from soothing insect stings to improving soil for agriculture and protecting rivers from harmful industrial waste.

## Meera's Kitchen Mystery Solved

Now you can explain the paneer-making process from the opening of this chapter!

- Lemon juice is **acidic** (it contains citric acid).
- When added to warm milk, the acid reacts with milk proteins and causes them to **coagulate** (clump together), forming solid paneer.
- The leftover liquid (whey) is mildly acidic.

You can test this yourself: dip blue litmus paper in whey, and it will turn red, confirming that whey is acidic!

## Summary

- Substances can be classified as **acidic**, **basic**, or **neutral** based on their chemical nature.
- **Indicators** such as litmus, hibiscus extract, and turmeric change colour in acidic or basic solutions.
- **Acids** turn blue litmus red. **Bases** turn red litmus blue.
- Hibiscus extract stays **red in acids** and turns **green in bases**.
- Turmeric strips turn **reddish-brown in bases** but remain yellow in acids and neutral substances.
- Mixing an acid and a base in the right proportions produces a **neutralisation reaction**, yielding **salt**, **water**, and **heat**.
- Neutralisation is used in everyday life: treating insect stings, balancing soil for agriculture, and managing industrial waste.

### Where you see this in real life

**Eno in the kitchen.** When dadu eats too much spicy food and his chest burns, he reaches for Eno. The fizz isn't just bubbles — it's a base meeting the extra acid in his stomach. Same neutralisation you read about, happening in 30 seconds at your kitchen sink. Antacids are quietly one of the most-used chemistry products in every Indian household.

**The Punjab farmer's soil test.** Farmers send small soil samples to the local Krishi Vigyan Kendra to learn whether their fields are acidic or basic. Too acidic? Add **lime**. Too basic? Add **gypsum**. Wheat and rice only grow in a narrow neutral range. Without these chemistry checks, India's food supply would collapse.

**Your municipal water.** Engineers at the city water plant test pH all day long. Too acidic and pipes corrode. Too basic and soap won't lather. Tiny adjustments — invisible to you — keep the water reaching your tap in the neutral zone every single morning.

### Try it at home — Make Your Own pH Indicator

*⏱ 30 min · 🧹 mild mess (purple stains on hands and cloth) · 👨‍👩‍👧 adult help for warm water only*

**What you'll need:**

- A handful of **red cabbage leaves** (ask your mother to spare a few from a vegetable shop)
- A small pan and warm water
- A strainer
- A glass jar to store the liquid
- 6 small katoris or transparent glasses for testing
- Six everyday substances: **nimbu paani, baking soda solution, plain water, soap water, vinegar, sugar water**

**Steps:**

1. Tear the red cabbage leaves into small pieces and place in a pan.
2. Pour just enough warm water to cover them and let them sit for 15 minutes. The water will turn deep purple as the pigment seeps out.
3. Strain the liquid into the glass jar. **This purple liquid is your indicator.**
4. Pour a small amount of each test substance into a separate katori.
5. Add a few drops of your purple indicator to each one and stir gently.
6. Record the colour change for every katori.

**What to look for:**

- **Pink or red colour** = acidic (you should see this with nimbu paani and vinegar)
- **Purple or blue** = neutral (plain water and sugar water)
- **Green or yellow** = basic (baking soda solution, soap water)

**Why this works:** Red cabbage contains a pigment called anthocyanin — the same family of compounds that gives jamun, beetroot, and red grapes their colour. Anthocyanin actually changes its own chemical shape depending on whether it is surrounded by acid or base, and that shape change is what we see as colour change.

**Show what you found:** Snap a photo of your six katoris in a row, from acid to base. Sketch the colour chart in your notebook. Show it to your daadi or someone at home — explain what each colour means. Watch them poke fingers into the kitchen jars to guess which is acidic. That's chemistry becoming family conversation.

**A question to keep asking all year:** *Every time something around me changes colour, smells different, or fizzes — what acid or base might be hiding inside?*
