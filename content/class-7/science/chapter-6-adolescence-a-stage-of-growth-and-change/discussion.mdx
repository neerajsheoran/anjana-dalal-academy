## What Is Happening to Our Bodies?

**Child:** Maa, everyone in my class seems to be changing. Some friends have grown really tall, some have pimples, and some boys' voices sound different. What is going on?

**Mother:** You are all entering adolescence, beta -- the stage of life between childhood and adulthood. It usually starts around age 10 and continues until about 19. During these years, your body is preparing itself for adult life, and that preparation involves many visible and invisible changes.

**Child:** But why is Ananya already so tall while Rohan has not grown at all this year?

**Mother:** Because every person's body follows its own schedule. Some children begin puberty earlier, others later. Height, weight, voice, skin, hair growth -- all these changes happen at different speeds for different people. There is no single "correct" timeline. The variation itself is what is normal.

**Child:** That makes me feel better. I was worried because I have not grown as much as some of my friends.

**Mother:** There is no need to worry at all. Your growth spurt will come when your body is ready. All these changes are controlled by hormones -- chemical messengers produced inside your body. Your brain sends signals to release these hormones at the right time, and they tell different parts of your body when and how to grow.

## The Body's Transformation

**Child:** Maa, what exactly changes during adolescence?

**Mother:** Quite a lot! Both boys and girls grow taller and gain weight. Boys may notice their shoulders broadening, their voice deepening because the voice box grows larger -- sometimes you can see a small bump on the throat called the Adam's apple. Boys also develop facial hair over time. Girls experience breast development and widening of the hips. Both boys and girls grow hair in new areas like underarms.

**Child:** And pimples? Why do they suddenly appear?

**Mother:** During adolescence, your skin produces more oil than before. When this extra oil blocks the tiny pores in your skin, bacteria can grow and cause small infections -- those are pimples. Washing your face regularly, drinking plenty of water, eating fresh fruits and vegetables, and avoiding greasy snacks all help manage acne.

**Child:** These changes that make boys look different from girls -- do they have a special name?

**Mother:** Yes, they are called secondary sexual characteristics. Deeper voice and facial hair in boys, breast development and wider hips in girls -- these are visible signs of the body maturing. They mark the beginning of puberty, which is when the body starts developing the capacity for reproduction. All of this is entirely natural and nothing to feel awkward about.

## Feelings That Ride a Roller Coaster

**Child:** Maa, lately I feel really happy one moment, then suddenly upset or angry for no reason. Am I going crazy?

**Mother:** Not at all! This is completely normal during adolescence. The same hormones that are changing your body also affect your emotions. Mood swings, feeling things very intensely, being extra sensitive to what friends say about you -- these are all typical experiences.

**Child:** How do I deal with these feelings?

**Mother:** There are many good strategies. Physical activity is one of the best -- playing badminton, doing yoga, going for a run. Exercise releases chemicals in your brain that naturally lift your mood. Creative activities help too -- drawing, singing, writing in a diary. And whenever you feel overwhelmed, talking to someone you trust -- me, your father, a teacher, or a close friend -- always helps.

**Child:** Is it normal to feel more curious and excited about everything?

**Mother:** Absolutely, and it is a wonderful quality. This is the age when many great thinkers discovered their passions. Sachin Tendulkar picked up his cricket bat seriously during adolescence. Malala Yousafzai began speaking about education rights as a teenager. Your curiosity is a treasure -- channel it into learning, hobbies, and positive experiences.

## Eating Right and Staying Clean

**Child:** Maa, our teacher said we need to eat better during adolescence than at any other time. Why?

**Mother:** Because your body is growing at a pace it has not seen since you were a baby. You need calcium from milk and ragi for strong bones. Proteins from moong dal, eggs, and almonds for building muscles and repairing tissues. Iron from palak and dates for healthy blood -- this is especially important for girls to prevent anaemia. Vitamins from seasonal fruits for immunity. And carbohydrates from roti and rice for the energy to study and play.

**Child:** What about personal hygiene? Is that more important now too?

**Mother:** Much more. Your body produces more sweat and oil during adolescence, so daily bathing and clean clothes are essential. Keeping underarm and private areas clean prevents infections and body odour. For girls, menstrual hygiene is crucial -- using sanitary pads or menstrual cups, changing them regularly, and disposing of them properly by wrapping in newspaper and placing in a dustbin.

**Child:** Some girls in my class feel embarrassed talking about periods.

**Mother:** There is absolutely no reason for embarrassment. Menstruation is a natural biological process that happens approximately every 28 to 30 days. It is a sign that the reproductive system is healthy and functioning normally. The myths -- like isolating girls during their period or treating them as impure -- have zero scientific basis. The government runs programmes like the Menstrual Hygiene Scheme specifically to provide sanitary pads to girls in rural areas. The more openly we discuss this, the more comfortable everyone becomes.

## Standing Strong Against Pressure

**Child:** Maa, some senior students at school chew tobacco behind the sports shed. They say everyone does it and it is no big deal. Is that true?

**Mother:** That is absolutely not true, and it is extremely dangerous. Tobacco, gutka, cigarettes, alcohol, and drugs are all addictive substances. What begins as "just trying once" can quickly trap a person. The brain starts craving the substance, and breaking free becomes incredibly difficult. These substances damage lungs, liver, memory, and overall health.

**Child:** What if someone pushes me to try?

**Mother:** Look them in the eye and say, "No, I am not interested." You do not owe anyone an explanation. True friends will never pressure you to do something harmful. Surround yourself with people who respect your choices. And if you ever feel unsafe or unsure, come straight to me, your father, or a teacher.

**Child:** So the smartest thing is to never start?

**Mother:** That is exactly right. Prevention is far easier than cure. Focus on what truly nourishes you -- good food, regular exercise, proper sleep, strong friendships, and the hobbies that make you happy. These are the real foundations of a healthy adolescence. And remember, the National De-addiction Helpline number is 14446 -- anyone struggling can call for confidential help.
