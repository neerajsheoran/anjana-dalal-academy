## What Is Happening to Our Bodies?

**Meera:** Arjun, have you noticed how much everyone in our class has changed this year? Ananya has grown so tall, some boys' voices sound different, and half the kids have pimples. What's going on?

**Arjun:** My mummy explained this to me last week. We're all entering adolescence — the stage between childhood and adulthood. It usually starts around age 10 and goes till about 19.

**Meera:** So all this is normal?

**Arjun:** Completely normal. The body is preparing itself for adult life. That preparation involves a lot of visible and invisible changes.

**Meera:** But why is Ananya already so tall while Rohan looks like he was in Class 5 last week?

**Arjun:** Because every person's body follows its own schedule, yaar. Some kids start puberty earlier, some later. Height, weight, voice, skin — all change at different speeds for different people. There's no single "correct" timeline.

**Meera:** That actually makes me feel better. I was a little worried because I haven't shot up like some friends.

**Arjun:** Same. Our school counsellor explained it's all controlled by hormones — chemical messengers the body releases when it's ready. The brain sends signals, the hormones get released, and different parts of the body know when to grow.

**Meera:** So our bodies are basically doing their own thing on their own clock. Good to know.

## The Body's Transformation

**Meera:** Arjun, what exactly changes during adolescence? Like, biology-wise.

**Arjun:** Quite a lot. Both boys and girls grow taller and gain weight. Boys may notice shoulders broadening and voice deepening — because the voice box itself grows larger.

**Meera:** Is that the bump on the throat I've seen on my cousin?

**Arjun:** Yes, that's called the Adam's apple. Boys also start to grow facial hair over time. For girls, it's breast development and widening of the hips. Both grow hair in new places like underarms.

**Meera:** And pimples? Where do those suddenly come from?

**Arjun:** Skin produces more oil during this time. When the oil blocks the tiny pores, bacteria grow inside and that's a pimple. Mummy told me washing your face regularly, drinking plenty of water, eating fresh fruits, and avoiding too many oily snacks all help.

**Meera:** Do these changes that make boys and girls look different have a name?

**Arjun:** Secondary sexual characteristics. Deeper voice and facial hair in boys, breast development and wider hips in girls. They're visible signs of the body maturing.

**Meera:** And they mark the start of puberty?

**Arjun:** Right. Puberty is when the body starts developing the capacity for reproduction. It's all entirely natural — nothing to feel awkward about.

## Feelings That Ride a Roller Coaster

**Meera:** Arjun, can I tell you something honestly? Lately I feel really happy one moment, then suddenly upset or annoyed for no reason. Am I going crazy?

**Arjun:** Not at all, yaar. I get it too. My cousin who is in Class 10 said the same hormones that change our bodies also mess with our moods. Mood swings, feeling everything intensely, getting too sensitive about what friends say — all normal during these years.

**Meera:** Okay good. So how do you deal with it?

**Arjun:** Lots of things help. Physical activity is one of the best — badminton, yoga, going for a run. Exercise releases chemicals in the brain that lift your mood naturally.

**Meera:** I tried writing in a journal last week. Just dumping everything I was feeling on a page. It actually helped.

**Arjun:** Creative stuff like that is supposed to be great. Drawing, singing, even cooking with daadi. And when things feel really heavy, talking to someone you trust — your parents, a teacher, a close friend — always helps.

**Meera:** Is it also normal to feel super curious about everything? I get excited about random things now — like I read about ISRO's new mission and couldn't sleep.

**Arjun:** That's actually a wonderful quality. This is the age when many people discover their real passions. Sachin Tendulkar took cricket seriously during adolescence. Malala Yousafzai started speaking about education rights as a teenager. Don't waste that curiosity — channel it.

## Eating Right and Staying Clean

**Meera:** Arjun, our teacher said we need to eat better during adolescence than at any other age. Why?

**Arjun:** Because the body is growing at a pace it hasn't seen since we were babies. We need calcium from milk and ragi for strong bones. Proteins from moong dal, eggs, almonds — to build muscles and repair tissues.

**Meera:** Iron is the one I keep hearing about for girls.

**Arjun:** Right, iron from palak and dates is super important. Helps prevent anaemia. Vitamins from seasonal fruits for immunity. And carbs from roti and rice — for the energy to study and play.

**Meera:** What about hygiene? Is that more important now too?

**Arjun:** Much more. The body produces more sweat and oil during adolescence. Daily bathing and clean clothes are essential. Keeping underarm and private areas clean prevents infections and body odour.

**Meera:** And for girls — menstrual hygiene. Some girls in my class still feel weird talking about periods.

**Arjun:** They shouldn't. My mummy explained it really clearly to me too — periods are a natural biological process, every 28 to 30 days roughly. It's a sign the reproductive system is working healthily.

**Meera:** It's strange that some elders still treat girls as impure during those days.

**Arjun:** The myths have zero scientific basis. The government runs the Menstrual Hygiene Scheme to provide sanitary pads to girls in rural areas. The more openly people discuss it, the more comfortable everyone becomes.

**Meera:** Use pads or menstrual cups, change them regularly, wrap them in newspaper and dispose properly. Daadi taught me that.

**Arjun:** That's all there is to know, really. No mystery, no shame.

## Standing Strong Against Pressure

**Meera:** Arjun, I want to ask you something serious. There are some senior students who chew tobacco behind the sports shed. They told my cousin "everyone does it, it's no big deal." Is that true?

**Arjun:** Absolutely not, and it's actually really dangerous. Tobacco, gutka, cigarettes, alcohol, drugs — all addictive. What begins as "just trying once" can trap a person quickly. The brain starts craving the substance and breaking free becomes incredibly hard.

**Meera:** What do these things actually do?

**Arjun:** Damage lungs, liver, memory, overall health. My uncle is a doctor and he told me he sees patients in their 30s with diseases that should belong to 70-year-olds. All because of substances they started using as teenagers.

**Meera:** What if someone pushes me to try?

**Arjun:** Look them in the eye and say "No, I am not interested." You don't owe anyone an explanation. True friends will never pressure you to do something harmful.

**Meera:** So the smartest move is to never start.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Prevention is far easier than cure. Focus on what actually nourishes you — good food, exercise, proper sleep, real friendships, hobbies that make you happy.

**Meera:** And if someone we know is already struggling?

**Arjun:** There's the National De-addiction Helpline — 14446. Free and confidential. Anyone can call. We should both remember that number. You never know when a friend might need it.
