## Plants Are Self-Feeders

**Meera:** Arjun, I have been staring at the tulsi on our balcony for ten minutes. It is alive. It is growing. But nobody feeds it. How is that even possible?

**Arjun:** Plants make their own food. They do not need anyone to feed them. That is literally their superpower.

**Meera:** Make their own food from what though? They have no kitchen.

**Arjun:** Their kitchen is in their leaves. They use sunlight as energy, water from the roots, and carbon dioxide from the air. They mix all three using chlorophyll — that green pigment — and produce glucose. The process is called photosynthesis.

**Meera:** So the green colour of leaves is not just decoration. It is the actual machine?

**Arjun:** Exactly. Chlorophyll is the hero. It traps sunlight. Without it the whole process stops.

**Meera:** Can we prove that?

**Arjun:** Yes. There is a croton plant in my mami's garden — the kind with green and white patches on one leaf. If you test the leaf with iodine solution, only the green patches turn blue-black, showing starch. The white patches make no starch at all.

**Meera:** So no chlorophyll, no food. What if you put a normal green plant in a dark room?

**Arjun:** Then even the green parts make no starch. Both chlorophyll AND sunlight are needed. That is why our money plant near the window always leans towards the light. It is literally chasing its energy source.

**Meera:** And here I thought money plants were just being dramatic.

## How Scientists Proved Photosynthesis

**Meera:** Arjun, all this sounds good in theory. But how do scientists actually KNOW carbon dioxide is needed? You cannot see the gas.

**Arjun:** There is a clever experiment. You take a leaf still attached to the plant, put half of it inside a sealed bottle filled with caustic soda. The caustic soda absorbs all the carbon dioxide inside the bottle. The other half of the leaf stays outside in normal air.

**Meera:** Then?

**Arjun:** Leave it in sunlight for a few hours. Then pluck the leaf and test both halves with iodine. The outside half turns blue-black — starch was made. The inside half does nothing.

**Meera:** Because no carbon dioxide, no photosynthesis. That is elegant.

**Arjun:** And for oxygen, you use hydrilla — the water plant. Put it in a beaker of water in bright sunlight. Tiny bubbles stream up from the leaves.

**Meera:** I have actually seen this in our school's aquarium!

**Arjun:** Collect those bubbles in an inverted test tube. Then bring a glowing matchstick to the mouth. It flares up into a bright flame — that means the gas is oxygen.

**Meera:** So the full equation is: carbon dioxide plus water, with sunlight and chlorophyll, makes glucose and oxygen.

**Arjun:** Bang on. The plant uses the glucose for energy and stores the extra as starch. Different plants store starch in different places — rice and wheat in seeds, gajar and shakarkandi in roots, aloo and haldi in stems, mango and banana in fruits.

**Meera:** So when I eat aloo paratha, I am eating photosynthesis from two different plants — wheat and potato.

**Arjun:** Plus the ghee, which came from a buffalo that ate grass that did photosynthesis. Three layers deep.

## The Plant's Internal Highway

**Meera:** Arjun, there is a huge peepal tree near my school. Easily 15 metres tall. How does water from the roots reach leaves all the way at the top? That is like climbing a four-storey building.

**Arjun:** Through tubes called xylem. They run from the roots up through the trunk and branches and into every leaf.

**Meera:** Can we actually see those tubes working?

**Arjun:** There is a beautiful home experiment. Take a white rajnigandha flower — or even a white rose. Put it in a glass of water mixed with red food colour. Wait a day.

**Meera:** And?

**Arjun:** The petals slowly turn pinkish-red. The xylem carries the coloured water up. If you slice the stem you can even see the red channels.

**Meera:** Whoa. But what about going the other way? Food made in the leaves — how does it reach the roots underground?

**Arjun:** Different tubes. Phloem. Think of it as a two-way highway in the trunk. Xylem carries water and minerals up. Phloem carries food down and sideways to wherever it is needed.

**Meera:** Like a supply chain — raw materials going up to the factory, finished goods coming down to the warehouses.

**Arjun:** Exactly. And the stomata on the leaves are the factory's ventilation — carbon dioxide enters, oxygen leaves, water vapour escapes too.

**Meera:** All this in a single leaf?

**Arjun:** All this in a single leaf. From tiny tulsi seedlings to giant banyans — same system, same plumbing.

## Plants Breathe Too

**Meera:** Arjun, do plants breathe like we do?

**Arjun:** Yes! Every single living cell in a plant respires — roots, stems, leaves, flowers, even fruits. Oxygen comes in, glucose gets broken down, energy is released, carbon dioxide goes out. Same chemistry as in our cells.

**Meera:** But wait — during the day plants TAKE IN carbon dioxide for photosynthesis. Now you are saying they also give off carbon dioxide for respiration. Both at the same time?

**Arjun:** Yes, both happen at the same time during the day. But here is the trick — photosynthesis makes much more oxygen than respiration uses up. So during daylight, the net result is the plant releases oxygen.

**Meera:** And at night?

**Arjun:** At night, no sunlight, so photosynthesis stops. But respiration keeps going. So at night plants only release carbon dioxide.

**Meera:** Daadi told me never to sleep right under a big tree at night because the tree will "steal my oxygen." Is that real?

**Arjun:** That is the traditional reasoning. Technically outdoors the amount of carbon dioxide one tree produces is way too small to harm you. But the underlying biology she described is actually correct.

**Meera:** Daadi was kind of right after all.

**Arjun:** The real takeaway is that photosynthesis and respiration are partner processes. Photosynthesis locks sunlight energy into glucose. Respiration unlocks it whenever the plant needs to grow or make flowers. Together they also keep oxygen and carbon dioxide balanced in the air we breathe.

## Without Plants, There Would Be No Us

**Meera:** Arjun, here's a scary thought. What if every plant on Earth disappeared tomorrow?

**Arjun:** Game over for animals. Within weeks honestly.

**Meera:** That fast?

**Arjun:** Plants make the oxygen we breathe. They are also the first link in every food chain. Goats eat grass, then we drink the milk. Hens eat grain, then we eat eggs. Even fish in the ocean depend on tiny algae doing photosynthesis. Remove all plants and algae, and there is no food and no air left.

**Meera:** So my dinner tonight — palak dal and rice — that is all photosynthesis on a plate.

**Arjun:** Every meal you have ever eaten. Even the ghee comes from a cow that ate grass. The air in your lungs right now was made by some plant or alga somewhere.

**Meera:** This makes the school's tree-planting drive feel less like a chore.

**Arjun:** Way less. Every tree you plant produces oxygen for years, soaks up carbon dioxide, gives shade, shelters birds, enriches the soil.

**Meera:** Last Van Mahotsav my class planted five neem saplings. Three are still alive.

**Arjun:** That is three trees more than there were before you. Multiply that by every school in India — it is a serious contribution.

**Meera:** Yaar, I am going to remind Mummy to actually water our balcony plants this week.

**Arjun:** Forget reminding — go water them yourself. They are quietly doing more for you than most of your friends.
