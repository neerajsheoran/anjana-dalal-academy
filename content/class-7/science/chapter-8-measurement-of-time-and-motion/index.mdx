## Introduction

Think about the last time you watched a cricket match on television. The speed of a bowler's delivery is flashed on screen within a second -- 145 km/h! But rewind a few thousand years, and our ancestors had no clocks, no speedometers, and no digital displays. They relied on the movement of shadows, the flow of water, and the stars in the sky to track the passage of time.

How did humanity go from watching shadows to building atomic clocks that lose barely a second in millions of years? And how do we measure whether something is moving fast or slow? Let us find out.

> **Key idea:** Measuring time has been a fundamental human challenge. From shadow-based sundials to vibrating atoms, the quest for precision has driven remarkable inventions.

---

## Measuring Time in the Ancient World

Our ancestors noticed that certain natural events repeat at regular intervals -- the Sun rises and sets each day, the Moon waxes and wanes each month, and the seasons cycle each year. They used these repeating patterns to build calendars and track time.

For measuring shorter durations within a single day, ancient civilisations invented several clever instruments:

### Sundial

![Measuring time in the ancient world — sundial](measuring-time-in-the-ancient-world-sundial.png)

A **sundial** tells time by tracking the shifting position of a shadow cast by sunlight. As the Sun moves across the sky, the shadow rotates around a central pointer, landing on different markings that correspond to different hours.

India is home to one of the most impressive sundials ever built -- the **Samrat Yantra** at the **Jantar Mantar observatory in Jaipur**, Rajasthan. Constructed roughly 300 years ago by Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II, this colossal stone structure towers 27 metres high. Its shadow creeps along at about 1 millimetre per second, precise enough to measure intervals as short as 2 seconds.

### Water Clock

![Measuring time in the ancient world — water clock](measuring-time-in-the-ancient-world-water-clock.png)

Water clocks measured time through the steady flow of water. Two designs were common:
- **Draining type:** A vessel with markings on its side released water through a hole at the bottom. As the water level dropped, the markings indicated how much time had passed.
- **Filling type (Ghatika-yantra):** A small metal bowl with a pinhole at the bottom was placed on a larger vessel of water. Water slowly seeped into the bowl until it sank -- typically in about 24 minutes. Each sinking was announced by a drumbeat, a conch shell, or the ringing of a bell.

The **Ghatika-yantra** was widely used across ancient India -- in monasteries, royal courts, and town centres -- to regulate daily activities.

### Hourglass

![Measuring time in the ancient world — hourglass](measuring-time-in-the-ancient-world-hourglass.png)

An hourglass consisted of two glass bulbs connected by a narrow neck. Fine sand flowed from the upper bulb to the lower one at a steady rate, measuring a fixed duration each time it was flipped.

### Candle Clock

![Measuring time in the ancient world — candle clock](measuring-time-in-the-ancient-world-candle-clock.png)

Marked candles burned at a roughly constant rate. As the candle shortened, the exposed markings showed how much time had elapsed.

> **Key idea:** Every ancient time-keeping device relied on the same principle: a process that repeats itself at regular intervals -- whether a flowing shadow, dripping water, falling sand, or melting wax.

## The Simple Pendulum

The development of the **pendulum clock** in the 17th century transformed timekeeping. Its origin traces back to a famous observation by the Italian scientist Galileo Galilei.

### Galileo's Insight

![The simple pendulum — Galileo's insight](the-simple-pendulum-galileos-insight.png)

The story goes that young Galileo was sitting in a cathedral in Pisa when he noticed a hanging chandelier swaying gently in the breeze. Using his own pulse as a timer, he discovered something remarkable: whether the chandelier swung widely or narrowly, each swing took **exactly the same amount of time**.

### What Makes a Simple Pendulum?

![The simple pendulum — what makes a simple pendulum](the-simple-pendulum-what-makes-a-simple-pendulum.png)

A **simple pendulum** is made by suspending a small heavy ball (called the **bob**) from a fixed support using a long thread. When the bob is gently displaced to one side and released, it swings back and forth in a regular pattern. This repetitive back-and-forth movement is called **oscillatory motion**.

### One Complete Oscillation

![The simple pendulum — one complete oscillation](the-simple-pendulum-one-complete-oscillation.png)

The bob completes **one oscillation** when it:
1. Starts from the resting position (the **mean position**, O)
2. Swings to one extreme end (A)
3. Reverses direction and swings all the way to the opposite extreme end (B)
4. Reverses again and returns to the mean position (O)

The time taken for this complete journey is called the **time period** of the pendulum.

### Activity: Finding the Time Period

1. Tie a heavy washer or stone to a thread about 80 cm long.
2. Fix the free end to a door frame or a horizontal rod.
3. Pull the bob slightly to one side and release it gently (do not push it).
4. Using a stopwatch, time how long 20 complete oscillations take.
5. Divide by 20 to get the time period of one oscillation.
6. Repeat three or four times. The values will be remarkably consistent.

### What Controls the Time Period?

![The simple pendulum — what controls the time period](the-simple-pendulum-what-controls-the-time-period.png)

| Factor | Effect on Time Period |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Length of the thread** | Longer thread = slower swing = longer time period |
| **Weight of the bob** | Changing the weight has **no effect** on the time period |
| **Location on Earth** | At a given place, the time period for a given length stays the same |

> **Key idea:** The time period of a simple pendulum depends only on its **length**, not on the weight of the bob. This reliable, repeating motion made pendulums ideal for building accurate clocks.

## Modern Clocks

![Modern clocks — pendulum, quartz, and atomic](modern-clocks.png)

Every clock ever built -- ancient or modern -- is based on a single principle: a **process that repeats at a steady rate**.

- **Pendulum clocks** count the steady swings of a pendulum
- **Quartz clocks** count the rapid, tiny vibrations of a quartz crystal
- **Atomic clocks** count the incredibly fast vibrations of specific atoms

Early pendulum clocks could drift by 10 seconds in a day. Modern atomic clocks are so astonishingly precise that they would lose just **one second in several million years**.

## The SI Unit of Time

The internationally agreed-upon unit for measuring time is the **second** (symbol: **s**).

Larger time units:
- 60 seconds = 1 minute (min)
- 60 minutes = 1 hour (h)

**Rules for writing units correctly:**
- Unit names are written in lowercase (second, minute, hour), except at the start of a sentence.
- Symbols stay lowercase and singular: **s**, **min**, **h** (never "sec", "secs", or "hrs").
- No full stop after a symbol, unless the sentence ends there.
- Always leave a gap between the number and the unit: write **15 s**, not **15s**.

## Understanding Speed

![Understanding speed — distance covered per unit time](understanding-speed.png)

At a school sports day, Priya finishes the 200 metre race in 30 seconds while Arjun takes 40 seconds. Who ran faster? Priya did -- she covered the **same distance in less time**. We describe this by saying Priya had a higher **speed** than Arjun.

### What is Speed?

**Speed** tells us how much distance an object covers in a given unit of time.

**Formula:**

**Speed = Total distance covered / Total time taken**

### Units of Speed

Since speed is distance divided by time:
- If distance is in **metres** and time is in **seconds**, speed is in **m/s** (metres per second)
- If distance is in **kilometres** and time is in **hours**, speed is in **km/h** (kilometres per hour)

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Rohan cycles 4.5 km from home to his tuition class in 20 minutes. What is his cycling speed?

- Distance = 4.5 km = 4500 m
- Time = 20 min = 1200 s
- Speed = 4500 / 1200 = **3.75 m/s**

**Example 2:** An auto-rickshaw travels at a steady speed of 30 km/h for 2.5 hours. How far does it go?

- Distance = Speed x Time = 30 x 2.5 = **75 km**

**Example 3:** The Shatabdi Express runs at 120 km/h. How long will it take to cover 480 km?

- Time = Distance / Speed = 480 / 120 = **4 hours**

> **Key idea:** Speed = Distance / Time. This formula can be rearranged: Distance = Speed x Time, or Time = Distance / Speed.

### Speedometer and Odometer

![Speedometer and odometer — measuring current speed and total distance](speedometer-and-odometer.png)

Vehicles come equipped with two key instruments:
- **Speedometer:** Displays the vehicle's current speed, typically in km/h.
- **Odometer:** Records the total distance the vehicle has travelled in kilometres.

## Uniform and Non-uniform Linear Motion

When an object moves along a straight path, its motion is called **linear motion**.

### Uniform Linear Motion

![Uniform and non-uniform linear motion — uniform linear motion](uniform-and-non-uniform-linear-motion-uniform-linear-motion.png)

If an object travels in a straight line at a **constant speed** -- covering **equal distances in equal time intervals** -- its motion is called **uniform linear motion**.

**Example:** A goods train moves along a straight track, covering exactly 15 km every 10 minutes:
- 0-10 min: 15 km
- 10-20 min: 15 km
- 20-30 min: 15 km
- 30-40 min: 15 km

Each interval has the same distance, so this is uniform motion.

### Non-uniform Linear Motion

![Uniform and non-uniform linear motion — non-uniform linear motion](uniform-and-non-uniform-linear-motion-non-uniform-linear-motion.png)

If the speed of an object moving along a straight line **keeps changing** -- meaning it covers **unequal distances in equal time intervals** -- its motion is called **non-uniform linear motion**.

**Example:** An auto-rickshaw in Jaipur traffic might cover 12 km in the first 10 minutes (on an open road), 4 km in the next 10 minutes (stuck near a red light), and 8 km in the third 10 minutes (moving again). The distances are unequal, so this is non-uniform motion.

### Real-Life Motion

A Rajdhani Express leaving New Delhi station illustrates both types:
1. It starts **slowly**, picking up speed (non-uniform)
2. On the open track, it reaches a **steady cruising speed** (uniform)
3. Approaching the next station, it **brakes and slows down** (non-uniform)

> **Key idea:** **Uniform motion** = constant speed, equal distances in equal time intervals. **Non-uniform motion** = changing speed, unequal distances in equal time intervals. In daily life, non-uniform motion is far more common.

## Average Speed

![Average speed — total distance divided by total time](average-speed.png)

Real-world journeys almost never happen at a perfectly constant speed. A school bus accelerates, slows for speed breakers, halts at stops, and navigates turns. For such journeys, we calculate the **average speed**:

**Average Speed = Total distance covered / Total time taken**

**Example:** A family drives from Jaipur to Udaipur. They cover 120 km in the first 2 hours, 90 km in the next 1.5 hours, and 90 km in the last 1.5 hours.
- Total distance = 120 + 90 + 90 = 300 km
- Total time = 2 + 1.5 + 1.5 = 5 hours
- Average speed = 300 / 5 = **60 km/h**

The car was not always going at 60 km/h -- sometimes faster on the highway, sometimes slower through towns. But the average speed gives a useful picture of the overall journey.

## Summary

- Ancient time-measuring devices include **sundials**, **water clocks** (Ghatika-yantra), **hourglasses**, and **candle clocks**. All rely on a repeating process.
- A **simple pendulum** completes one oscillation when the bob travels from the mean position to one extreme, to the opposite extreme, and back to the mean position.
- The **time period** depends on the **length** of the pendulum but **not** on the weight of the bob.
- The **SI unit of time** is the **second (s)**. 60 s = 1 min; 60 min = 1 h.
- **Speed** = Distance / Time. The SI unit is **m/s**; another common unit is **km/h**.
- **Uniform linear motion** involves constant speed with equal distances in equal intervals. **Non-uniform linear motion** involves changing speed with unequal distances in equal intervals.
- **Average speed** = Total distance / Total time. It provides a useful overall measure for journeys with varying speed.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

Time and motion measurement is invisibly built into almost everything modern India runs on.

**Jantar Mantar -- a 300-year-old computer**: The massive sundials at the **Jantar Mantar observatory** in Jaipur, built by Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II in the 1720s, are still functional today. The **Samrat Yantra** (the world's largest sundial) measures time to within two seconds -- accurate enough that astronomers from the Indian Institute of Astrophysics still occasionally use it for educational demonstrations. You can stand at its base and watch the shadow inch forward at exactly 1 millimetre per second.

**Vande Bharat Express and the speedometer in your pocket**: When the **Vande Bharat** trains speed across the New Delhi–Varanasi corridor at 160 km/h, sensors update the driver's speedometer **ten times a second**. The same maths -- distance / time -- but happening so fast that humans cannot perceive the calculation. Your phone's GPS app uses the same formula to tell you how long Mumbai's Western Express Highway will take during morning traffic.

**Olympic timing -- the Neeraj Chopra story**: When Neeraj Chopra threw the javelin to win India's first ever Olympic athletics gold in Tokyo 2020, the distance (87.58 metres) and his approach speed were measured with laser sensors that work in microseconds. The same physics you just learned, just with much fancier stopwatches.

**Pendulums in temple towers**: Many old temple clocks across South India -- including the famous one at **Brihadeeswarar Temple in Thanjavur** -- use the constant time-period property of pendulums you just studied. Galileo's discovery walked all the way from a cathedral in Pisa into Tamil Nadu temple architecture.

**ISRO's atomic clocks**: India's **NavIC** satellite navigation system depends on atomic clocks accurate to **one second every ten million years**. Every time a Mumbai cab driver opens Google Maps, an atomic clock somewhere in orbit is timing his journey to the millimetre.

### Try it at home -- The Pendulum Investigation

**What you'll need:**

- About 1 metre of strong thread
- A heavy washer, a small stone, or a key (this becomes your bob)
- A stopwatch (phone is fine)
- A horizontal pole, the back of a chair, or the top of a door frame to hang from
- Your wonder notebook

**Setup:**

1. Tie one end of the thread firmly to the washer or stone.
2. Tie the other end to your chosen support so the bob can swing freely.
3. Start with the thread length at about 50 cm.

**Experiment 1 -- Find the time period**

1. Pull the bob gently to one side (about 20 degrees from vertical -- not too high). Release without pushing.
2. Use the stopwatch to time how long the pendulum takes to complete **20 oscillations**.
3. Divide by 20 to get the **time period** of one swing.
4. Repeat twice more. The values should be remarkably close.

**Experiment 2 -- Does the bob's weight matter?**

1. Replace the bob with something twice as heavy (two stones taped together, or a bigger key).
2. Keep the thread length the same.
3. Time 20 oscillations again.
4. Did the time period change?

**Experiment 3 -- Does thread length matter?**

1. Shorten the thread to 25 cm.
2. Time 20 oscillations.
3. Now lengthen it to 75 cm.
4. Time 20 oscillations.
5. Compare.

**What to look for:**

- **Experiment 2**: The weight of the bob should make **no difference** to the time period. This will surprise you -- and it is exactly what Galileo discovered.
- **Experiment 3**: A longer pendulum takes longer per swing. Specifically, doubling the length increases the time period by about 1.4 times (√2).

**Bring it back to chapter**: You have just done, in your kitchen, the exact experiment Galileo did in a cathedral in 1583. The time period depends only on length -- which is why grandfather clocks all have pendulums of roughly the same length (about 99 cm for a one-second swing).
