## How Did People Tell Time Long Ago?

**Child:** Maa, before clocks existed, how did people know what time it was?

**Mother:** They watched nature's patterns, beta. The Sun rises and sets every day. The Moon changes shape every month. Seasons rotate every year. For measuring smaller time gaps within a day, they built clever devices. A sundial, for instance, uses the moving shadow of a stick to show the hour.

**Child:** Like the giant one at Jantar Mantar in Jaipur? I saw it in our textbook!

**Mother:** Yes, the Samrat Yantra! Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II had it built around 300 years ago. It stands 27 metres tall and its shadow moves so steadily that it can measure time down to 2 seconds. Our ancestors in India also used the Ghatika-yantra -- a small metal bowl with a tiny hole at the bottom, floating on water. It slowly filled up and sank every 24 minutes. A drumbeat or bell ring would announce each sinking.

**Child:** So every old clock was based on something that repeats?

**Mother:** Exactly right. A shadow moving, water filling, sand dropping, wax melting -- all repeat at steady rates. Even today's most advanced atomic clocks work the same way, just with atoms vibrating instead of water dripping. The principle has never changed.

## The Pendulum and What Controls It

**Child:** Maa, how did Galileo figure out the pendulum?

**Mother:** The story is lovely. Young Galileo was sitting in a cathedral and noticed a chandelier swinging in the breeze. He timed the swings using his own pulse and found something surprising -- whether the chandelier swung widely or narrowly, each swing took the same time.

**Child:** So the size of the swing does not matter?

**Mother:** Not for the time period, no. What matters is the length of the pendulum. A longer pendulum swings more slowly. And here is the fascinating part -- the weight of the bob does not affect the time period at all. A pendulum with a heavy brass bob and one with a light plastic bob, both the same length, will swing at exactly the same rate.

**Child:** That feels wrong! I would think the heavier one would be different.

**Mother:** Many people expect that. But when you actually experiment, the results are clear. This is what makes science special -- we trust experiments over guesses. Try it at home with two threads of the same length, one with a heavy lock as the bob and one with a light eraser. Time them yourself and you will see identical periods.

## Speed: How Fast Is Fast?

**Child:** Maa, during sports day, how do judges decide who ran faster?

**Mother:** Simple -- the runner who covers the same distance in less time is faster. We measure this with speed. Speed equals distance divided by time. If your friend Meera runs 100 metres in 16 seconds, her speed is 100 divided by 16, which is 6.25 metres per second.

**Child:** And if I know speed and time, I can find distance?

**Mother:** Yes! Distance equals speed multiplied by time. If an auto-rickshaw goes at 30 km/h for 2 hours, it covers 60 km. And if you know distance and speed, you can find time. The Shatabdi Express at 120 km/h takes 4 hours to cover 480 km. One formula with three uses.

**Child:** What are those dials on the dashboard of our car, Maa?

**Mother:** The speedometer shows how fast the car is moving right now, in km/h. The odometer shows the total distance the car has ever travelled. When we drove from Lucknow to Varanasi last month, the odometer went up by about 300 km.

## Uniform Motion vs Non-Uniform Motion

**Child:** Maa, does a train always move at the same speed?

**Mother:** Almost never. When it leaves the platform, it starts slowly and picks up speed -- that is non-uniform motion. On the open track between stations, it may cruise at a steady speed -- that is uniform motion, covering equal distances in equal time intervals. As it approaches the next station, it slows down and stops -- non-uniform again.

**Child:** So most movement in real life is non-uniform?

**Mother:** Yes. Think about our car in city traffic -- it speeds up, slows down at speed breakers, stops at red lights. The speed constantly changes. For such journeys, we use average speed. If we cover 45 km in the first hour and 35 km in the second, our average speed is 80 divided by 2, which is 40 km/h.

**Child:** Even though we were not always going exactly 40?

**Mother:** Correct. Average speed gives us a useful overall picture. The Vande Bharat Express might have an average speed of about 100 km/h over its entire route, but its actual speed at any moment varies -- zero at stations, much higher in between.

## Why Precise Units Matter

**Child:** Maa, my teacher marked me wrong for writing "20 sec" instead of "20 s" in a science answer. Is it really that important?

**Mother:** In science, units follow strict international rules. The SI unit of time is the second, and its symbol is just "s" -- lowercase, no full stop, no plural. Writing "sec" or "secs" is considered incorrect. Similarly, speed in metres per second is written as "m/s", not "mps" or "m.p.s."

**Child:** And we must leave a space between the number and the unit?

**Mother:** Always. "20 s" is correct, "20s" is not. These rules exist so that scientists, engineers, and students everywhere in the world can communicate without confusion. Getting units wrong can have real consequences -- there was a famous incident where a space mission failed because one team used one system of units and another team used a different one. The spacecraft was lost because of a simple unit mismatch!

**Child:** So being careful with units is not just about marks -- it matters in real engineering?

**Mother:** Absolutely, beta. A bridge engineer needs millimetre precision. An Olympic timer needs millisecond accuracy. And modern atomic clocks are so precise they drift by only one second in millions of years. As our technology grows, so does our need for exact measurement.
