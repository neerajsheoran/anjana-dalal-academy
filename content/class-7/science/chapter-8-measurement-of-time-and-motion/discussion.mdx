## How Did People Tell Time Long Ago?

**Meera:** Arjun, I've been thinking about this for days — before clocks existed, how did anyone know what time it was? Like, how did people in our great-great-grandparents' generation know when to wake up?

**Arjun:** They watched the sky. Sun rises and sets every day. Moon changes shape every month. Seasons repeat every year. For shorter time, they built clever devices.

**Meera:** Like a sundial? I saw a HUGE one in our textbook — Jantar Mantar in Jaipur.

**Arjun:** The Samrat Yantra! That one was built by Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II around 300 years ago. It's 27 metres tall — taller than most apartment buildings in our mohalla.

**Meera:** And it actually tells the time?

**Arjun:** Down to 2 seconds. Its shadow moves at exactly 1 millimetre per second. People stand at the base just to watch the shadow creep along. That's a 300-year-old computer made of stone.

**Meera:** Yaar. And what did people do indoors, where there was no Sun?

**Arjun:** Water clocks. There was an Indian one called the Ghatika-yantra — a small metal bowl with a tiny hole at the bottom, floating on water. It slowly filled up and sank in about 24 minutes. Every time it sank, someone would beat a drum or ring a bell.

**Meera:** So whether it's shadows, water, sand, melting wax — they all use something that repeats at a steady rate?

**Arjun:** That's the trick. Even modern atomic clocks work the same way, just with atoms vibrating instead of water dripping. The principle hasn't changed in thousands of years.

## The Pendulum and What Controls It

**Meera:** Arjun, my science teacher told us this story about Galileo and a chandelier. Is it actually true?

**Arjun:** The story is famous. Young Galileo was sitting in a cathedral in Pisa, bored probably, and noticed a chandelier swinging in the breeze. He timed the swings using his own pulse.

**Meera:** His pulse? Like, his heartbeat?

**Arjun:** He didn't have a stopwatch. And he found something weird — whether the chandelier swung wide or narrow, every swing took the same amount of time.

**Meera:** Wait, really? The size of the swing doesn't matter?

**Arjun:** Doesn't matter at all. What matters is how long the pendulum is. Longer thread = slower swing.

**Meera:** OK but what about the weight? If I tie a heavy lock vs a tiny eraser to the same length of thread, surely the heavier one swings differently?

**Arjun:** That's what everyone expects. But no — weight makes zero difference. Same length = same time period, no matter what's hanging at the end.

**Meera:** That can't be right. Let me try it myself.

**Arjun:** Please do, yaar. That's the whole point — don't trust me, trust the experiment. Tie a heavy bunch of keys on one thread and a light pencil eraser on another thread of the same length. Time them. You'll see I'm telling the truth.

**Meera:** Fine. But if I prove you wrong, I'm telling daadi.

**Arjun:** Deal. But you won't.

## Speed: How Fast Is Fast?

**Meera:** Arjun, during sports day last week, how did the judges know who actually ran fastest? Some of those finishes looked exactly the same.

**Arjun:** Whoever covers the same distance in less time wins. That's speed. Speed equals distance divided by time.

**Meera:** OK give me a real number. I ran 100 metres in 16 seconds.

**Arjun:** Then your speed was 100 divided by 16, which is 6.25 metres per second. Not bad, actually.

**Meera:** Hmm. And what if I know the speed of something and want to figure out distance?

**Arjun:** Just flip the formula. Distance = speed multiplied by time. If a rickshaw goes at 30 km/h for 2 hours, it covers 60 km. Or for time: the Shatabdi Express at 120 km/h takes 4 hours to cover 480 km. One formula, three uses.

**Meera:** That's neat. By the way — my dadu's car has those two round dials on the dashboard. What are they?

**Arjun:** Speedometer and odometer. Speedometer shows how fast right now, in km/h. Odometer shows the total distance the car has ever travelled. When you drove from Lucknow to Varanasi last month, the odometer went up by about 300 km.

**Meera:** So the odometer is basically the car's life story written in kilometres.

**Arjun:** Pretty much. Dadu can tell you exactly when he bought a new car based on the number.

## Uniform Motion vs Non-Uniform Motion

**Meera:** Arjun, when a train is running, does it always go at the same speed?

**Arjun:** Almost never. Think about it — when it leaves the platform, it starts slow and picks up speed. That's non-uniform motion. Then on the open track between stations, it might cruise steadily — that's uniform motion.

**Meera:** And then it slows down again when it reaches the next station?

**Arjun:** Right. Non-uniform again. So one journey has both kinds of motion in it.

**Meera:** So most actual movement in real life is non-uniform?

**Arjun:** Almost all of it. Think about an auto-rickshaw in city traffic. It speeds up, brakes for speed breakers, stops at red lights, jerks forward again. The speed never holds still for more than a few seconds.

**Meera:** Then how do you describe such a messy journey with one number?

**Arjun:** Average speed. Total distance divided by total time. If you cover 45 km in the first hour and 35 km in the second hour, your average is 80 divided by 2, which is 40 km/h.

**Meera:** Even though I was never actually going exactly 40 at any moment?

**Arjun:** Exactly. The average is just a useful summary. The Vande Bharat Express might average about 100 km/h over its whole route, but its real speed at any second varies — zero at stations, much higher between them.

**Meera:** So average is like a report card grade. It doesn't tell you what happened in any one test, but it summarises the whole year.

**Arjun:** That's actually a perfect comparison.

## Why Precise Units Matter

**Meera:** Arjun, my teacher cut a mark off my answer because I wrote "20 sec" instead of "20 s". Doesn't that feel a bit harsh?

**Arjun:** It does feel petty, but actually there's a real reason. Science units follow strict international rules. The SI unit of time is the second, and its symbol is just "s" — lowercase, no full stop, no plural. "Sec" and "secs" don't exist in SI.

**Meera:** Same for speed?

**Arjun:** Same. Metres per second is written "m/s", not "mps" or "m.p.s." Kilometres per hour is "km/h". And there's always a space between the number and the unit — "20 s", never "20s".

**Meera:** Why so strict though?

**Arjun:** Because scientists, engineers, and students across every country need to read each other's work without confusion. There was this famous incident — a NASA spacecraft heading to Mars was lost because one team was working in imperial units and another in metric, and nobody caught the mismatch. The whole mission vanished.

**Meera:** A whole mission lost because of units? That's painful.

**Arjun:** Hundreds of crores of rupees, gone. So yes, the strictness sounds annoying in seventh standard, but it actually matters a lot. A bridge engineer needs millimetre precision. An Olympic timer needs millisecond accuracy.

**Meera:** And ISRO's satellites use atomic clocks accurate to one second in millions of years.

**Arjun:** Right. As technology gets sharper, the demand for exact measurement only grows. Your teacher cutting one mark is honestly a kindness — better to learn the discipline now than mess up a spacecraft in twenty years.

**Meera:** Fine yaar, I'll write "s" properly from tomorrow. But I'm still a little upset.
