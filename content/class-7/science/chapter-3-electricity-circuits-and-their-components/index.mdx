## Introduction

Imagine waking up one morning and discovering that there is no electricity in your entire city. No lights, no fans, no refrigerator to keep food fresh, no mobile phone charging, no metro trains running. Life would come to a standstill within hours!

Electricity reaches our homes, schools, and hospitals from power stations that generate it using various sources -- flowing water in dams (hydroelectric), sunlight (solar panels), wind (windmills), and burning fuels like coal and natural gas. Wires stretching across poles and underground cables carry this energy to wherever it is needed.

Here are some ways we use electricity every day:

- **In the kitchen:** Mixer-grinder, induction cooktop, electric roti maker, microwave
- **For comfort:** Ceiling fan, air cooler, room heater, geyser
- **For light:** Tube lights, bulbs, street lamps, decorative lights during Diwali
- **For travel:** Metro trains, electric rickshaws, electric scooters, lifts in buildings
- **For staying connected:** Smartphones, laptops, Wi-Fi routers
- **For fun:** Television, gaming consoles, music speakers

> **Key idea:** Electricity is among the most essential forms of energy in modern life. It is generated from multiple sources and delivered to our homes through networks of wires.

**Safety first:** Never experiment with electricity from wall sockets, switchboards, or generators. Household electricity is extremely dangerous and can cause fatal injuries. For all experiments, use only dry cells (like the ones in TV remotes, wall clocks, or torchlights).

## Inside a Torchlight

A torchlight is one of the simplest electrical devices, and taking one apart can teach you a great deal about how electricity works. Inside a typical torch, you will find:

- One or more **electric cells** (which store chemical energy and convert it to electrical energy)
- A **lamp** (the component that produces light)
- A **sliding switch** (to turn the torch on and off)

Slide the switch forward and the lamp lights up. Slide it back and the lamp goes dark. But what exactly happens inside when you move that switch? The answer involves understanding **electrical circuits**.

## Building Blocks of a Circuit

### The Electric Cell

An **electric cell** is a compact, portable source of electrical energy. Examine a cell closely and you will see:

- One end has a raised **metal button** -- this is the **positive terminal (+)**
- The opposite end has a **flat metal plate** -- this is the **negative terminal (-)**

These terminals are clearly stamped with **+** and **-** markings on the cell's body.

### From Cell to Battery

A single cell provides a limited amount of energy. When you need more power or longer-lasting energy, you connect multiple cells together. This combination is called a **battery**.

In a battery, cells are arranged **in series**:
- The **positive end** of the first cell connects to the **negative end** of the second cell
- The **positive end** of the second cell connects to the **negative end** of the third, and so on

Adding more cells provides greater energy to the circuit.

> **Key idea:** A single cell is a source of electrical energy. Connecting two or more cells end-to-end (positive to negative) creates a **battery** that provides more energy.

### The Incandescent Lamp

![Labelled diagram of an electric cell showing positive terminal, negative terminal, and internal structure](electric-cell-labelled.png)

An **incandescent lamp** (the traditional light bulb) contains:

- A **sealed glass globe** that encloses and protects the internal parts
- A very thin coiled wire inside called the **filament** -- this is what produces light
- Two **support wires** holding the filament in position
- A **threaded metal base** (one electrical contact point)
- A **small metal dot** at the bottom of the base (the second contact point)

When electricity flows through the filament, it heats up enormously and begins to **glow**, emitting bright light. The two contact points on the lamp must be connected to different terminals of the cell -- they should never touch each other directly.

> **Key idea:** Inside an incandescent lamp, the **filament** -- a thin wire -- heats up and glows brilliantly when electric current passes through it.

### The LED Lamp

Modern torchlights and household lighting increasingly use **Light Emitting Diodes (LEDs)** instead of traditional incandescent lamps.

Key distinctions between LEDs and incandescent lamps:

| Feature | Incandescent Lamp | LED Lamp |
|---------|------------------|----------|
| Contains a filament? | Yes | No |
| Connection direction matters? | No (works either way) | Yes (must connect correctly) |
| Electricity flow | Both directions | Single direction only |
| Energy usage | Higher (wastes energy as heat) | Lower (much more efficient) |

An LED has two wire legs of different lengths:
- The **longer leg** is the **positive terminal (+)**
- The **shorter leg** is the **negative terminal (-)**

> **Key idea:** An LED lights up only when its longer leg (positive) connects to the battery's positive terminal and its shorter leg (negative) connects to the negative terminal. Current travels through an LED in one direction only.

## Lighting Up a Lamp

To make a lamp glow, you must create an unbroken pathway for electric current. You will need:

- A cell or battery
- A lamp (incandescent or LED)
- Connecting wires (with exposed metal ends)

**How to assemble the circuit:**

1. Strip approximately 1 cm of plastic insulation from each end of two wires to reveal the metal inside.
2. Secure one wire to each terminal of the cell (using a cell holder or adhesive tape).
3. Connect the free end of one wire to one contact point of the lamp.
4. Connect the free end of the other wire to the other contact point of the lamp.

The lamp will glow only when:
- Each terminal of the lamp connects to a different terminal of the cell
- The pathway from cell to lamp and back is **completely unbroken**

![Simple electrical circuit with a cell, connecting wires, switch, and lamp showing current flow](simple-electric-circuit.png)

This arrangement is called an **electrical circuit** -- a closed loop that allows current to flow continuously.

> **Key idea:** An electrical circuit is an unbroken loop that gives electric current a complete path to travel. The lamp glows only when this loop has no gaps.

### Which Way Does Current Flow?

By convention, electric current is said to flow from the **positive terminal** of the cell, through the circuit, and back to the **negative terminal**.

## The Electric Switch

A **switch** is a device that either **completes** or **interrupts** an electrical circuit at will.

### How It Operates

- **ON (closed circuit):** The switch bridges the gap, completing the loop. Current flows and the lamp glows.
- **OFF (open circuit):** The switch creates a gap in the loop. Current cannot cross the gap, so the lamp stays dark.

### Build Your Own Switch

You can construct a working switch from simple materials:

1. Gather two metal thumb tacks, a metal paper clip, and a thick piece of cardboard.
2. Push one thumb tack through the loop end of the paper clip and press it into the cardboard.
3. Push the second thumb tack into the cardboard at a distance where the paper clip can swing to touch it.
4. Attach a wire to each thumb tack.

When the paper clip touches both thumb tacks, the circuit closes and current flows. When the paper clip swings away, the circuit opens and current stops.

You can position a switch **at any point** in a circuit -- it does not need to be next to the cell or the lamp. Home switches operate on this same principle, though they are designed with safety enclosures.

> **Key idea:** A switch opens or closes an electrical circuit. In the ON position, current flows through the complete loop. In the OFF position, a gap stops the current.

## Reading and Drawing Circuit Diagrams

Sketching realistic pictures of wires, cells, and lamps is cumbersome. Scientists instead use standardised **symbols** to represent circuit components. A drawing made with these symbols is called a **circuit diagram**.

![Circuit diagram symbols for cell, battery, lamp, LED, switch (on and off), and wire](circuit-diagram-symbols.png)

### Standard Symbols

| Component | Symbol Description |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Electric cell** | Two parallel lines: the longer line represents positive (+), the shorter line represents negative (-) |
| **Battery** | Several cell symbols drawn in sequence |
| **Incandescent lamp** | A circle with an X inside |
| **LED** | A triangle pointing in the current direction, with two small arrows indicating emitted light |
| **Switch (ON)** | Two dots connected by a straight line |
| **Switch (OFF)** | Two dots with a tilted line showing a gap |
| **Wire** | A simple straight line |

> **Key idea:** A **circuit diagram** uses universally accepted symbols to represent electrical circuits. An engineer in Bengaluru and a scientist in Tokyo can read the same circuit diagram without any confusion.

## Which Materials Carry Electricity?

Have you noticed that the inner core of a charging cable is metal, but the outer covering is plastic? There is an important reason for this design.

### Testing Materials for Conductivity

![Conduction tester circuit setup with two free wire ends for testing materials](conduction-tester-setup.png)

You can build a **conductivity tester** to check which materials let electricity pass:

1. Set up a circuit with a cell and a lamp, leaving two wire ends free (not connected to anything).
2. Touch the two free ends together -- if the lamp glows, your tester is working.
3. Now place various objects between the two free ends and observe whether the lamp lights up.

### What You Will Find

| Material | Does the lamp glow? | Classification |
|----------|-------------------|----------------|
| Iron safety pin | Yes | Conductor |
| Five-rupee coin | Yes | Conductor |
| Brass key | Yes | Conductor |
| Aluminium foil from a chocolate wrapper | Yes | Conductor |
| Graphite from a pencil (pencil lead) | Yes | Conductor |
| Wooden ruler | No | Insulator |
| Plastic bottle cap | No | Insulator |
| Rubber band | No | Insulator |
| Piece of cardboard | No | Insulator |
| Glass marble | No | Insulator |
| Cotton thread | No | Insulator |
| Wax candle | No | Insulator |

### Conductors

Materials that allow electric current to pass through them freely are called **conductors**. Nearly all **metals** are good conductors. That is why the wire inside an electrical cable is made of metal -- usually **copper**.

Among metals, **silver** conducts electricity best, followed by copper and gold. However, **copper** is the practical choice for wiring because it is far less expensive than silver or gold and is available in large quantities.

### Insulators

Materials that block the passage of electric current are called **insulators** (also known as poor conductors). Plastic, rubber, wood, glass, ceramic, and cloth are common insulators.

Insulators serve a critical safety function -- they are used to **coat wires, enclose switches, and cover plug tops** so that people do not accidentally touch live conductors.

> **Key idea:** **Conductors** (mainly metals) let current flow through them easily. **Insulators** (like plastic, rubber, and wood) block current flow. Both are essential -- conductors deliver electricity, and insulators protect us from harm.

## Staying Safe Around Electricity

The human body can conduct electricity. If household current passes through your body, it can cause severe burns, muscle damage, or even death. Follow these rules strictly:

- Never touch any switch, plug, or appliance with **damp or wet hands**
- Never use electrical equipment near **water or in wet areas**
- Never play with or touch wires that have **damaged or torn insulation**
- Never insert anything into a **wall socket or power strip**
- Only use **dry cells** (not household current) for science experiments

---

## Two Kinds of Electric Current

Electric current comes in two forms:

- **Direct Current (DC):** Produced by cells and batteries. It flows steadily in **one direction**. Used in torchlights, remote controls, wristwatches, and mobile phone batteries.
- **Alternating Current (AC):** Supplied by power stations through wall sockets. It rapidly **reverses direction** many times per second. It powers heavy appliances like refrigerators, washing machines, air conditioners, and industrial machinery.

## Summary

- An **electric cell** stores chemical energy and converts it to electrical energy. It has a positive (+) and a negative (-) terminal.
- Multiple cells connected in series (positive to negative) form a **battery**.
- In an **incandescent lamp**, a thin **filament** heats up and glows when current flows through it.
- An **LED** has no filament, passes current in only one direction, and is more energy-efficient. Its longer leg is positive.
- An **electrical circuit** is a complete, unbroken loop that allows current to flow.
- A **switch** opens or closes a circuit, controlling the flow of current.
- Current is conventionally said to flow from positive to negative terminal.
- **Circuit diagrams** use standardised symbols recognised worldwide.
- **Conductors** (metals) allow current to flow; **insulators** (plastic, rubber, wood) do not.
- Household electricity (AC) is dangerous. Only use cells (DC) for experiments.
