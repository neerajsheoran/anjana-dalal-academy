## Why Metals Rule the Kitchen

**Meera:** Arjun, yaar, daadi made aloo paratha this morning on her iron tawa. I tried picking up the tawa by its iron edge and almost burned my fingers. But the wooden handle wrapped around the rod stayed totally cool. How?

**Arjun:** Because iron conducts heat beautifully. The flame's energy travels through the tawa quickly — that's why your paratha cooks evenly. But if the handle were also iron, it would burn you the second you touched it.

**Meera:** So daadi has been quietly using science her whole life. Iron for the cooking part, wood for the holding part.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Wood is a terrible conductor of heat. It stays cool even when the metal next to it is hot enough to fry an egg.

**Meera:** Is that the only special thing about metals?

**Arjun:** Oh no. They have a whole personality. They're shiny — that gleam on your new steel lunchbox is called metallic lustre. They're malleable — they can be hammered into thin sheets, like the aluminium foil in your tiffin. They're ductile — they can be pulled into thin wires, like the copper wiring at home. And they're sonorous — they ring when you tap them.

**Meera:** Sonorous! That's why my mummy taps the steel tumbler to check if the oil in the kadhai is the right temperature — she listens for the ring.

**Arjun:** And that's also why mandir bells, ghungroos, manjiras during bhajan-kirtan — all metal. Non-metals like charcoal or wood can't ring like that. They thud or crack.

**Meera:** So each metal has, like, a list of superpowers.

**Arjun:** Pretty much. And this chapter is basically the manual to those superpowers.

## The Battle Against Rust

**Meera:** Arjun, the iron railing on our balcony has gone all reddish-brown and rough. Bauji says it is rusting and we should call someone. What is rust actually?

**Arjun:** Rust is what happens when iron meets oxygen from the air AND moisture from water at the same time. The brown flaky stuff is iron oxide — a totally new substance forming on the iron's surface.

**Meera:** So if I keep iron away from water, no rust?

**Arjun:** Or away from air. Scientists actually proved this with three sealed bottles. One nail in dry air — no rust. One nail in water with no air access — no rust. One nail in moist air, where both reached it — rusted in days. You need both.

**Meera:** So Mumbai is rust-paradise. All that humidity and salty sea breeze.

**Arjun:** Exactly. People near the sea repaint their gates every monsoon. Painting blocks air and moisture. So does oil or grease. And there's galvanisation — coating iron with a thin layer of zinc, so the zinc faces the weather instead of the iron.

**Meera:** But Arjun, what about that Iron Pillar near Qutub Minar? My cousin took me there last winter. Hasn't rusted in over a thousand years!

**Arjun:** Sixteen hundred years, actually. Built during Chandragupta II. Indian metalworkers back then somehow made iron of such ridiculous purity and composition that it just refuses to rust. Scientists at IIT have studied it for decades. They found a thin protective phosphate layer on the surface — basically ancient metallurgy outperforming modern factories.

**Meera:** And we think we invented technology.

**Arjun:** Haan, our ancestors were not playing around. Also — corrosion isn't just an iron problem. Copper develops green patches. Silver turns black. Every metal has its own way of slowly giving in.

## When Metals and Non-Metals Burn

**Meera:** Arjun, what actually happens when you burn a metal? Like, in the air?

**Arjun:** The metal combines with oxygen and forms a metal oxide. And here's the interesting part — metal oxides are basic in nature.

**Meera:** Basic like baking soda?

**Arjun:** Yes. Take magnesium ribbon. We did this in school last term — you light it with a spirit lamp and it burns with a dazzling white flame. Don't look directly, it's actually painful. What's left behind is a white powder, magnesium oxide. Dissolve it in water, dip red litmus paper — turns blue. Confirmed basic.

**Meera:** And non-metals?

**Arjun:** Opposite. Burn sulfur — that yellow powdery non-metal — and you get sulfur dioxide gas with a sharp, choking smell. Dissolve that in water and you get sulfurous acid. Blue litmus turns red. So non-metal oxides are acidic.

**Meera:** Yaar, this connects directly to the last chapter — acids and bases! Metal oxides one side, non-metal oxides the other.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Chemistry chapters always end up holding hands.

**Meera:** Does this mean some metals and non-metals are too dangerous to even keep in the open?

**Arjun:** Yes. Sodium is so reactive it catches fire the moment it touches air or water. So it's stored submerged in kerosene. Phosphorus, a non-metal, is the same — bursts into flame in open air, so it's stored under water. Every element has its own personality. Some are calm, some are drama queens.

## Metals That Do Not Follow the Rules

**Meera:** Arjun, are ALL metals hard and solid? Because I read somewhere that some metals are basically squishy.

**Arjun:** You read right. Sodium and potassium are metals so soft you can slice them with a butter knife. And mercury is the only metal that exists as a liquid at room temperature — that shiny silver stuff in old-style thermometers is mercury.

**Meera:** Liquid metal sounds straight out of a sci-fi film.

**Arjun:** It is real, just rare. And just to flip the rule the other way — diamond is a form of carbon, which is a non-metal. But diamond is the hardest naturally occurring substance on Earth.

**Meera:** Wait. So a non-metal can be harder than a metal?

**Arjun:** Sometimes, yes. And remember graphite from the electricity chapter — also a form of carbon, also a non-metal, but it conducts electricity through pencil lead.

**Meera:** So science keeps these exceptions on purpose just to confuse us during exams.

**Arjun:** Haha. Not to confuse. To remind us that nature is messier and more creative than any simple rule. The patterns help us guess, but the exceptions keep us humble.

**Meera:** Humble and squishy. Like sodium.

**Arjun:** Yaar, please don't put that in your project.

## Non-Metals: Quiet Heroes of Life

**Meera:** Arjun, metals seem so dramatic — bridges, vehicles, jewellery, the gold mummy locks away in the cupboard. Are non-metals even close in importance?

**Arjun:** Without non-metals, there is literally no life. The oxygen you're breathing right this second — non-metal. Every cell in your body is built around carbon — another non-metal. The nitrogen in the urea fertiliser that farmers around Pune use to grow wheat — non-metal.

**Meera:** And the chlorine in our tap water?

**Arjun:** Also a non-metal. The municipal water department adds tiny amounts to kill harmful bacteria so we don't fall sick. And iodine — that orange-brown antiseptic mummy dabs on your scraped knee — non-metal.

**Meera:** So metals build the world but non-metals keep us alive.

**Arjun:** Beautifully put. And one more thing to remember — metals and non-metals are categories of elements. Elements are pure substances, can't be broken down further. Things like plastic, glass, rubber — those aren't elements. They are mixtures made from combinations of different elements.

**Meera:** How many elements are there in total?

**Arjun:** 118 known so far. Every single object you see — your phone, the tawa, your school bag, the ceiling fan, even the dahi in the fridge — is just some combination of those 118.

**Meera:** From the iron in our tawa to the oxygen we breathe — it is all just elements, mixing and matching.

**Arjun:** That's the magic of chemistry. Understanding what each element does helps us pick the right material for the right job. Iron for tawas. Copper for wires. Rubber for tyres. Oxygen for breathing. Each one chosen because of what it is good at.

**Meera:** And what it is good at depends on whether it's a metal, a non-metal, or one of those weird exceptions.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Now come, daadi is calling. She's tapping the steel tumbler again — must be checking the oil.

**Meera:** Sonority in action.
