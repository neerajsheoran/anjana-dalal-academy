## Why Metals Rule the Kitchen

**Child:** Maa, why is our tawa made of iron but its handle is wrapped in wood?

**Mother:** Because iron conducts heat beautifully -- the flame's energy travels through the tawa quickly and evenly, cooking your roti perfectly. But if the handle were also iron, you would burn your fingers the moment you touched it! Wood is a poor conductor of heat, so it stays cool even when the tawa is blazing hot.

**Child:** So metals are good at passing heat along, and non-metals are not?

**Mother:** That is one of the key differences. But metals have many other special properties. They are shiny -- that gleam on your new steel lunchbox is called metallic lustre. They are malleable -- they can be beaten into thin sheets, like the aluminium foil you use to pack your lunch. They are ductile -- they can be pulled into thin wires, like the copper wiring in our house. And they are sonorous -- they ring when struck.

**Child:** That is why Maa uses a steel glass to check if the oil is hot enough -- she taps it and listens for the ring!

**Mother:** And that is why mandir bells, ghungroos, and manjiras are all made from metal. Non-metals like charcoal, sulfur, and wood cannot produce that ringing sound. They are dull, brittle, and crack instead of bending. Each material has its strengths, and understanding those strengths is what this chapter is all about.

## The Battle Against Rust

**Child:** Maa, the iron railing on our balcony has become brown and rough. Bauji said it is rusting. What exactly is rust?

**Mother:** Rust is what happens when iron reacts with both oxygen from the air and moisture from water vapour. The brown, flaky coating you see is iron oxide -- a completely new substance formed on the iron's surface.

**Child:** Does iron always rust?

**Mother:** Only when both air and water are present together. Scientists tested this with three sealed bottles. An iron nail in dry air did not rust. A nail in water with no air access did not rust. But a nail in moist air -- where both oxygen and water vapour reached it -- rusted within days.

**Child:** So keeping iron dry prevents rusting?

**Mother:** Yes, that is one approach. Painting iron creates a barrier against air and moisture. Applying oil or grease does the same. And then there is galvanisation -- coating iron with a thin layer of zinc metal. The zinc shields the iron from direct contact with the environment.

**Child:** What about that famous iron pillar in Delhi? It is hundreds of years old and has not rusted!

**Mother:** Over 1600 years old! Built during Chandragupta II's reign. Ancient Indian metalworkers created iron of such remarkable purity and composition that it naturally resists corrosion. It is a testament to the advanced metallurgical knowledge of that era. And corrosion is not just an iron problem -- copper develops green patches and silver turns black over time.

## When Metals and Non-Metals Burn

**Child:** Maa, what happens when you burn a metal in air?

**Mother:** Metals combine with oxygen to form metal oxides, which are basic in nature. Take magnesium, for example. A magnesium ribbon burns with a dazzling white flame so bright you should not look directly at it. The white powder left behind is magnesium oxide. Dissolve it in water and test with litmus -- red litmus turns blue, proving it is basic.

**Child:** And non-metals?

**Mother:** Non-metals produce oxides that are acidic -- the opposite! When sulfur burns, it forms sulfur dioxide gas with a sharp, unpleasant smell. Dissolve that gas in water and you get sulfurous acid, which turns blue litmus red.

**Child:** So metal oxides are basic and non-metal oxides are acidic. That connects to what we learned about acids and bases!

**Mother:** Exactly! And this connection explains some practical things too. Sodium is a metal so reactive that it catches fire if exposed to air or water. That is why it must be stored submerged in kerosene. Phosphorus, a non-metal, is equally dangerous -- it bursts into flame in open air, so it is stored underwater. Every element has its own personality!

## Metals That Do Not Follow the Rules

**Child:** Maa, are all metals hard and solid?

**Mother:** Most are, but nature loves exceptions. Sodium and potassium are metals so soft you can slice them with a butter knife. And mercury is the only metal that exists as a liquid at room temperature -- that shiny silver liquid in old-style thermometers is mercury.

**Child:** And are all non-metals soft and dull?

**Mother:** Generally, yes. But diamond -- which is a form of carbon, a non-metal -- is the hardest naturally occurring substance known! And graphite, another form of carbon, conducts electricity even though it is not a metal. You encountered graphite in the electricity chapter when pencil lead made the bulb glow.

**Child:** So there are always exceptions in science!

**Mother:** Always. The general patterns help us predict and understand, but the exceptions are what keep science interesting. They remind us that nature is far more complex and creative than any simple rule can capture.

## Non-Metals: Quiet Heroes of Life

**Child:** Metals seem so important -- buildings, vehicles, jewellery, tools. Are non-metals equally important?

**Mother:** Without non-metals, there would be no life at all. The oxygen you are breathing right now is a non-metal. Every cell in your body is built around carbon -- another non-metal. The nitrogen in urea fertiliser helps farmers across India grow enough food to feed over a hundred crore people. Chlorine is mixed into our tap water to kill harmful germs. And iodine is the antiseptic Maa dabs on your scrapes and cuts.

**Child:** So metals give us infrastructure, but non-metals keep us alive?

**Mother:** Beautifully put. And remember -- metals and non-metals are types of elements, meaning they are pure substances that cannot be broken down further. Materials like plastic, glass, and rubber are not elements -- they are made from combinations of different elements. There are 118 known elements in all, and every object you see around you is made from some combination of them.

**Child:** From the iron in our tawa to the oxygen we breathe -- it is all just elements combining in different ways!

**Mother:** And that is the magic of chemistry. Understanding the properties of these elements helps us pick exactly the right material for every purpose -- strong metals for bridges, flexible rubber for tyres, and life-sustaining oxygen for our lungs.
