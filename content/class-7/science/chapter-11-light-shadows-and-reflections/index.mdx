## Introduction

Close your eyes for a moment. Everything goes dark. Now open them -- instantly, the room appears around you, full of colours, shapes, and details. Light makes all of this possible. Without it, we would see nothing at all.

From the blazing Sun to a tiny diya on Diwali night, sources of light fill our world. In this chapter, we will investigate how light moves, why shadows form, how mirrors create images, and how clever devices like pinhole cameras and periscopes put the science of light to practical use.

---

## Sources of Light

### Luminous and Non-luminous Objects

![Sources of light — luminous and non-luminous objects](sources-of-light-luminous-and-non-luminous-objects.png)

Objects that **produce and give off their own light** are called **luminous objects**.

Examples of luminous objects:
- **Natural sources:** The Sun, twinkling stars, a flash of lightning, glowing fireflies (jugnu)
- **Artificial sources:** Electric bulbs, tube lights, LED lamps, burning candles, mobile phone screens

Objects that **cannot produce their own light** are called **non-luminous objects**. Think of the Moon, a cricket bat, your textbook, or a dining table. We can see them only because light from a luminous source falls on them and bounces into our eyes.

The **Moon** is the most familiar non-luminous object. It lights up the night sky not because it generates light, but because it **reflects** the sunlight falling on it.

> **Key idea:** **Luminous objects** generate their own light (Sun, stars, bulbs). **Non-luminous objects** do not generate light but become visible when they reflect light from a luminous source.

### LED Lamps

**LED (Light Emitting Diode) lamps** have transformed lighting in India. They consume far less electricity than traditional incandescent bulbs, produce brighter light, and last many times longer. The widespread adoption of LEDs through government initiatives has helped save enormous amounts of electricity across the country.

## Light Travels in Straight Lines

One of the most fundamental properties of light is that it **moves only in straight lines**. Scientists call this the **rectilinear propagation of light**.

### Activity 1: Aligning the Holes

![Light travels in straight lines — activity 1, aligning the holes](light-travels-in-straight-lines-activity-1-aligning-the-holes.png)

1. Take three pieces of thick cardboard and punch a small hole at the same spot on each one.
2. Stand them upright in a line on a table so that all three holes are perfectly aligned.
3. Shine a torch through the first hole. A bright spot of light appears on the wall behind the third cardboard -- light has passed straight through all three holes.
4. Now slide the middle cardboard slightly to the left. The bright spot vanishes instantly.

**Why?** Light cannot curve or bend around the shifted cardboard. It only travels in a straight line, so misaligning even one hole blocks the path completely.

### Activity 2: Straight Tube vs Curved Tube

![Light travels in straight lines — activity 2, straight tube vs curved tube](light-travels-in-straight-lines-activity-2-straight-tube-vs-curved-tube.png)

1. Look at a glowing candle through a straight, narrow tube -- you can see the flame clearly.
2. Now try looking through a flexible tube that you have bent into a curve -- the flame is invisible.

**Why?** Light cannot follow the curved path inside the bent tube. It only travels along straight paths.

> **Key idea:** Light always travels in **straight lines**. This property explains why we cannot see around corners and why shadows have well-defined edges.

## Transparent, Translucent, and Opaque Materials

![Transparent, translucent, and opaque materials — light interacting with each](transparent-translucent-and-opaque-materials.png)

When light strikes an object, one of three things happens depending on the material:

| Material Type | What Happens to Light | Everyday Examples |
|--------------|----------------------|-------------------|
| **Transparent** | Light passes through almost entirely; objects behind are clearly visible | Clear glass windowpane, pure water, clean air |
| **Translucent** | Only some light passes through; objects behind appear blurry | Frosted bathroom glass, thin dupatta, oiled paper |
| **Opaque** | No light passes through; objects behind are invisible | Brick wall, wooden door, iron sheet, thick textbook |

> **Key idea:** **Transparent** materials allow light through completely. **Translucent** materials allow partial light. **Opaque** materials block light entirely.

## Shadow Formation

### How Shadows Are Born

Since light travels in straight lines, when an **opaque object** stands in its path, the light is blocked. Behind the object, there is a region that receives no light -- this dark region is the **shadow**.

![Shadow formation — how shadows are born](shadow-formation-how-shadows-are-born.png)

Three things must be present for a shadow to form:
1. A **light source** (torch, Sun, lamp)
2. An **opaque object** (anything that blocks light)
3. A **screen** (wall, floor, white sheet -- any surface where the shadow can be seen)

### Interesting Properties of Shadows

Experimenting with shadows reveals several fascinating facts:

- **Switch off the light, lose the shadow:** No light source means no shadow.
- **Remove the object, lose the shadow:** Nothing to block the light means nothing to cast a shadow.
- **No screen, no visible shadow:** Without a surface, there is nowhere for the shadow to appear.
- **Position affects size:** Moving the object closer to the light source makes the shadow **bigger**. Moving it closer to the screen makes it **smaller** and sharper.
- **Angle affects shape:** Tilting the object changes the shape of its shadow dramatically.
- **Colour is irrelevant:** Whether the object is red, green, yellow, or blue, its shadow is always **dark**. Shadows have no colour because they are simply regions where light is absent.

**What about non-opaque objects?**

![Shadow formation — what about non-opaque objects (opaque vs translucent vs transparent)](shadow-formation-what-about-non-opaque-objects.png)

- Opaque objects produce the **darkest**, sharpest shadows
- Translucent objects produce **faint, lighter** shadows
- Transparent objects may produce extremely faint shadows or none at all

> **Key idea:** A shadow forms when an opaque object blocks light. Its size and shape depend on the positions of the light source, object, and screen. The object's colour does not affect the shadow's appearance.

### Shadow Puppetry -- India's Ancient Light Art

Shadow puppetry is one of India's oldest performing arts, using the science of shadows for storytelling. Flat figures made of leather or cardboard are held between a bright lamp and a white cloth screen. As the puppeteer moves them, their shadows dance and tell stories from the Ramayana, Mahabharata, and local folklore.

Celebrated regional traditions include:
- **Tholu Bommalata** (Andhra Pradesh)
- **Togalu Gombeyaata** (Karnataka)
- **Tholpavakoothu** (Kerala)
- **Ravana Chhaya** (Odisha)
- **Charma Bahuli Natya** (Maharashtra)
- **Bommalattam** (Tamil Nadu)

## Reflection of Light

### What is Reflection?

![Reflection of light — what is reflection](reflection-of-light-what-is-reflection.png)

When light strikes a **smooth, shiny surface** such as a mirror, polished steel, or still water, it **bounces back** in a definite direction. This redirection of light by a surface is called **reflection**.

### Activity: Steering Sunlight

![Reflection of light — activity, steering sunlight](reflection-of-light-activity-steering-sunlight.png)

1. Take a small plane mirror outdoors on a sunny day.
2. Angle the mirror so sunlight falls on its surface.
3. Tilt the mirror gently -- you can direct a bright spot of light onto a wall, a friend's notebook, or even the ceiling of a corridor.
4. Changing the tilt moves the bright spot around.

**Conclusion:** The mirror changes the direction of the light beam. This is reflection in action.

### Seeing the Light Path Change

![Reflection of light — seeing the light path change](reflection-of-light-seeing-the-light-path-change.png)

1. Cover all the teeth of a comb except one gap to create a narrow slit.
2. Shine a torch through the slit onto a white paper to create a thin line of light.
3. Place a mirror in the light beam's path at an angle.
4. You can clearly observe that the beam **changes direction** after hitting the mirror surface.

> **Key idea:** **Reflection** is the bouncing back of light from a shiny surface. Mirrors redirect light and change its direction.

## Images in a Plane Mirror

When you stand before a flat mirror (a **plane mirror**), you see a clear likeness of yourself. This likeness is called an **image**.

![Images in a plane mirror — object and its mirror image](images-in-a-plane-mirror.png)

### Properties of a Plane Mirror Image

![Images in a plane mirror — properties of a plane mirror image](images-in-a-plane-mirror-properties-of-a-plane-mirror-image.png)

| Property | Description |
|----------|------------|
| **Size** | The image is **exactly the same size** as the object |
| **Orientation** | The image is **upright** (not flipped upside down) |
| **Distance** | The image appears to be the **same distance** behind the mirror as the object is in front |
| **Screen** | The image **cannot be captured on a screen** -- you can only see it by looking into the mirror |
| **Lateral inversion** | Left and right are **swapped** in the image |

### Lateral Inversion

![Images in a plane mirror — lateral inversion (left-right swap)](images-in-a-plane-mirror-lateral-inversion-1.png)

![Images in a plane mirror — lateral inversion (AMBULANCE example)](images-in-a-plane-mirror-lateral-inversion-2.png)

Hold up your **right** hand in front of a mirror. Your image appears to hold up its **left** hand. Touch your **left** ear, and the image seems to touch its **right** ear. This left-right swap is called **lateral inversion**.

A brilliant practical application: the word **AMBULANCE** is painted in **mirror-reversed letters** on the front of ambulances. When a driver ahead sees it in their rear-view mirror, the reversed letters flip back to normal -- reading correctly as "AMBULANCE" -- alerting them to move aside quickly.

> **Key idea:** A plane mirror creates an image that is the same size as the object, upright, and laterally inverted (left and right are exchanged). This image cannot be caught on a screen.

## The Pinhole Camera

A **pinhole camera** is a delightfully simple device that produces an image of an object using nothing more than a tiny hole.

![The pinhole camera — light rays crossing through the pinhole to form an inverted image](the-pinhole-camera.png)

### How It Works

1. Light rays from the top of a bright object travel in a straight line through the pinhole and land on the **bottom** of the screen inside the camera.
2. Light rays from the bottom of the object pass through the same pinhole and land on the **top** of the screen.
3. Because the rays cross at the pinhole, the image formed on the screen is **upside down** (inverted).

### Build Your Own Pinhole Camera

![The pinhole camera — build your own pinhole camera](the-pinhole-camera-build-your-own-pinhole-camera.png)

1. Find two cardboard boxes -- one slightly smaller so it slides snugly inside the larger one.
2. Poke a tiny pinhole in the centre of one face of the larger box.
3. On the opposite face of the smaller box, cut a square window and tape a piece of tracing paper (or butter paper) over it -- this is your screen.
4. Slide the smaller box into the larger one, with the tracing paper facing inward.
5. Point the pinhole at a well-lit scene -- a tree in sunlight or a lit lamp.
6. Peek through the open end of the smaller box. You will see an **inverted, full-colour** image on the tracing paper!

### Pinhole Camera vs Plane Mirror

| Feature | Pinhole Camera | Plane Mirror |
|---------|---------------|-------------|
| Image orientation | Inverted (upside down) | Erect (right-side up) |
| Lateral inversion | No | Yes |
| Image on screen | Yes (real image) | No (virtual image) |

> **Key idea:** A **pinhole camera** produces an **inverted** image on a screen because light rays travelling in straight lines cross at the pinhole.

## Useful Devices Based on Light

### Periscope

A **periscope** is a device that uses two plane mirrors mounted at angles inside a Z-shaped tube. It lets you see objects that are not in your direct line of sight.

![Useful devices based on light — periscope with two mirrors in a Z-shaped tube](useful-devices-based-on-light-periscope.png)

How it works:
1. Light from a distant object enters the top of the tube and hits the **upper mirror**, which reflects it downward.
2. The reflected light travels down the tube and hits the **lower mirror**, which reflects it horizontally into your eyes.

**Where periscopes are used:**
- In **submarines** -- the crew can observe the ocean surface while remaining safely below water
- In **military bunkers and armoured vehicles** -- soldiers can see outside without exposing themselves to danger
- At **parades and crowded events** -- people in the back rows can see over taller heads

### Kaleidoscope

![Useful devices based on light — kaleidoscope with three mirror strips](useful-devices-based-on-light-kaleidoscope.png)

A **kaleidoscope** is a tube containing three narrow mirror strips arranged in a triangular formation. A handful of coloured glass beads or broken bangle pieces are placed at one end. When you peer through the other end and rotate the tube, the multiple reflections between the three mirrors create **stunning, ever-changing symmetrical patterns**. Textile designers and rangoli artists sometimes use kaleidoscopes for pattern inspiration.

## Summary

- **Luminous objects** produce their own light (Sun, stars, lamps). **Non-luminous objects** do not (Moon, furniture, books).
- Light travels in **straight lines** -- a property called rectilinear propagation.
- **Transparent** materials let light through completely. **Translucent** materials let some through. **Opaque** materials block it entirely.
- A **shadow** forms when an opaque object blocks light. Three requirements: a light source, an opaque object, and a screen.
- Shadow size and shape depend on the positions of the source, object, and screen. The object's colour does not affect the shadow.
- **Reflection** is the bouncing back of light from a shiny surface.
- A **plane mirror** produces an image that is same-sized, upright, laterally inverted, and cannot be captured on a screen.
- **Lateral inversion** (left-right swap) is why AMBULANCE is written in reverse on emergency vehicles.
- A **pinhole camera** forms an inverted image on a screen, demonstrating that light travels in straight lines.
- **Periscopes** use two mirrors to see objects around corners or over obstacles. **Kaleidoscopes** use three mirrors to create beautiful symmetrical patterns.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Diwali diyas and the dancing shadows**: On Diwali night, watch a single diya placed on the floor of a dark courtyard. Move your hand near it and a giant wobbly shadow of your fingers leaps onto the wall. Move your hand close to the wall and the shadow shrinks and sharpens. Same opaque-object + point-source physics — but turned into a festival memory. The flickering flame gives the shadow its restless edge, because the source itself never stays still.

**Tholu Bommalata — India's ancient shadow cinema**: Long before films, families in Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, and Kerala gathered at night to watch leather puppets cast shadows on a white sheet, lit by a single oil lamp behind them. Tholu Bommalata, Togalu Gombeyaata, and Tholpavakoothu still tell Ramayana and Mahabharata stories using exactly your chapter's physics: opaque puppet + light source + screen = story. The puppeteers add colour using translucent leather patches — the same principle as frosted glass.

**Why ambulances paint AMBULANCE backwards**: Look at the front of the next ambulance that passes. The word is mirror-reversed. When a driver ahead glances in their rear-view mirror, lateral inversion flips the letters back to normal — they read "AMBULANCE" instantly and pull aside. The same physics that confuses your reflection's left and right is literally saving lives on Indian roads every day.

### Try it at home -- Build a Pinhole Camera

*⏱ 30-40 min setup, 10 min viewing · 🧹 low mess (some cardboard scraps) · 👨‍👩‍👧 adult help only if cutting with a craft knife*

**What you'll need:**

- A small empty shoebox or rectangular cardboard box (a chappal box works well)
- A sharp pin or thin needle
- A small square of **tracing paper** (or thin butter paper / wax paper)
- Cello tape
- Scissors or a craft knife
- A dark cloth or thick towel
- Your wonder notebook

**Setup -- one face becomes the pinhole, the opposite face becomes the screen:**

1. Stand the box on its long side so you have a tall narrow rectangle.
2. On one **short end** of the box, make a small clean hole in the centre using the pin. The hole should be about the size of a normal pen nib's dot. **Smaller is sharper.**
3. On the **opposite short end**, cut a square window of about 5×5 cm.
4. Tape the tracing paper over this window from the inside. This is your screen.
5. Cover the lid and any gaps with tape so no stray light gets in. The only light should enter through the pinhole.

**Steps:**

1. Take your pinhole camera to a sunny outdoor scene -- a tree, a bright building, a parked car under sunlight.
2. Point the **pinhole side toward the scene**.
3. Hold the **screen side toward yourself** and cover your head and the screen with the dark cloth (so no other light reaches the tracing paper).
4. Wait a moment for your eyes to adjust.
5. Look at the screen.

**What you should see:**

- A faint but **clear, full-colour image of the scene**, **upside down**.
- Move closer to or farther from the scene to focus the image.

**Try this variation**: Make a second pinhole right next to the first. You will see **two overlapping images**. Cover one and the second vanishes. Each pinhole creates its own image because each ray of light travels in its own perfect straight line.

**A tip**: The smaller and rounder your pinhole, the sharper the image. Larger holes let through more light but blur the picture -- the same trade-off photographers manage with camera apertures.

**Show what you found:** Snap a photo of the upside-down image on your screen. Sketch the setup in your wonder notebook. Show your daadi (or dadu, mummy, older cousin) — and ask them to guess why the tree appears upside down before you explain. Tell them this is the same principle their eyes have been using their whole life without them realising.

**A question to keep asking all year:** *Every time I see something glow, shine, sparkle, or cast a shadow — what is the light actually doing, and what is it telling me?*
