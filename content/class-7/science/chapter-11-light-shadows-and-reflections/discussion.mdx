## Why Light Goes Only Straight

**Child:** Maa, why can I not see what is happening around the corner of our hallway?

**Mother:** Because light only travels in straight lines, beta. This property has a fancy name -- rectilinear propagation. Try this: take three postcards, punch a hole in each at the same spot, and line them up on the dining table. Shine a torch through the first hole. If all three holes are perfectly aligned, the light passes straight through to the wall. But shift any one postcard even slightly and the light vanishes.

**Child:** So light cannot curve around anything?

**Mother:** Not on its own, no. That is also why shadows have such sharp, clean edges. When an opaque object blocks the straight-line path of light, a dark region forms behind it -- the shadow. For a shadow to exist, you need three things: a light source, an opaque object, and a surface for the shadow to fall on.

**Child:** Can I make a shadow bigger or smaller?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Move the object closer to the torch and the shadow grows larger. Move it closer to the wall and the shadow shrinks. But no matter what colour your object is -- red, blue, yellow -- the shadow is always dark. Shadows simply do not have colour because they are areas where light does not reach.

## How Materials React to Light

**Child:** Maa, I can see clearly through our glass window, but through the frosted glass of the bathroom I can only see blurry shapes, and through the wooden door I cannot see anything at all. Why?

**Mother:** Those are the three categories of materials based on how they treat light. Clear glass is transparent -- light passes through completely, so you see everything clearly. Frosted glass is translucent -- it lets some light through but scatters it, so shapes look blurry. And the wooden door is opaque -- it blocks all light, so you cannot see through it at all.

**Child:** So only opaque things make proper dark shadows?

**Mother:** Opaque objects make the darkest, crispest shadows. Translucent objects create faint, lighter shadows. And truly transparent objects may cast no visible shadow at all. This is exactly why we choose different materials for different purposes -- opaque curtains for bedrooms when you want to block light, translucent curtains for the living room when you want soft light and privacy, and transparent glass for windows when you want full sunlight and a clear view.

**Child:** I never thought about it that way -- choosing curtains is actually applied science!

**Mother:** Everything around us uses science, beta. Architects think carefully about which materials to use for every window, wall, and door based on exactly these properties.

## Mirrors and the Mystery of Left-Right Swap

**Child:** Maa, when I wave my right hand in front of the mirror, my reflection seems to wave its left hand. Why does the mirror swap left and right?

**Mother:** That is called lateral inversion. A plane mirror -- a flat mirror -- creates an image that is the same size as you, standing upright, and at the same distance behind the mirror as you are in front of it. But left and right get exchanged. Your right becomes the image's left, and vice versa.

**Child:** Is that why AMBULANCE is written backwards on the front of ambulances?

**Mother:** Exactly! When a car driver checks their rear-view mirror, the mirror performs lateral inversion on those reversed letters, flipping them back to normal. So the driver reads "AMBULANCE" correctly and knows to give way. Clever, no?

**Child:** Very clever! Can a mirror image be caught on a screen, like a photo?

**Mother:** No, that is another important property. A plane mirror image is what scientists call a virtual image -- it appears to exist behind the mirror, but you can never project it onto a wall or paper. You can only see it by looking into the mirror itself.

## The Upside-Down World of the Pinhole Camera

**Child:** Maa, I made a pinhole camera from a shoebox for my science project. The image on the butter paper screen is upside down! Did I do something wrong?

**Mother:** Not at all -- that is exactly how it should work! Since light travels in straight lines, rays from the top of an object pass through the tiny pinhole and travel downward, landing at the bottom of the screen. Rays from the bottom travel upward to the top. The rays cross each other at the pinhole, flipping the image upside down.

**Child:** So a pinhole camera gives an inverted image while a mirror gives an upright one?

**Mother:** Correct. And there is another difference -- a mirror image is laterally inverted (left-right swapped) but a pinhole camera image is not. Also, the pinhole camera image appears on the screen -- you can actually see it projected there. The mirror image exists only when you look into the mirror. You know what is amazing? Your own eyes work exactly like a pinhole camera! Light enters through the pupil -- a tiny opening -- and forms an inverted image on the retina at the back of your eye. Your brain flips it right-side up so you perceive everything correctly.

**Child:** Our eyes are like tiny pinhole cameras? That is incredible!

## Periscopes, Kaleidoscopes, and Shadow Art

**Child:** Maa, in movies I have seen submarine crews looking through a long tube to see above the water. What is that device?

**Mother:** That is a periscope. It has two flat mirrors arranged at angles inside a Z-shaped tube. Light from above the water hits the top mirror, bounces downward, hits the bottom mirror, and bounces horizontally into the observer's eyes. Two reflections redirect the light so you can see something that is not in your direct line of sight.

**Child:** So periscopes use reflection to let you see around obstacles?

**Mother:** Yes. They are used in submarines, military tanks, and even by spectators trying to see a procession over a tall crowd. And kaleidoscopes use three mirrors arranged in a triangle to create gorgeous, symmetrical patterns from a few coloured beads. Every rotation produces a new pattern through multiple reflections.

**Child:** India also has traditional shadow puppet shows, right, Maa?

**Mother:** Beautiful ones! Tholu Bommalata in Andhra Pradesh, Togalu Gombeyaata in Karnataka, and Tholpavakoothu in Kerala are centuries-old art forms. Flat leather puppets are held between a bright oil lamp and a white cloth screen. The shadows of the puppets on the screen tell stories from epics like the Ramayana. It is science and storytelling woven together -- the straight-line travel of light, shadow formation, and the artistry of the puppeteer all combining into something magical.
