## Why Light Goes Only Straight

**Meera:** Arjun, yaar, our hallway is so weird. I can hear Daadi singing in the kitchen but I cannot see her until I actually reach the corner. Sound bends around the corner, light does not. Why?

**Arjun:** Because light only travels in straight lines. Sound waves spread in all directions, but light just goes — bam — forward.

**Meera:** Wait, really? Light has a fancy name for being stubborn like this?

**Arjun:** Rectilinear propagation. I memorised it for last week's surprise test. Try this — take three postcards, punch the same hole in each, line them up on the dining table, shine a torch through the first one. If all three holes are perfectly aligned, the light goes straight through to the wall.

**Meera:** And the moment I nudge one card sideways?

**Arjun:** The light vanishes. Doesn't bend around your shifted card. That is also why shadows have such sharp edges. An opaque thing blocks the straight-line path, and behind it — dark patch. Shadow.

**Meera:** So three things needed for a shadow — light, opaque object, and a screen or wall to fall on?

**Arjun:** Exactly. And here is something funny — move the object closer to the torch, shadow gets bigger. Move it closer to the wall, shadow shrinks.

**Meera:** Like how my Diwali diya throws this huge wobbly shadow of my hand on the wall when I bring my hand close to the flame.

**Arjun:** Same physics. And no matter what colour the object is — red, blue, your bright pink dupatta — the shadow is always just dark. Shadows have no colour because they are basically "no light here" zones.

## How Materials React to Light

**Meera:** Arjun, I noticed something at home this morning. Through our living room window I see the whole street. Through the bathroom's frosted glass I only see blurry shapes of the neem tree. And through the wooden door — nothing. Why three different results?

**Arjun:** Three different categories of material. Clear glass is transparent — light passes through completely, so you see everything. Frosted glass is translucent — some light gets through but scatters, so shapes look blurry. Wooden door is opaque — blocks everything.

**Meera:** So only opaque things make proper dark shadows?

**Arjun:** Opaque makes the darkest, crispest shadows. Translucent makes faint lighter ones. And transparent stuff — like clean glass — might cast almost no shadow at all.

**Meera:** Now this is making me realise — Mummy chose thick cotton curtains for the bedrooms but light voile ones for the drawing room. That is exactly this principle!

**Arjun:** Total applied science. The architect who designed Daadi's old house in our mohalla probably thought about which window gets opaque shutter, which gets translucent glass, which gets clear pane.

**Meera:** Choosing curtains is physics homework. I will tell Mummy that when she asks why I keep staring at the window.

## Mirrors and the Mystery of Left-Right Swap

**Meera:** Arjun, every time I wave my right hand in front of the bathroom mirror, my reflection waves its left hand. Why does the mirror swap my hands?

**Arjun:** It is called lateral inversion. A plane mirror — a flat one — makes an image the same size as you, standing upright, at the same distance behind the mirror as you are in front. But left and right get exchanged.

**Meera:** Oh! So that is why the word AMBULANCE is written backwards on the front of ambulances?

**Arjun:** Bingo. The driver ahead checks their rear-view mirror, which performs lateral inversion on those already-reversed letters — flips them back to normal. Driver reads "AMBULANCE" correctly and pulls over.

**Meera:** That is so smart. The same effect that confuses my reflection literally saves lives.

**Arjun:** Yaar, one more thing — can a mirror image ever be caught on a screen like a photo?

**Meera:** Hmm. No? It always lives inside the mirror. I cannot project it onto a piece of paper.

**Arjun:** Correct. Scientists call it a virtual image. Appears to exist behind the mirror, but you can never project it. You can only see it by looking into the mirror itself.

## The Upside-Down World of the Pinhole Camera

**Meera:** Arjun, I made a pinhole camera from a chappal box for my science project. The image on the butter paper is upside down! Did I mess it up?

**Arjun:** No, no, that is exactly how it should look. Light travels in straight lines, remember? Rays from the top of the tree pass through the tiny pinhole and travel downward, landing at the bottom of your screen. Rays from the bottom travel up to the top.

**Meera:** So they cross at the pinhole, like an X?

**Arjun:** Exactly. The crossing is what flips the image. Same principle as how your eye works actually.

**Meera:** Wait, what?

**Arjun:** Light enters your eye through the pupil — a tiny opening — and forms an inverted image on the retina at the back. Your brain quietly flips it right-side up so the world looks normal to you.

**Meera:** Our eyes are tiny pinhole cameras with a built-in image-flipping software in the brain? That is wild.

**Arjun:** And here is the difference from a mirror — mirror image is upright but laterally inverted. Pinhole camera image is upside down but NOT laterally inverted. And the pinhole image actually exists on the screen, you can photograph it. The mirror image lives only when you are looking into the mirror.

**Meera:** Two different physics tricks, two different kinds of images. I love this.

## Periscopes, Kaleidoscopes, and Shadow Art

**Meera:** Arjun, in submarine movies they look through this long tube to see above the water. What is that called?

**Arjun:** A periscope. Two flat mirrors arranged at angles inside a Z-shaped tube. Light from above the water hits the top mirror, bounces down, hits the bottom mirror, bounces horizontally into the observer's eyes.

**Meera:** Two reflections to see around a corner. Could I use one to peek into Dadu's room without him knowing?

**Arjun:** Technically yes. Spectators at a procession use them to see over taller crowds. INS Arihant, our nuclear submarine, uses one to scan the surface of the Bay of Bengal without revealing its position.

**Meera:** And kaleidoscopes? My cousin gave me one last Diwali. The patterns inside it are gorgeous.

**Arjun:** Three mirrors arranged in a triangle. Few coloured beads or broken bangle pieces at one end. Rotate the tube, the multiple reflections create new symmetrical patterns every time. My mausi designs textiles — she literally uses a kaleidoscope for inspiration.

**Meera:** Yaar, India also has those traditional shadow puppet shows, right?

**Arjun:** Beautiful ones. Tholu Bommalata in Andhra Pradesh, Togalu Gombeyaata in Karnataka, Tholpavakoothu in Kerala. Flat leather puppets held between a bright oil lamp and a white cloth screen. Shadows tell stories from the Ramayana, Mahabharata, local folklore.

**Meera:** So it is the same opaque-object-plus-light-source-plus-screen physics we just learned about, but turned into centuries-old art.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Science and storytelling, stitched together. Now come — Daadi made aloo paratha and the shadows of our hands are getting hungrier by the minute.

**Meera:** Last one. Why is the shadow of my hand on the tawa fuzzy at the edges?

**Arjun:** Because the gas flame is not a perfect point of light. We will figure that one out over breakfast.
