## Introduction

![Introduction — life processes in animals](introduction.png)

Every living creature on Earth -- from a tiny ant carrying a grain of sugar to a massive elephant pulling down a tree branch -- needs energy to survive. This energy comes from **food**. But the roti, rice, dal, and vegetables on your thali contain complex substances like carbohydrates, proteins, and fats. Your body cannot use these directly. They must be broken down into much simpler molecules before your cells can absorb and use them.

The processes that keep animals alive -- **nutrition**, **respiration**, **circulation**, **excretion**, and **reproduction** -- are collectively called **life processes**. In this chapter, we will focus on how the human body digests food and how it uses oxygen to release energy from that food.

> **Key idea:** Animals get energy from the food they eat. Complex food molecules must be broken down into simpler forms through **digestion** before the body can use them for energy.

## The Human Digestive System

Food is processed inside a long, continuous tube called the **alimentary canal** (also called the digestive tract). This tube begins at the **mouth** and ends at the **anus**, with several specialised sections in between.

![The human digestive system — labelled diagram of the alimentary canal](the-human-digestive-system.png)

The major parts of the digestive system are:
1. Mouth
2. Oesophagus (food pipe)
3. Stomach
4. Small intestine
5. Large intestine
6. Rectum and Anus

Two important organs that assist in digestion but are not part of the main tube are the **liver** and the **pancreas**.

---

### The Mouth

![The mouth — teeth, saliva, and tongue starting digestion](the-mouth.png)

Everything begins here. When you take a bite of paratha:

**Teeth** crush and grind the food into tiny pieces. This physical breakdown is called **mechanical digestion**.

**Saliva**, produced by glands inside the mouth, does two things:
- It **wets** the food, turning it into a soft mass that slides easily down the food pipe.
- It contains a chemical substance that **converts starch into sugar**.

**Try this at home:** Place a small piece of plain roti in your mouth and chew it slowly for about a minute without swallowing. Initially it tastes bland, but gradually you will notice a distinctly **sweet** flavour. That sweetness appears because saliva is converting the starch in the roti into sugar right inside your mouth.

The **tongue** rolls the chewed food around, mixing it thoroughly with saliva, and then pushes the soft lump towards the food pipe.

> **Key idea:** Digestion starts in the mouth. Teeth break food physically (mechanical digestion) while saliva begins the chemical breakdown of starch into sugar (chemical digestion).

**Oral care tip:** Brush your teeth and scrape your tongue every morning and night. Rinse your mouth with water after every meal to wash away food particles that cause decay.

---

### The Oesophagus (Food Pipe)

![The oesophagus — food pipe with wave-like muscular action](the-oesophagus-food-pipe.png)

The oesophagus is a muscular tube connecting the mouth to the stomach. Its walls perform rhythmic **squeeze-and-release movements** that push the food ball steadily downward, much like squeezing toothpaste through a tube. This wave-like muscular action continues throughout the alimentary canal.

---

### The Stomach

![The stomach — churning food with acid and digestive juice](the-stomach.png)

The stomach is a thick-walled, bag-like organ where food undergoes intense processing:

- Its muscular walls **churn and squeeze** the food, mixing it thoroughly -- like a mixer-grinder.
- The inner lining releases three important substances:
  - **Digestive juice** -- attacks proteins and begins breaking them into smaller fragments
  - **Hydrochloric acid** -- assists in protein breakdown and also kills harmful germs that enter with food
  - **Mucus** -- coats the stomach wall to shield it from the acid's corrosive action

After being processed in the stomach, the food becomes a thick, soupy, **semi-liquid paste**, ready for the next stage.

---

### The Small Intestine

![The small intestine — finger-like projections absorbing nutrients](the-small-intestine.png)

Despite its name, the small intestine is the **longest segment** of the alimentary canal -- stretching about **6 metres** in length (roughly the length of a school bus!).

This is where the most important work happens. The small intestine receives digestive fluids from three sources:

1. **Its own lining** -- produces juices that break down carbohydrates, proteins, and fats further
2. **Liver** -- sends **bile**, a greenish-yellow fluid that neutralises stomach acid and breaks **fats into tiny droplets** (imagine how dish soap breaks grease into small droplets in water)
3. **Pancreas** -- sends **pancreatic juice**, which is mildly alkaline and digests carbohydrates, proteins, and fats

**Absorption of nutrients:** The inner wall of the small intestine is lined with **millions of tiny, finger-shaped projections**. These projections enormously increase the surface area available for absorbing nutrients. Digested food molecules pass through these projections into the **blood vessels** running through the intestinal wall. The blood then carries these nutrients to every cell in the body.

> **Key idea:** The small intestine is where digestion is completed and nutrients are absorbed into the blood. Its finger-like projections massively increase the absorption surface area.

---

### The Large Intestine

![The large intestine — absorbing water and forming waste](the-large-intestine.png)

After the small intestine extracts most nutrients, the remaining **undigested waste** enters the **large intestine**. It is about 1.5 metres long -- shorter than the small intestine but **wider in diameter**.

The large intestine performs several functions:
- **Absorbs water** and dissolved salts from the waste material
- Converts the waste into a **semi-solid form** (called **stool** or **faeces**)
- Stores the stool in the **rectum** until the body is ready to expel it

The waste is finally pushed out through the **anus**. This removal of undigested waste is called **egestion**.

**Healthy habit:** Eating **fibre-rich foods** -- whole wheat atta, brown rice, fruits like guava and apple, and vegetables like spinach and beans -- helps the large intestine work smoothly by adding bulk to the stool.

The large intestine also hosts billions of **helpful bacteria** that aid digestion and support overall health. Fermented foods like **dahi, chaas, idli**, and **dosa** (made from fermented batter) nourish these beneficial bacteria.

---

### The Complete Path of Food

Here is the route food travels through your body:

**Mouth** --> **Oesophagus** --> **Stomach** --> **Small Intestine** --> **Large Intestine** --> **Rectum** --> **Anus**

| Organ | Key Function |
|-------|-------------|
| **Mouth** | Teeth grind food; saliva converts starch to sugar |
| **Oesophagus** | Pushes food to stomach via wave-like muscle action |
| **Stomach** | Churns food; acid kills germs; enzymes attack proteins |
| **Small intestine** | Completes digestion of all nutrients; absorbs them into blood |
| **Large intestine** | Absorbs water and salts; forms semi-solid waste |
| **Rectum and Anus** | Stores and expels waste (egestion) |

## Digestion in Other Animals

Animals have evolved remarkably different digestive systems depending on what they eat.

### Ruminants (Cows, Buffaloes, Goats)

![Digestion in other animals — ruminants (cows, buffaloes, goats) and rumination](digestion-in-other-animals-ruminants-cows-buffaloes-goats.png)

Grass-eating animals like cows and goats are called **ruminants**. Their digestive process is quite special:

1. They hastily swallow partially chewed grass, which goes into a special stomach compartment called the **rumen**.
2. Inside the rumen, bacteria help partially break down the tough plant fibres.
3. Later, when the animal is resting, the partially digested food is **pushed back up to the mouth** for thorough re-chewing. This is called **rumination** or "chewing the cud" -- this is why you see cows seemingly chewing all day even when they are not eating fresh grass!
4. A cow may spend nearly **8 hours every day** just re-chewing its food.
5. The well-chewed food is swallowed again for complete digestion.

### Birds

![Digestion in other animals — birds and the gizzard](digestion-in-other-animals-birds.png)

Birds have no teeth at all. Instead, they possess a muscular chamber called the **gizzard**. The thick walls of the gizzard contract powerfully to crush food. Many birds swallow small pebbles or **grit**, which sit inside the gizzard and help grind the food -- acting like a natural stone mill.

> **Key idea:** Different animals have digestive systems adapted to the specific foods they eat and the environments they live in.

## Respiration in Animals

Digestion breaks food down into simple nutrients like glucose. But the body still needs to **unlock the energy** stored in glucose. This requires **oxygen**, and the process is called **respiration**.

### Breathing vs Respiration

![Respiration in animals — breathing vs respiration](respiration-in-animals-breathing-vs-respiration.png)

These two words sound similar but mean different things:

| Feature | Breathing | Respiration |
|---------|-----------|-------------|
| **What is it?** | The physical act of pulling air in and pushing it out | A chemical reaction that releases energy from glucose |
| **Type** | Physical process | Chemical process |
| **Where?** | Lungs, nose, windpipe | Inside every living cell |
| **What is involved?** | Air is inhaled and exhaled | Glucose combines with oxygen to produce energy, carbon dioxide, and water |

**The respiration equation:**

**Glucose + Oxygen --> Carbon dioxide + Water + Energy**

> **Key idea:** **Breathing** is the mechanical process of moving air in and out of the lungs. **Respiration** is the chemical process where oxygen reacts with glucose inside cells to release the energy the body needs. They are connected but distinct.

## The Human Respiratory System

### Parts of the Respiratory System

![The human respiratory system — parts of the respiratory system](the-human-respiratory-system-parts-of-the-respiratory-system.png)

1. **Nostrils** -- The two openings through which air enters and exits
2. **Nasal passages** -- Narrow channels lined with fine hair and sticky mucus that trap dust, pollen, and germs from incoming air (this is why breathing through the nose is healthier than mouth-breathing)
3. **Windpipe (Trachea)** -- A sturdy tube reinforced with rings of cartilage that carries filtered air down to the lungs
4. **Lungs** -- A pair of spongy organs sheltered within the **rib cage**. Inside, the windpipe divides into finer and finer tubes, ending in clusters of extremely tiny air sacs called **alveoli**

### The Mechanics of Breathing

![The human respiratory system — the mechanics of breathing](the-human-respiratory-system-the-mechanics-of-breathing.png)

**Inhalation (Breathing in):**
1. The **ribs** swing upward and outward
2. The **diaphragm** (a dome-shaped muscle sheet below the lungs) flattens and moves **downward**
3. The chest cavity expands, creating more space
4. Air rushes into the lungs to fill the expanded space

**Exhalation (Breathing out):**
1. The ribs move downward and inward
2. The diaphragm curves back **upward**
3. The chest cavity shrinks
4. Air is squeezed out of the lungs

### Gas Exchange in the Alveoli

![The human respiratory system — gas exchange in the alveoli](the-human-respiratory-system-gas-exchange-in-the-alveoli.png)

The **alveoli** have walls so thin that gases can pass through them easily. A dense network of blood vessels wraps around each alveolus. This is where the vital gas swap occurs:

- **Oxygen** from the inhaled air crosses the thin alveolar wall and enters the **blood**
- **Carbon dioxide** (a waste product from cells) crosses from the blood into the alveoli and is **breathed out**

### Inhaled Air vs Exhaled Air

| Gas | Inhaled Air | Exhaled Air |
|-----|------------|-------------|
| **Oxygen** | About 21% | About 16-17% |
| **Carbon dioxide** | About 0.04% | About 4-5% |

Our body does not use up all the oxygen in each breath. But exhaled air has significantly more carbon dioxide than the air we breathe in.

**Simple test:** Blow exhaled air through a straw into a glass of **lime water**. The lime water turns **milky**, confirming the presence of carbon dioxide. If you pump ordinary room air through lime water using a syringe, it stays clear -- because inhaled air has very little carbon dioxide.

> **Key idea:** Gas exchange happens in the **alveoli** of the lungs. Oxygen enters the blood while carbon dioxide exits. Blood then carries oxygen to every cell in the body.

## The Circulatory System

![The circulatory system — heart, blood, and blood vessels](the-circulatory-system.png)

How do nutrients from digestion and oxygen from breathing reach every one of the trillions of cells in your body? Through the **circulatory system** -- the body's internal delivery network.

Its key components:
- **Heart** -- a powerful muscular pump that beats roughly 100,000 times each day
- **Blood** -- the transport fluid carrying oxygen, nutrients, and waste products
- **Blood vessels** -- a vast network of tubes (arteries, veins, and capillaries) through which blood flows

The heart pumps oxygen-rich, nutrient-laden blood to every cell, and simultaneously collects carbon dioxide and other waste for removal.

## Breathing in Other Animals

![Breathing in other animals — lungs, gills, and moist skin](breathing-in-other-animals.png)

Nature has equipped different animals with different breathing tools, perfectly matched to their habitats:

| Animal | Breathing Organ | Habitat |
|--------|----------------|---------|
| Humans, elephants, parrots, cobras, monitor lizards | **Lungs** | Land |
| Fish (rohu, catla, pomfret) | **Gills** (absorb oxygen dissolved in water) | Water |
| Tadpoles (baby frogs) | **Gills** | Water |
| Adult frogs | **Lungs** on land; **moist skin** in water | Both land and water |
| Earthworms | **Moist skin** (absorb oxygen directly) | Soil |

Frogs undergo a remarkable transformation called **metamorphosis** -- their breathing system changes from gills (as tadpoles in water) to lungs and skin (as adults on land and in water).

Earthworms must keep their skin moist to breathe. When heavy rain floods the soil, oxygen levels in the ground drop, forcing earthworms to the surface to absorb oxygen from the air.

> **Key idea:** Animals breathe using different organs suited to their environments -- lungs for land, gills for water, and moist skin for soil-dwelling creatures.

## Staying Healthy

![Staying healthy — oral, digestive, and respiratory care](staying-healthy.png)

### Oral Hygiene
- Brush teeth and scrape your tongue morning and night
- Rinse your mouth with plain water after every meal or snack

### Digestive Wellness
- Eat plenty of fibre: whole grains, fruits, vegetables, and sprouts
- Include fermented foods like dahi, chaas, and idli in your diet
- Eat meals at regular times and chew food slowly
- Avoid overeating -- stop when you feel comfortably full

### Respiratory Care
- Breathe through your nose to filter dust and germs
- Practise **pranayama** and deep breathing regularly for stronger lungs
- Stay away from cigarette smoke -- it destroys the delicate alveoli and causes severe diseases
- Even **passive smoking** (inhaling someone else's smoke) damages health, especially in children

## Summary

- **Life processes** (nutrition, respiration, circulation, excretion, reproduction) are essential for animal survival.
- The **human digestive system** includes the alimentary canal (mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, anus) and helper organs (liver, pancreas).
- Digestion begins in the mouth (saliva converts starch to sugar), continues in the stomach (acid and enzymes attack proteins), and is completed in the small intestine (nutrients absorbed into blood).
- The large intestine absorbs water and forms solid waste for egestion.
- **Ruminants** like cows re-chew their food through rumination. **Birds** use a muscular gizzard to grind food.
- **Breathing** is the physical process of inhaling and exhaling. **Respiration** is the chemical process of breaking down glucose with oxygen to release energy.
- Gas exchange occurs in the **alveoli** of the lungs -- oxygen enters the blood, carbon dioxide exits.
- The **circulatory system** (heart, blood, blood vessels) delivers oxygen and nutrients to every cell.
- Different animals breathe using different organs -- lungs, gills, or moist skin -- adapted to their habitats.
