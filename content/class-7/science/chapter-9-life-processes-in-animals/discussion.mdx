## What Happens to the Food We Eat?

**Child:** Maa, when I eat a paratha in the morning, how does it give me energy to run around during games period?

**Mother:** That is a wonderful question, beta. The moment you take a bite, your body starts a long process. Your teeth crush the paratha into tiny bits -- that is mechanical digestion. Meanwhile, saliva in your mouth begins turning the starch in the flour into sugar. Here is a fun experiment -- chew a small piece of plain roti for about a minute without swallowing. You will notice it starts tasting sweet!

**Child:** Really? So digestion begins right in the mouth?

**Mother:** Yes! From the mouth, the food slides down the oesophagus -- the food pipe -- pushed along by wave-like muscle contractions. In the stomach, powerful acids kill any germs in the food, and special juices start breaking down proteins. A protective coating called mucus keeps the acid from harming the stomach walls. Then everything moves into the small intestine, which is about 6 metres long -- the real powerhouse of digestion.

**Child:** How does the digested food reach the rest of my body?

**Mother:** The inner wall of the small intestine is covered with millions of tiny finger-like bumps. These bumps create a huge surface area for absorbing nutrients. The digested food passes through them into tiny blood vessels, and the blood carries it to every cell in your body. Whatever the body cannot digest moves into the large intestine, where water is absorbed, and the remaining waste is eventually pushed out.

## How Other Animals Digest Food

**Child:** Maa, do all animals eat and digest food the way we do?

**Mother:** Not at all! Take our neighbour's cow, for example. Cows are ruminants. They quickly swallow grass without chewing it thoroughly, sending it to a special stomach chamber called the rumen. Later, when the cow is resting under a tree, she brings the partially digested grass back up to her mouth and chews it again. This is called rumination -- that is why you always see cows moving their jaws even when they are not eating fresh grass. A cow spends nearly 8 hours a day just re-chewing!

**Child:** What about birds? Sparrows and pigeons swallow seeds whole.

**Mother:** Exactly -- birds have no teeth at all. Instead, they have a strong muscular chamber called the gizzard. It squeezes and grinds the food. Many birds swallow tiny pebbles that sit inside the gizzard and act like grinding stones. It is nature's own flour mill!

**Child:** So every animal's digestive system matches the kind of food it eats?

**Mother:** Precisely. Grass-eaters need long, complex digestive systems because plant fibre is tough to break down. Meat-eaters have shorter systems because flesh digests more easily. Each species has evolved the perfect machinery for its diet.

## Breathing and Respiration Are Different

**Child:** Maa, my friend says breathing and respiration are the same thing. Are they?

**Mother:** Many people think so, but they are actually different. Breathing is the physical act of pulling air into your lungs and pushing it out -- inhaling and exhaling. Respiration is a chemical reaction that happens inside every cell of your body, where oxygen combines with glucose to release energy.

**Child:** So breathing brings the oxygen in, and respiration uses it inside cells?

**Mother:** Exactly. The equation for respiration is: glucose plus oxygen produces carbon dioxide, water, and energy. Your lungs handle the breathing part -- getting oxygen in and carbon dioxide out. The actual energy release happens quietly inside billions of cells.

**Child:** Why is there more carbon dioxide in the air we breathe out?

**Mother:** When you breathe in, the air has about 21 percent oxygen and barely 0.04 percent carbon dioxide. When you breathe out, oxygen drops to about 16 percent and carbon dioxide jumps to about 4 to 5 percent. Your cells have consumed some oxygen and produced carbon dioxide as waste. You can prove this easily -- blow through a straw into a glass of lime water. It turns milky because of the carbon dioxide in your breath.

## How Different Animals Breathe

**Child:** Do all animals use lungs like us, Maa?

**Mother:** Not at all! Each animal's breathing system is designed for where it lives. Land animals like us, elephants, parrots, and even snakes use lungs. Fish like rohu and pomfret use gills to extract oxygen that is dissolved in the water. Earthworms absorb oxygen directly through their moist skin -- they do not have lungs or gills.

**Child:** What about frogs? They live in water and on land.

**Mother:** Frogs are fascinating. When they are tadpoles -- young baby frogs living entirely in water -- they breathe through gills, just like fish. But as they grow into adults, they develop lungs and start breathing air on land. They can also absorb oxygen through their moist skin when they are in water. This transformation is called metamorphosis.

**Child:** That explains why earthworms come out of the ground when it rains heavily!

**Mother:** Smart connection! When the soil gets waterlogged, the earthworms cannot get enough oxygen through their skin underground. They crawl to the surface where they can breathe from the air directly. They need their skin to stay moist, but not so wet that they are submerged.

## Keeping Our Body Systems Healthy

**Child:** Maa, what should I do to keep my digestion and breathing working well?

**Mother:** For digestion, eat plenty of fibre-rich foods -- whole wheat roti instead of maida, fruits like guava and apple, and green vegetables. Include dahi or chaas with your meals because they contain helpful bacteria that support digestion. Eat at regular times and chew your food slowly instead of rushing.

**Child:** And for my lungs?

**Mother:** Always breathe through your nose, not your mouth. Your nasal passages have tiny hairs and mucus that filter out dust and germs before the air reaches your lungs. Practise pranayama and deep breathing exercises regularly -- they strengthen your lungs and improve your oxygen intake. And never, ever go near cigarette smoke.

**Child:** Even if someone else is smoking nearby?

**Mother:** Especially then. Passive smoking -- breathing in someone else's cigarette smoke -- is extremely harmful, particularly for children. Your body is like a well-coordinated team, beta. Digestion breaks down the food, breathing brings in the oxygen, respiration converts them into energy, and the circulatory system delivers that energy to every corner of your body. Take good care of all these systems and they will take care of you.
