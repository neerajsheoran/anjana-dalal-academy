## What Happens to the Food We Eat?

**Meera:** Arjun, yaar, weird question. I ate two parathas at breakfast. By games period I was running across the field. How did the paratha actually become running?

**Arjun:** That is not weird, that is just digestion. Mummy says digestion every time I do not finish my dal.

**Meera:** But step by step — what actually happens? I chew. I swallow. Then what?

**Arjun:** Teeth crush the paratha. Saliva mixes in. Try this — chew a plain roti for one full minute without swallowing. It starts tasting sweet.

**Meera:** Sweet? From roti?

**Arjun:** Yes! Daadi told me this trick. Saliva is quietly turning the starch into sugar inside your mouth. Digestion has already started before you swallow.

**Meera:** Whoa. Then it goes down the food pipe to the stomach, right?

**Arjun:** Yes. And the stomach is brutal. Strong acid in there kills germs and breaks proteins. There is also a mucus layer protecting the stomach from its own acid.

**Meera:** So the stomach is acidic enough to dissolve food but somehow doesn't dissolve itself.

**Arjun:** That mucus is the bodyguard. After that the food enters the small intestine, which is about 6 metres long — like a folded school bus inside your tummy.

**Meera:** Six metres? Inside ME?

**Arjun:** Coiled up. And its inner wall has millions of tiny finger-like bumps. They suck the nutrients out and push them into the blood. The blood then carries the paratha-energy to your leg muscles for games period.

**Meera:** So my running was literally roti.

## How Other Animals Digest Food

**Meera:** Arjun, I was at my cousin's village last summer. Their cow Lakshmi was lying in the shade chewing for hours, but nobody had given her fresh grass. What was she chewing?

**Arjun:** Old grass! Cows are ruminants. They swallow grass quickly without chewing properly, send it to a special stomach part called the rumen, and then later bring it back up to chew it again.

**Meera:** Wait — they vomit it back into the mouth on purpose?

**Arjun:** Sort of. It is called rumination, or "chewing the cud." A cow can spend nearly 8 hours a day just re-chewing.

**Meera:** That explains so much. I used to think she was bored.

**Arjun:** Plant fibre is super tough. You need extra rounds of grinding plus bacteria in the rumen to break it down properly.

**Meera:** OK but what about birds? The sparrows on our balcony swallow whole grains. They do not chew at all.

**Arjun:** Birds have no teeth. Instead they have a muscular pouch called a gizzard. It squeezes the food really hard. Many birds also swallow tiny pebbles that sit inside the gizzard and grind the grain like a mini chakki.

**Meera:** A stone grinder INSIDE the bird?

**Arjun:** Yes! Nature's own flour mill. So basically every animal's digestive system is designed for what it eats. Grass-eaters get long systems with extra chambers. Meat-eaters get shorter systems because flesh breaks down faster.

**Meera:** So when Mummy says "do not eat like a bird," she does not understand bird gizzards.

**Arjun:** She really does not.

## Breathing and Respiration Are Different

**Meera:** Arjun, our class teacher made a big deal yesterday about breathing being different from respiration. They sound like the same word to me.

**Arjun:** They are different. Breathing is the physical thing — air going in and out of your lungs. Respiration is the chemical thing happening inside every cell.

**Meera:** Chemical reaction? Inside my cells right now?

**Arjun:** Right now, billions of times. Glucose plus oxygen reacts to give carbon dioxide, water, and energy. That energy is what you are using to argue with me.

**Meera:** So the lungs are basically the delivery system, but the actual energy-making happens deeper inside?

**Arjun:** Exactly. Lungs bring oxygen in, push carbon dioxide out. The cells take that oxygen and burn glucose with it.

**Meera:** But why does the air we breathe out have so much more carbon dioxide?

**Arjun:** Because the cells made it as waste. Inhaled air has about 21% oxygen and almost no carbon dioxide. Exhaled has only 16% oxygen and around 4-5% carbon dioxide. You consumed some and dumped some.

**Meera:** Is there a way to actually see the carbon dioxide in my breath?

**Arjun:** Easy. Take some lime water in a glass, blow into it through a straw. It turns milky.

**Meera:** Milky from my breath alone?

**Arjun:** Yes. That milkiness is the carbon dioxide reacting. If you pump regular room air through the same lime water with a syringe, nothing happens. Pure proof.

**Meera:** I am trying this after dinner.

## How Different Animals Breathe

**Meera:** Arjun, do all animals breathe with lungs like us?

**Arjun:** No way. Each animal's breathing organ matches where it lives. Humans, elephants, parrots, even cobras — all lungs. Fish like rohu and pomfret use gills.

**Meera:** Gills? How do gills work in water?

**Arjun:** Water has dissolved oxygen in it — tiny amounts. Gills are designed to pull that oxygen out as water flows past them. That's why fish die in dirty or boiled water — no dissolved oxygen.

**Meera:** What about frogs though? They live in water AND on land.

**Arjun:** Frogs are wild. When they are tadpoles — baby frogs swimming in ponds — they have gills, just like fish. When they grow up into adults, they develop lungs and breathe air. The whole transformation is called metamorphosis.

**Meera:** So a frog's breathing system literally changes during its life?

**Arjun:** Yes. And even as adults, frogs can absorb oxygen through their moist skin when they are sitting in water.

**Meera:** Hold on, this connects to something. Last monsoon, after that big rain, my mohalla road was full of earthworms crawling out. Why?

**Arjun:** Earthworms breathe through their moist skin. They live in soil with tiny air pockets between the grains. When the rain floods the soil, those air pockets fill with water, oxygen drops, and the worms basically suffocate underground. So they climb out to where they can breathe directly from air.

**Meera:** I always thought they came up to enjoy the rain.

**Arjun:** They came up to survive.

## Keeping Our Body Systems Healthy

**Meera:** Arjun, ok serious question. What do we actually do to keep all these systems running well? Mummy keeps telling me to "eat properly" but that is not a real instruction.

**Arjun:** Fibre. Whole wheat roti instead of maida. Fruits like guava and apple. Lots of vegetables. Fibre keeps the large intestine working smoothly.

**Meera:** What about dahi? Daadi swears by chaas with every meal.

**Arjun:** Daadi is right. Fermented stuff — dahi, chaas, idli, dosa — feeds the helpful bacteria in your gut. They actually help you digest.

**Meera:** And for breathing?

**Arjun:** Always breathe through your nose, not your mouth. Your nose has tiny hairs and sticky mucus that trap dust before it reaches your lungs. The mouth has no such filter.

**Meera:** My PT teacher told us about pranayama last week. Is that actually useful or just tradition?

**Arjun:** Both. Pranayama and deep breathing genuinely strengthen the lungs and improve oxygen intake. It is one of those things modern doctors and old yoga people agree on.

**Meera:** And cigarettes?

**Arjun:** Stay far away. Not just smoking yourself — even being near someone smoking is bad. It is called passive smoking. Damages the alveoli, especially in kids whose lungs are still developing.

**Meera:** So we have to defend our own lungs even when other people are damaging the air.

**Arjun:** Yes. Your body is basically a team — digestion gives nutrients, breathing gives oxygen, respiration converts them to energy, circulation delivers it everywhere. Take care of all of them.

**Meera:** Yaar, after this conversation I feel like apologising to my body for everything I ate at last week's school mela.

**Arjun:** Just balance it with a guava today. Your small intestine forgives easily.
