## A Familiar Scene

It's Sunday morning. Your family goes to the local **mandi**. Vegetables are piled high on jute mats. A vegetable seller calls out prices. Your mother examines tomatoes, asks the price, says it's too high, the seller comes down a bit, your mother adds a few more — and the deal is done.

Or maybe today is online. Your father opens an app. He scrolls through phones, compares prices, reads reviews, taps a button. Tomorrow, a delivery person rings the bell with a brand new phone in hand.

Both of these are **markets** in action. The vegetable mandi. The online shop. The neighbourhood kirana store. The shopping mall. The factory selling to wholesalers. The stock market on TV. All markets.

What is a market? How does it work? Why does it matter? That is what this chapter is about.

## What Is a Market?

A **market** is **any place where buyers and sellers come together to exchange goods, services, or money**.

The word "market" has many local names:
- **Bazaar** — Persian-origin, used across India
- **Mandi** — wholesale food market (Hindi)
- **Haat** — periodic rural market (Hindi, Bengali)
- **Markatte** — Kannada
- **Santhai** — Tamil
- **Vippanam** — Sanskrit (literally, "selling place")

Markets can be:
- A **physical place** — like a mandi, a mall, a kirana shop
- A **virtual space** — like Amazon, Flipkart, Myntra
- A **time-bound event** — like a Sunday haat
- A **specialised place** — like a stock market or a wholesale fish market

What makes something a market? Three things:
1. **Buyers** who want to buy
2. **Sellers** who want to sell
3. A **price** they agree on

Without these three, nothing happens. With these three, a market exists.

## A Glimpse of an Ancient Market — Hampi Bazaar

To understand how powerful and lively markets can be, let us go back **500 years** — to the great **Hampi Bazaar** of the Vijayanagara Empire.

Hampi (in modern Karnataka) was the capital of one of India's wealthiest empires (1336-1646 CE). The Hampi Bazaar was located opposite the famous **Virupaksha Temple**. Foreign travellers who visited it left descriptions full of wonder.

**Domingos Paes**, a Portuguese traveller, called Hampi **"the best-provided city in the world."** He wrote that the bazaar sold:
- Grains and seeds in immense quantities
- Milk, oil, butter
- Silk, cotton, and other fabrics
- Cows, rabbits, horses
- Quails, partridges, and other birds
- Spices from every region

Another Portuguese, **Fernão Nuniz**, marvelled:
> "There were craftsmen working in their streets — golden jewels, gewgaws, rubies, diamonds, pearls, precious stones. Sellers of cloths of cotton. Grass and straw in infinite abundance... how could anyone describe it to be believed?"

The Hampi Bazaar was busy with traders from:
- All over India
- Persia and Arabia
- China and South East Asia
- Portugal and other European countries

It was a **global market** in the 16th century. People bought and sold across continents. Diamonds from Golconda. Pepper from Kerala. Silk from China. Horses from Arabia. Cotton from the Deccan. All flowing through Hampi.

Today, most of Hampi is in ruins (a UNESCO World Heritage Site). But the bazaar street still exists. You can walk it. Imagine the colours, the voices, the smells.

## How Do Prices Get Set?

Imagine a guava seller wants to sell guavas at **₹80 per kg**. But buyers think that's too expensive. Some refuse to buy. Others offer **₹30**. The seller refuses — he won't make a profit at that price.

What happens?

**Scenario 1 — Price too high (₹80/kg).**
Many guavas remain unsold. The seller takes them home. Some rot. He earns less than expected.

**Scenario 2 — Price too low (₹20/kg).**
Buyers swarm in. Guavas sell out in minutes. But the seller barely covers his costs. He loses money. He may stop selling guavas next time.

**Scenario 3 — Price just right (₹40/kg).**
Most guavas sell. Buyers feel they got a fair deal. The seller makes a reasonable profit. Both sides are happy.

Over time, sellers adjust prices based on what works. Markets find a **balance** between what sellers want and what buyers will pay. Economists call this the **equilibrium price** — the price at which supply (quantity sellers offer) roughly equals demand (quantity buyers want).

### Why Vegetables Get Cheaper at Night

Have you noticed that vegetables sell cheaper late in the evening at weekly markets? That's because:
- Vegetables are **perishable** — they will rot by tomorrow
- Sellers prefer **some money now** to nothing tomorrow
- So they **lower prices** to clear stock

### Why Sweaters Are Cheaper at Season's End

Garment stores discount woollens heavily in spring because:
- Demand falls when warm weather arrives
- Storing them for next winter costs money
- Better to sell at a discount than hold inventory

Both examples show that **prices respond to supply and demand**, balanced by what sellers and buyers actually need.

### Bargaining vs Fixed Prices

In some markets — vegetable mandis, sabzi mandis, local shops — **bargaining** is common. The first price is just a starting point; the real price emerges through negotiation.

In other markets — supermarkets, malls, online shops — prices are **fixed**. You either pay the marked price or you don't buy.

Why the difference?
- **Bargaining** works in small markets with individual sellers who know their stock and costs.
- **Fixed prices** work for large businesses with many products and standardised systems. They save time. They prevent uneven treatment of customers.

Indian markets have both. Both work, in different contexts.

## Types of Markets

Markets come in many varieties. Let us look at the main types.

### 1. Physical vs Online Markets

**Physical markets** — buyers and sellers meet in person:
- Local vegetable mandi
- Kirana store
- Shopping mall
- Wholesale market
- Weekly haat

**Online markets** — buyers and sellers connect through the internet:
- Amazon, Flipkart, Myntra (for goods)
- Swiggy, Zomato (for food delivery)
- Uber, Ola (for rides)
- Airbnb (for stays)
- E-Bay, OLX (peer-to-peer)

Online markets have grown enormously. By 2024, Indian e-commerce was about ₹6-7 lakh crore (₹6-7 trillion). It is expected to double again in the next 5-7 years.

**Pros of physical markets:**
- Touch, smell, examine the product
- Immediate possession
- Personal relationships
- Bargaining possible
- Cash transactions

**Pros of online markets:**
- Wide selection (millions of products)
- Compare prices easily
- Door-to-door delivery
- Customer reviews
- 24/7 availability

**Cons of physical markets:**
- Limited selection in any one shop
- Travel required
- Limited hours
- Hard to compare prices

**Cons of online markets:**
- Cannot touch/examine
- Delivery time
- Risk of fraud or fake products
- Returns can be inconvenient
- Personal data is collected

Both types coexist. Most Indians use both, depending on the situation.

### 2. Domestic vs International Markets

**Domestic markets** — buying and selling within India.

**International markets** — selling abroad (**exports**) or buying from abroad (**imports**).

#### India's Major Exports

India exports to nearly every country in the world. Major exports include:
- **Software services** — IT companies like TCS, Infosys, Wipro serve clients globally
- **Pharmaceuticals** — India is the "pharmacy of the world" — generic drugs go everywhere
- **Engineering goods** — auto parts, machinery, equipment
- **Textiles and garments** — cotton clothes, sarees, jeans
- **Gems and jewellery** — Surat is the world centre for diamond cutting
- **Agricultural products** — rice (especially basmati), spices, tea, coffee
- **Petroleum products** — refined fuels (we import crude oil and re-export refined)
- **Chemicals**
- **Iron and steel**

India's total exports in 2023-24 were around **$435 billion**.

#### India's Major Imports

India imports goods we cannot produce enough of, or which are cheaper to buy abroad:
- **Crude oil** — India is one of the world's largest oil importers
- **Gold** — Indians love gold; we import enormous quantities
- **Electronics** — phones, semiconductors, chips
- **Machinery** — specialised industrial equipment
- **Coal** — though we mine coal too, we import more
- **Plastics and chemicals**
- **Edible oils** — palm oil, soybean oil
- **Pulses** — sometimes when domestic supply is short

India's total imports in 2023-24 were around **$675 billion**.

The difference between exports and imports is called the **trade deficit**. India typically has a deficit, meaning we import more than we export. This is offset by remittances from Indians working abroad and by services exports (especially software).

### 3. Wholesale vs Retail Markets

**Wholesale markets** — large quantities sold to other businesses or shops. A farmer sells 100 kg of tomatoes to a wholesale buyer at a low per-kg price.

**Retail markets** — small quantities sold to final consumers. The kirana shop sells you 1 kg of tomatoes at a higher per-kg price.

Wholesale price is usually lower than retail because:
- Wholesalers buy in **bulk** (lower cost per unit)
- They don't deal with individual customers (lower service costs)
- They take **risks** (storage, transport)
- They sell to retailers, who add their own profit margin

### 4. Goods Markets vs Services Markets

**Goods** — physical things: clothes, food, electronics, cars.
**Services** — actions performed: a haircut, a doctor's visit, an online class, a taxi ride.

Both have markets, but services have unique features:
- Cannot be stored (you can't save a haircut for later)
- Cannot be transported easily
- Often require **direct contact** between provider and customer
- Quality varies a lot from one provider to another

Modern India is increasingly a **services economy** — services contribute about 50%+ of GDP.

### 5. Other Specialised Markets

- **Labour market** — where workers offer their work and employers offer jobs
- **Capital market** — where companies raise money (shares, bonds)
- **Stock market** — where shares are bought and sold (BSE, NSE)
- **Commodity market** — where bulk goods like wheat, gold, oil are traded
- **Real estate market** — for buying and selling property
- **Foreign exchange market** — where currencies are exchanged

You will learn more about these later. The point is: markets are everywhere. They are the **invisible network** through which the modern economy runs.

## What Makes Markets Work

For markets to work well, several things need to be in place:

1. **Buyers and sellers** in sufficient numbers
2. **Information** — buyers know what is available; sellers know what buyers want
3. **Trust** — that what is promised will be delivered
4. **Money** (or trusted barter) — to complete transactions
5. **Rules** — about quality, weights, contracts, returns
6. **Infrastructure** — roads, internet, banks for moving goods, information, and money
7. **Some competition** — multiple sellers compete, multiple buyers can choose

When all of these exist, markets thrive. When one or more is missing, markets struggle or fail.

## When Markets Fail

Markets are powerful but not perfect. Sometimes they fail:

### Monopoly
When one seller dominates, buyers have no choice. The seller can charge unfair prices, give poor service, treat customers badly. This is why governments break up monopolies.

### Information Asymmetry
When sellers know more than buyers (or vice versa), the less-informed party loses. Used car markets, for example — sellers know the car's faults better than buyers. This is why we have laws requiring disclosure.

### Externalities
Sometimes a market transaction affects third parties not involved in it. A factory producing cheap goods may pollute a river — but the river is not in the market price. Other people, downstream, pay the cost. Government regulation handles this.

### Public Goods
Some things benefit everyone but no one wants to pay for them — like clean air, street lighting, national defence. The market alone can't provide these. Government has to.

### Inequality
Markets give more to those who already have more. A rich person can outbid a poor person for everything. This is why countries have minimum wages, food subsidies, free education, and other supports for the poor.

So markets are essential — but they need to be **balanced** by government, by ethics, by social institutions.

## The Role of the Consumer

In every market transaction, the buyer (consumer) has rights and responsibilities. India's Consumer Protection Act gives every consumer:

- **Right to safety** — products should be safe
- **Right to information** — labels, ingredients, warnings
- **Right to choose** — fair competition, no monopoly
- **Right to be heard** — complaints should reach manufacturers
- **Right to redressal** — get refunds or replacements for faulty goods
- **Right to consumer education** — know your rights

When you grow up, **be a smart consumer**:
- Compare prices and quality
- Read reviews and labels
- Don't fall for misleading ads
- Save receipts and warranties
- File complaints if cheated
- Boycott companies that mistreat customers or harm society

The consumer is the king/queen of the market — but only when they exercise their power wisely.

## Before We Close

- A **market** is anywhere buyers and sellers come together to exchange goods or services.
- Markets are physical (mandis, malls), online (Amazon, Flipkart), or specialised (stock market).
- The **price** is set by what buyers will pay and sellers will accept — the meeting of supply and demand.
- Prices change with **time**, **season**, and **scarcity**.
- **Hampi Bazaar** in the Vijayanagara Empire (16th century) was one of the great markets in world history.
- Markets come in many types: **physical/online**, **domestic/international**, **wholesale/retail**, **goods/services**, and specialised markets (labour, capital, stock, real estate).
- India's **exports**: software, pharmaceuticals, engineering, textiles, jewellery (~$435 billion).
- India's **imports**: crude oil, gold, electronics, machinery (~$675 billion).
- Markets need: enough buyers and sellers, information, trust, money, rules, infrastructure, competition.
- Markets can **fail** through monopoly, hidden information, externalities, neglect of public goods, or inequality — which is why government and ethics also matter.
- The **consumer has rights** under Indian law — and the responsibility to use them.
