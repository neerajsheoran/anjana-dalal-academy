## Why Does the Indian Year Have Six Seasons When Most of the World Has Four?

**Child:** Maa, in Europe and America, people learn four seasons — spring, summer, autumn, winter. But we have six ritus. Why?

**Mother:** Because India's climate is more varied. Most of Europe and North America has a temperate climate with four clean seasons. India has tropical monsoon climate plus several other types, and we needed more words to describe what we experienced.

**Child:** So we added rainy season?

**Mother:** Yes — **varsha**, the monsoon, is so distinct in India that calling it just "part of summer" doesn't capture it. The world feels completely different in the monsoon. Plants explode into green. Frogs and insects emerge. Special foods are cooked. Specific festivals happen.

We also added **hemanta** — pre-winter — between autumn and winter. This is when temperatures cool but it's not yet truly cold, days are still pleasant.

**Child:** So six ritus describe what we actually experience?

**Mother:** Exactly. The seasons in any culture reflect what people actually live through. Inuit people in the Arctic have many words for different kinds of snow. Tropical people have words for different kinds of rain. India has six ritus because Indian weather has six distinct flavours.

**Child:** That's beautiful.

**Mother:** And practical. Ayurveda has different food and lifestyle recommendations for each ritu. Indian classical music has different ragas (melodic frameworks) for different seasons. Indian poetry celebrates the moods of each ritu. The six-season system is the basis for thousands of years of Indian culture.

## What Makes the Monsoon So Important?

**Child:** Maa, why is everyone always so worried about the monsoon? I see news debates about it every June.

**Mother:** Because the monsoon is literally the **economic engine** of India.

Think about it. About **60% of Indian farmland** depends on rain — not irrigation. If the monsoon is poor:
- Crops fail
- Farmers lose income
- Food prices rise nationally
- Rural India suffers
- The whole economy slows down
- India's GDP can shrink by 1-2%

If the monsoon is good:
- Bountiful crops
- Farmer incomes rise
- Food prices stable
- Rural India prospers
- Economy grows
- Even urban India benefits (rural workers can spend on consumer goods)

**Child:** So monsoon is not just about weather?

**Mother:** It's about everything. Drinking water in cities, electricity from dams, the price of vegetables in your local market, jobs for daily-wage workers, even the inflation that affects your father's investments — all linked to the monsoon.

**Child:** What happens if climate change disrupts the monsoon?

**Mother:** That's one of India's biggest fears. Studies show the monsoon is becoming more **erratic** — sometimes too much rain in too few days (causing floods), sometimes long dry spells in between. If this continues, Indian agriculture will need to change. Farmers will need different crops, better water management, more reliable irrigation.

It's why India invests heavily in monsoon research and weather prediction. A few crores spent on satellites and supercomputers can save thousands of crores in agricultural losses.

## Why Are Cherrapunji and Mawsynram So Wet?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says Cherrapunji and Mawsynram in Meghalaya are among the wettest places on Earth. Why so wet?

**Mother:** Geography is the answer. These places sit on the **southern slopes of the Khasi Hills** in Meghalaya. The southwest monsoon, coming from the Bay of Bengal, **hits these hills directly and rises sharply**.

When air rises, it cools. When it cools, water vapour condenses. When water condenses, rain falls.

The Khasi Hills force the monsoon air to rise from sea level to over 1,300 meters in a short distance. All the moisture squeezes out as rain. Cherrapunji and Mawsynram receive **11-12 meters of rain per year** — that's a 4-story building of water falling on every square meter every year.

**Child:** Why don't people drown there?

**Mother:** Because the rain falls over many months (not all at once), and the steep hills let the water drain quickly to the rivers below. Cherrapunji is on a plateau with steep edges, so water runs off rather than pooling.

**Child:** Does anyone live there?

**Mother:** Yes, the Khasi people have lived there for thousands of years. They have a fascinating tradition: **living root bridges**. They guide the roots of rubber trees across rivers to grow into bridges. Over decades, the roots strengthen and the bridges can hold many people. These bridges are alive — they get stronger with time, unlike concrete bridges that decay. A perfect adaptation to a wet, rainy land.

**Child:** That's amazing — bridges grown, not built.

**Mother:** It's one of India's many beautiful examples of how communities adapt to extreme local geography.

## How Will Climate Change Affect My Future?

**Child:** Maa, the chapter says climate change is happening. What will it do to my generation's India?

**Mother:** Several things, mostly challenging:

**1. More extreme weather.** More frequent and intense heat waves, floods, droughts, and cyclones. The 2022 heat wave killed over 90 people in India and devastated wheat crops. Worse may come.

**2. Glacier loss.** Himalayan glaciers feed major rivers. As they melt faster, river flow temporarily increases (causing floods) but eventually decreases (causing water shortage). The Ganga, Indus, and Brahmaputra are all glacier-fed.

**3. Sea level rise.** Coastal cities like Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata may face more flooding. Some low-lying areas may become unlivable.

**4. Agriculture stress.** Crops that grew well for centuries may struggle as temperatures and rainfall change. Wheat in Punjab is already showing yield reductions on hot years.

**5. Health impacts.** Diseases that thrived in tropical heat may spread to new areas. Air pollution combined with heat will harm city dwellers.

**6. Migration pressure.** People in worst-affected areas may move to cities or other regions, creating new social challenges.

**Child:** That sounds terrifying. Is there hope?

**Mother:** Yes, real hope. Several reasons:

**1. India is investing heavily in renewable energy.** Solar power, wind power, electric vehicles. India aims for 500 GW of renewable energy by 2030 — one of the world's largest commitments.

**2. Better water management.** Rainwater harvesting, drip irrigation, water-efficient crops — many solutions being adopted.

**3. International cooperation.** India is a leader of the **International Solar Alliance**, which helps developing countries adopt solar power.

**4. Youth awareness.** Your generation is much more environment-aware than past ones. Young people are choosing careers in sustainability, demanding climate-friendly policies, changing consumption habits.

**5. Technology.** Climate-resilient crops, better forecasting, cleaner industry, electric transport, AI-managed grids — many tools being developed.

**6. Indian tradition supports it.** Indian culture has always emphasised harmony with nature — sacred rivers, sacred mountains, sacred trees. Reviving this respect can drive climate action.

**Child:** What can I personally do?

**Mother:** A lot, even as a Class 7 student:
- Save electricity at home
- Use public transport, walk, or cycle when possible
- Don't waste water
- Don't waste food (food waste contributes to climate change too)
- Plant trees
- Talk about climate to friends and family — awareness spreads
- Vote (when you grow up) for policies that protect the climate
- Choose a career that helps — engineering, science, policy, education — anywhere you can contribute

The climate crisis is enormous, but Indians have faced enormous challenges before — poverty, colonialism, disease — and overcome them. Your generation can do the same.

## Why Do We Have So Many Words for "Rain" in Indian Languages?

**Child:** Maa, in Hindi we have baarish, varsha, baadal, megh, jal, paani — so many words. In English they just say "rain." Why?

**Mother:** Because Indian culture has paid careful attention to **different kinds of rain**, just as Inuit cultures have many words for snow.

In Sanskrit-derived languages:
- **Varsha** — the formal word, the monsoon season itself
- **Baarish** — everyday word for rain
- **Megh / Badal** — clouds (often used poetically)
- **Boondaabaandi** — light drizzle
- **Mausladhar** — torrential rain
- **Phuhar** — light spraying rain

Tamil has equally rich vocabulary:
- **Mazhai** — rain
- **Thuli** — drop
- **Anizhal** — drizzle

**Child:** Why does language do this?

**Mother:** Because language reflects what people pay attention to. India is shaped by rain — every drop matters. Farmers care about whether the rain is too heavy (damages crops), too light (insufficient), at the right time (perfect), at the wrong time (disastrous). Different words let people communicate these differences precisely.

**Child:** So vocabulary tells you what a culture cares about?

**Mother:** Always. English has many words for business and trade — "deal, agreement, contract, transaction, exchange, settlement." That tells you something about English-speaking commercial culture. Indian languages have many words for rain, family relationships, food, and spiritual states. That tells you what Indian culture has always cared about.

**Child:** I will pay more attention to the words I use.

**Mother:** Good. Language carries the wisdom of generations.
