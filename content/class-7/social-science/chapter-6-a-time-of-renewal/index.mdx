## After the Mauryans — A Time of Change

When the Mauryan Empire fell in **185 BCE**, India did not collapse into chaos. Instead, it entered a long period of **reorganisation**. Many smaller kingdoms emerged, each competing for power. The map of India changed significantly. Cultures blended. New ideas arrived. Indian civilisation **renewed itself**.

This is why historians call this period the **Age of Reorganisation**. It lasted roughly from **185 BCE to 320 CE** — about 500 years.

## The Fall of the Mauryans

The last Mauryan emperor was **assassinated around 185 BCE** by his own commander-in-chief, **Pushyamitra Shunga**. The Mauryan dynasty ended; the **Shunga dynasty** rose in its place — but it controlled only the central part of the former empire.

The vast Mauryan empire **broke into many kingdoms** — some former tributaries declared independence; others were carved up by ambitious local rulers; the northwest became vulnerable to foreign invaders.

## Five Major Powers of the Age

### 1. The Shungas (~185-73 BCE)

- Founded by **Pushyamitra Shunga**
- Capital: **Pataliputra**, then **Vidisha**
- Ruled Magadha and central India for about a century
- Patrons of **Brahmanical (Hindu) traditions** — built temples, supported Sanskrit learning
- Famous monument: the **Bharhut Stupa** in Madhya Pradesh — covered with beautifully carved scenes from Buddhist Jataka tales
- The Shungas sometimes clashed with Buddhists but also patronised Buddhist art at certain sites

### 2. The Indo-Greeks (~190 BCE - 10 CE)

After Alexander the Great's death, his successors established Greek-ruled kingdoms in what is now Pakistan and Afghanistan. By 190 BCE, some Indo-Greek kings invaded India.

- **Menander I (Milinda)** was the most famous — converted to Buddhism after debating Indian sage **Nagasena** (recorded in the text **Milindapanha**)
- Greek styles blended with Indian — birth of **Greco-Buddhist art** (Gandhara school)
- Introduced **stamped silver coins** with portraits of kings
- Spread Greek scientific knowledge — astronomy, mathematics

### 3. The Shakas (Scythians, ~1st century BCE - 4th century CE)

- Originally **Central Asian nomads** who entered India through the northwest
- Settled in **Gujarat, Rajasthan, parts of Maharashtra and the Deccan**
- Most famous: **Rudradaman I** (~150 CE) — his **Junagadh inscription** in Sanskrit is one of the most important inscriptions of the period
- The Junagadh inscription describes the **repair of the Sudarshana Lake dam** — an early example of public infrastructure maintenance

### 4. The Kushans (~1st century CE - 3rd century CE)

A powerful Central Asian dynasty that ruled a huge empire from Central Asia into northern India.

- Most famous: **Kanishka the Great** (~127-150 CE)
- Capitals: **Purushapura** (modern Peshawar) and **Mathura**
- Patrons of **Buddhism** — Kanishka convened the **Fourth Buddhist Council**
- Sponsored the **Gandhara School of art** — Buddha statues in Greco-Roman style
- Promoted **trade** between India, Central Asia, China, and Rome
- **Silk Route** flourished — Indian goods went west; foreign goods came east

### 5. The Satavahanas (~1st century BCE - 3rd century CE)

The first major dynasty of the **Deccan Plateau**.

- Capital: **Pratishthana** (modern Paithan, Maharashtra)
- Ruled most of the Deccan and parts of central India
- Famous king: **Gautamiputra Satakarni** (early 2nd century CE)
- Patrons of both **Buddhism and Hinduism**
- Promoted Sanskrit and Prakrit literature
- Important for **trade with Rome** — Roman gold coins found across Deccan sites

## The Pattern of Foreign Invasions

A striking feature of this period: **many foreign peoples invaded India** — Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Parthians, Kushans, and Hunas (later).

But here's what's fascinating — **almost none of them stayed foreign**. Within a few generations, they:

- Adopted Indian names
- Married into Indian families
- Followed Indian religions (often Buddhism or Hinduism)
- Patronised Indian art
- Spoke Indian languages
- Became part of Indian society

**Menander** the Greek king became Buddhist. **Kanishka** the Kushan emperor was a great Buddhist patron. **Rudradaman** the Shaka king wrote in Sanskrit. The Shakas eventually became fully Indian — many Indian communities today trace ancestry to them.

This is called **assimilation** — when one group blends into another's culture. Indian civilisation has shown a remarkable ability to **absorb invaders rather than be destroyed by them**.

## Cultural Confluence — Blending of Traditions

This period saw extraordinary **cultural blending**:

### Gandhara Art

Combined **Greek techniques** (realistic human bodies, flowing robes) with **Indian themes** (the Buddha, bodhisattvas, Jataka stories). The first **statues of the Buddha** were carved in Gandhara — before this, Buddhists used symbols (a tree, a footprint) rather than human images.

### Sanskrit Inscriptions

The **Junagadh inscription of Rudradaman** is one of the earliest long inscriptions in Sanskrit. Before this, Prakrit dominated. This marked the beginning of **Sanskrit's revival** in inscriptions.

### Trade Networks

The Kushans connected India to the **Silk Route**. Indian silk, spices, and gemstones travelled to Rome and China. Roman gold poured into India. Buddhism spread to Central Asia, China, Korea, Japan.

### New Coinage

Indo-Greeks introduced **portrait coins** — kings' faces in profile, names in Greek and Indian scripts. Indian dynasties adopted this style. Modern coins still use the basic format.

### Religious Tolerance

Most rulers of this period — Buddhist, Hindu, or otherwise — patronised multiple religions. The Indian tradition of religious tolerance continued.

## The Sangam Age in the South

While north India saw the Age of Reorganisation, **south India** had its own remarkable era — the **Sangam Age** (roughly 3rd century BCE to 3rd century CE).

**Three Tamil dynasties** dominated:
- **Cholas** (capital Uraiyur, then Kaveripattanam)
- **Cheras** (capital Vanji)
- **Pandyas** (capital Madurai)

Together they patronised the **Sangam Literature** — one of the world's great ancient bodies of poetry. The Sangam poems describe **love, war, kings, farms, sailors, and daily life** in vivid detail.

**Notable features of Sangam society**:
- Active **maritime trade** with Rome, Egypt, Southeast Asia
- Strong **literary culture** — over 2,000 Sangam poems survive
- Distinction between **Marudam** (farmlands), **Mullai** (forests), **Kurinji** (hills), **Neydal** (coasts), **Palai** (deserts) — the five tinai we learnt in Class 6
- Independent of north Indian empires

The Sangam Age is a reminder that **Indian history isn't only the history of north India**. The south had parallel kingdoms, parallel achievements, parallel literature — equally important to the Indian story.

## Why This Period Matters

The Age of Reorganisation is sometimes overlooked because it's "between" the famous Mauryans and Guptas. But it was crucial:

1. **Cultural blending** — Greek + Indian + Central Asian art and ideas
2. **Spread of Buddhism** abroad — to China, Tibet, Korea, Japan
3. **Trade boom** — India connected to global networks (Silk Route, Indian Ocean)
4. **First Buddha statues** — Gandhara school
5. **Sanskrit revival** in inscriptions
6. **Religious tolerance** as Indian norm
7. **Sangam literature** — Tamil's classical age
8. **Demonstrated India's ability to absorb invasions** while remaining Indian

## Before We Close

- After the Mauryans fell in **185 BCE**, India entered a 500-year **Age of Reorganisation**
- Five major powers: **Shungas, Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Kushans, Satavahanas**
- The Mauryan commander **Pushyamitra Shunga** assassinated the last Mauryan and founded the Shunga dynasty
- **Foreign invaders** (Greeks, Shakas, Kushans) entered India but **assimilated** into Indian society
- **Greco-Buddhist art (Gandhara school)** — first statues of the Buddha
- **Kushan emperor Kanishka** was a great Buddhist patron and trade promoter
- **Satavahanas** ruled the Deccan and traded with Rome
- In the south, the **Sangam Age** flourished under Cholas, Cheras, Pandyas
- The period demonstrated India's remarkable capacity for **cultural assimilation and renewal**
