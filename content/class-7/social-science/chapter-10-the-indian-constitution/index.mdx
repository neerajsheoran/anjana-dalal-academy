## A Republic Day Morning

Every **26th January**, a Republic Day Parade winds down Kartavya Path in New Delhi. The President takes the salute. Tanks roll past. Children dance, marching bands play, the tricolour goes up. Cameras capture all of it for hundreds of millions watching at home.

But what is this day really about?

It marks the day in **1950** when the **Constitution of India** came into effect. On that day, India became a **Republic** — a country governed by its own laws, made by its own people, not by any foreign power and not by any king.

The Constitution itself is preserved in a **helium-filled glass case** in Parliament. Helium is an inert gas — it doesn't react with paper or ink. Inside that case is the actual handwritten, hand-illustrated Constitution, preserved like a national treasure.

Because that is what it is.

## What is a Constitution?

A **constitution** is the **basic rulebook of a country**. It lays out:

1. **What kind of government the country will have** (democracy, republic, etc.)
2. **The three organs of government** — legislature, executive, judiciary — and what each does
3. **Checks and balances** between the three organs
4. **The rights of citizens** — what the state cannot do to you
5. **The duties of citizens** — what you owe to the country
6. **The values and goals the country is committed to** — equality, justice, freedom, fraternity

Think of a sports tournament. There is always a **rulebook**. When a dispute arises, the referee opens the rulebook and reads aloud what it says. Both teams accept it. Without a rulebook, every match would end in arguments.

A country is the same. Without a constitution, every dispute about who can do what would have no answer. With one, there is a shared document everyone agrees to.

## How the Indian Constitution Was Made

India's Constitution was not written by one person or one party. It was written by a **Constituent Assembly** — a group elected specifically for this purpose.

### Timeline

- **9 December 1946** — Constituent Assembly first met (before independence!)
- **15 August 1947** — India became independent
- **26 November 1949** — Constitution adopted by the Assembly
- **26 January 1950** — Constitution came into effect

The work took almost **three years** — about 165 days of actual sitting time, with deep debate on every clause.

### Who Wrote It?

The Constituent Assembly initially had **389 members**, reduced to **299 after Partition**. These members represented every region, every major language, every major religion, every major social group. **15 women** were members — including Sarojini Naidu, Vijayalakshmi Pandit, Hansa Mehta, and Durgabai Deshmukh.

Key people:
- **Dr. Rajendra Prasad** — Chairman of the Constituent Assembly (later India's first President)
- **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** — Chairman of the Drafting Committee (the chief architect of the Constitution)
- **Jawaharlal Nehru** — moved the Objectives Resolution
- **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** — handled fundamental rights and minorities
- **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad** — represented Muslim interests with grace
- **Dr. Sachidananda Sinha** — first temporary President of the Assembly
- **Sir B.N. Rau** — Constitutional Adviser who prepared early drafts

### Dr. B.R. Ambedkar — Chief Architect

**B.R. Ambedkar** deserves special mention. Born into a Dalit family that faced terrible discrimination, he overcame extraordinary obstacles to become one of the most educated Indians of his time — with degrees from Mumbai, Columbia University (USA), and the London School of Economics.

He understood constitutions, comparative law, economics, and Indian society deeply. As Chairman of the Drafting Committee, he led the writing of the actual text. He fought to ensure the Constitution protected:
- **Equal rights for women**
- **Abolition of untouchability**
- **Reservations for Dalits and tribals**
- **Religious freedom for all**
- **A strong, federal but united India**

Without Ambedkar, the Indian Constitution would not be what it is. He is rightly honoured today as **the father of the Indian Constitution**.

## What Shaped Our Constitution

The Constitution was not made in a vacuum. It drew on three big sources:

### 1. The Indian Freedom Movement

The struggle against British rule had **forged a set of ideals** over 60+ years:
- **Equality** — all Indians equal, regardless of caste, religion, gender
- **Justice** — both social and economic
- **Freedom** — speech, religion, movement, association
- **Fraternity** — Indians as one people
- **Self-rule** — power from the people, not from any foreign throne

Leaders like **Gandhi, Tilak, Tagore, Nehru, Patel, Ambedkar, Azad, Bhagat Singh** had spent their lives fighting for these. The Constitution turned their fight into law.

### 2. India's Own Civilisational Heritage

The Constitution drew on **Indian thought going back thousands of years**:
- **Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam** — "The world is one family" (from the Maha Upanishad)
- **Sarve Bhavantu Sukhinah** — "May all be happy" (an ancient Vedic prayer)
- **Acceptance of different viewpoints** — India's tradition of pluralism in religion and philosophy
- **Respect for women** — though imperfectly practised, was an ideal in Indian thought
- **Rajadharma** — the moral limits on rulers
- **Village councils (panchayats)** — democracy at the grassroots
- **The Uttaramerur tradition** — anti-corruption, accountable representatives

These ideas, sometimes expressed in modern legal language, run through the Constitution. The famous **bull capital from Sarnath** — the four-lion symbol of Ashoka — became India's national emblem. The wheel from that capital (the **Ashoka Chakra**) sits on the Indian flag. India is rooted in its own past, even as it embraces the modern world.

### 3. Lessons from Other Countries

The Constituent Assembly also studied **constitutions of other countries** carefully:
- **United Kingdom** — parliamentary system, single citizenship, rule of law
- **United States** — fundamental rights, independent judiciary, written constitution
- **Ireland** — directive principles of state policy
- **Canada** — federal system with strong centre
- **Australia** — concurrent list, freedom of trade between states
- **Germany** — emergency provisions (with safeguards)
- **South Africa** — procedure for amendment
- **Soviet Union** — fundamental duties (added later in 1976)

This was not copying — it was **learning from the best**. India's framers picked what worked, adapted it for Indian conditions, and combined it with Indian traditions. The result is a unique document, unmistakably Indian.

## Key Features of the Indian Constitution

The Indian Constitution has **25 parts** and **12 schedules** — the **longest written constitution in the world**. Here are its main features.

### 1. The Preamble — A Mission Statement

The Constitution begins with a **Preamble** — a short, beautiful paragraph that declares what kind of country India will be:

> "WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens:
> JUSTICE, social, economic and political;
> LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship;
> EQUALITY of status and of opportunity;
> and to promote among them all
> FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation;
> IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION."

Look at the words: **Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic**. Each carries deep meaning. The Preamble is short but it captures India's soul.

### 2. Fundamental Rights

The Constitution guarantees **six fundamental rights** to every citizen:

1. **Right to Equality** — equal before the law, no discrimination by caste, religion, gender, etc.
2. **Right to Freedom** — speech, movement, profession, assembly
3. **Right against Exploitation** — no forced labour, no trafficking, no child labour
4. **Right to Freedom of Religion** — practise any religion (or none)
5. **Cultural and Educational Rights** — minorities can preserve language and culture
6. **Right to Constitutional Remedies** — if any right is violated, you can go to the Supreme Court

These rights cannot be taken away by the government. Even Parliament cannot remove them. The Supreme Court can strike down any law that violates them.

### 3. Directive Principles of State Policy

These are **goals the government should work towards**:
- A just social order
- Adequate livelihood for all
- Equal pay for equal work
- Free and compulsory education for children
- Protection of the environment
- Promotion of cottage industries
- Promotion of international peace

These are **not enforceable** by courts, but they guide all policy. Every government, regardless of party, is expected to work towards them.

### 4. Fundamental Duties

Added in 1976 (the 42nd Amendment), these are duties of every citizen:
- Respect the Constitution and the national flag
- Cherish freedom and the ideals of the freedom movement
- Protect India's unity and integrity
- Defend the country
- Promote harmony, brotherhood, and the dignity of women
- Protect the environment
- Develop the scientific temper
- Safeguard public property
- Strive for excellence

### 5. Federal Structure

India is a **federation** — power is divided between the **Central (Union) Government** and the **State Governments**. The Seventh Schedule lists three "lists":
- **Union List** — subjects only the centre handles (defence, foreign affairs, currency)
- **State List** — subjects only states handle (police, public health, agriculture)
- **Concurrent List** — subjects both can handle (education, criminal law)

This balance lets India be **one country** while letting **states make their own decisions** on local matters.

### 6. Independent Judiciary

The Constitution creates the **Supreme Court** and **High Courts** with strong protections:
- Judges cannot be easily removed
- Their salaries are protected
- They can strike down unconstitutional laws

This independence is what allows courts to protect rights even against the government.

### 7. Universal Adult Franchise

Every Indian adult — regardless of gender, caste, religion, wealth, or education — has the right to **vote**. This was revolutionary in 1950 when many democracies still restricted voting.

### 8. Secular State

India has **no official religion**. The state respects all religions equally. Citizens are free to follow any faith (or none). Religious institutions cannot dictate state laws.

### 9. Amendment Procedure

The Constitution can be **amended** (changed) but **not easily**. Different types of amendments need different majorities — some need a 2/3 majority of Parliament, some also need approval from half the states.

This means the Constitution is a **living document** — it can adapt to new times — but its basic structure cannot be casually overturned.

### 10. The Basic Structure Doctrine

In a 1973 case (Kesavananda Bharati case), the Supreme Court ruled that even Parliament cannot amend the **basic structure** of the Constitution — features like democracy, secularism, federal structure, judicial review, and rule of law.

This was a brilliant move. It protects the Constitution from being overturned even by a powerful Parliament.

## Why the Constitution Still Matters

The Constitution was written **77 years ago** (as of 2027). Many things have changed since 1950 — technology, society, the world.

Yet the Constitution remains **alive and relevant**. Why?

1. **It was written wisely** — the framers anticipated many problems.
2. **It is broad** — its principles are timeless even if specific provisions need updating.
3. **It can be amended** — 105+ amendments so far have kept it current.
4. **The Supreme Court protects its core** — courts strike down laws that violate it.
5. **Citizens know it** — even if not in detail, Indians know they have rights, that judges can be appealed to, that elections matter.

The Constitution is **India's binding agreement with itself**. Every government, every politician, every citizen is bound by it. It is what makes India a republic — not a kingdom, not a dictatorship, not a theocracy, but a country governed by laws of its own making.

## Before We Close

- A **constitution** is a country's basic rulebook — it lays out government structure, citizen rights, and national values.
- The **Constitution of India** came into effect on **26 January 1950** — celebrated as Republic Day.
- It was written by a **Constituent Assembly** of 299 members over nearly **3 years**.
- **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** chaired the Drafting Committee and is honoured as its chief architect.
- **15 women** were members — including Sarojini Naidu, Vijayalakshmi Pandit, Hansa Mehta.
- The Constitution drew from (1) the **Indian freedom movement**, (2) **Indian civilisational heritage**, (3) **other countries' constitutions**.
- It is the **world's longest written constitution** — 25 parts, 12 schedules.
- The **Preamble** captures its soul: Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic; Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity.
- It guarantees **fundamental rights**, sets out **directive principles**, and lists **fundamental duties**.
- It creates a **federal structure** with division between centre and states.
- It establishes an **independent judiciary** and **universal adult franchise**.
- The **basic structure doctrine** protects core features from even constitutional amendments.
- The Constitution is preserved in a **helium-filled glass case** in Parliament — a national treasure.
