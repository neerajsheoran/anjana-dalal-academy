## A Country That Has Welcomed Many

Long before "asylum policy" or "refugee convention" were modern concepts, India was already doing what they describe: **giving shelter to people fleeing persecution**.

For over 2,000 years, communities from around the world have arrived in India — fleeing wars, religious oppression, ethnic violence. Many have stayed, become Indian citizens, and added to the country's diversity.

This is not just a historical curiosity. It is a **defining feature of Indian civilisation**. As **Swami Vivekananda** said at the **World Parliament of Religions** in Chicago in 1893:

> "I am proud to belong to a nation which has sheltered the persecuted and the refugees of all religions and all nations of the Earth. I am proud to tell you that we have gathered in our bosom the purest remnant of the Israelites, who came to Southern India and took refuge with us in the very year in which their holy temple was shattered to pieces by Roman tyranny. I am proud to belong to the religion which has sheltered and is still fostering the remnant of the grand Zoroastrian nation."

In this chapter, we will look at the major communities that came to India seeking refuge — and at the Indian habit of acceptance that made it possible.

## The Bene Israel — Jews on the Konkan Coast

The **Bene Israel** ("Sons of Israel") are one of the oldest Jewish communities in the world living outside Israel.

### The Tradition

According to community tradition, the Bene Israel came to India around **175 BCE** — escaping the persecution of Jews in the ancient Kingdom of Israel by foreign rulers. Their ship was caught in a storm and **shipwrecked on the Konkan coast**, just south of present-day Mumbai. The survivors swam ashore and settled.

They lost most of their religious texts in the wreck. But they remembered:
- **The Shema** — the central Jewish prayer ("Hear, O Israel: the Lord our God, the Lord is One")
- The **Sabbath** (a day of rest)
- **Circumcision** of male children
- Some dietary laws

For about **2,000 years**, the Bene Israel lived in coastal Maharashtra villages, mainly as **oil-pressers** (called "Shanwar Telis" — "Saturday oil-pressers" — because they kept the Sabbath on Saturday).

### Becoming Indian

They:
- Adopted Marathi as their first language
- Wore Indian clothes
- Had Indian family names (often village-based, like Pendharkar, Akbarkar)
- Practised Jewish religion in a distinctly Indian form
- Built **synagogues** (Jewish places of worship) across Maharashtra

### Reconnection with Judaism

In the **18th century**, Jewish travellers and missionaries from Iraq, Yemen, and Britain encountered the Bene Israel. They were amazed to find a thriving Jewish community in India that had preserved its identity for 2,000 years.

They helped the Bene Israel:
- Reconnect with Hebrew scriptures
- Learn more about Jewish law
- Build more synagogues
- Strengthen religious education

### After 1948

After Israel was created in 1948, many Bene Israel **emigrated to Israel**. Today, there are over 60,000 Bene Israel in Israel — more than remaining in India.

The **Bene Israel community in India** today numbers a few thousand, mostly in Mumbai. They maintain several active synagogues.

## The Cochin Jews

A second wave of Jews came to India later — settling in **Kerala** (then called **Malabar**).

These were Jews fleeing persecution in West Asia, the Iberian Peninsula (after the 1492 expulsion of Jews from Spain), and other places. They arrived between roughly the **9th and 16th centuries** at the port of **Cranganore** (now Kodungallur) and later **Cochin**.

### Royal Welcome

The Hindu Raja of Kochi welcomed the Jews and granted them:
- Land for settlement
- Religious freedom
- Trading privileges

A bronze plaque from the Raja Bhaskara Ravivarma (10th century CE) granted Joseph Rabban (a Jewish leader) special privileges — **"as long as the world, sun, and moon endure."** This plaque, called the **Jewish Copper Plates**, is one of the most cherished documents in the community's history.

The Jews settled in **"Jew Town"** (Jewish quarter) in Mattancherry, Kochi. They built the **Paradesi Synagogue** in 1568 — still standing today.

### Becoming Indian

Like the Bene Israel, the Cochin Jews:
- Adopted Malayalam as their main language
- Wore Indian dress
- Followed Indian customs in many areas
- But preserved Jewish religion through generations

### Today

After 1948, most Cochin Jews emigrated to Israel. Only a handful remain in Kochi today — perhaps a dozen.

The **Paradesi Synagogue** remains a UNESCO heritage site. The Maharaja of Travancore gave the synagogue a beautiful gift — an elaborate **gold and silver crown for the Torah** (Jewish holy scroll). This shows how the local rulers honoured the Jewish community.

## The Baghdadi Jews

A third wave of Jews came in the **18th and 19th centuries** — from Iraq, Syria, and Iran. They settled mainly in **Kolkata and Mumbai**.

Among the most famous: **the Sassoon family**, who came from Baghdad. They built large fortunes through trade and:
- Built **synagogues** in Mumbai (Magen David, Knesseth Eliyahoo)
- Funded **schools, hospitals, and libraries** in Mumbai
- Built the famous **Sassoon Docks** in Mumbai (still operating)
- Created the **Sassoon Library**
- Made philanthropic contributions across India

Most Baghdadi Jews also emigrated to Israel, the UK, USA, and Australia after 1948.

## The Syriac (or Syrian) Christians of Kerala

Long before British missionaries arrived in India, **Christianity** was already well-established in Kerala — for **over 1,900 years**.

### The Saint Thomas Tradition

According to ancient tradition, the **Apostle Thomas** (one of Jesus's twelve disciples) came to India in **52 CE**. He landed in **Muziris** (modern Kodungallur) and preached the Gospel along the Malabar Coast.

He converted local people, founded **seven churches**, and was eventually martyred in **Mylapore** (now part of Chennai) around 72 CE.

While historians debate whether Thomas himself came, **early Christian presence in Kerala by the 1st-2nd centuries CE is well documented**.

### The Syriac Connection

Some Christian groups in West Asia used the **Syriac language** (a form of Aramaic, the language Jesus spoke) for worship. From the **4th century CE onwards**, some of them faced persecution in the Persian and Roman Empires.

They migrated along trade routes to **Malabar coast**, where they found:
- A welcoming local society
- Existing Christian community (the Thomas Christians)
- Freedom to practise their faith
- Trade opportunities (Indian spices, peppers, gemstones)

The Syriac Christians integrated with the existing Indian Christian community, adopting Malayalam, becoming part of Kerala society.

### Indian Christian Society

Indian Christianity:
- Used Syriac in liturgy (and still does in some churches today)
- Had its own bishops
- Was protected by local Hindu rulers
- Practised traditions distinct from Roman Catholicism
- Was a respected part of Kerala's social fabric

When the Portuguese arrived in Kerala in 1498, they were astonished to find a flourishing Christian community already there — Indian, Syriac-speaking, with its own traditions.

The Portuguese tried to bring these Christians under Roman Catholic control, which caused conflicts. Today, Kerala has multiple Christian denominations — Roman Catholic, Syro-Malabar, Syro-Malankara, Orthodox, Marthoma, Pentecostal, etc.

But the original tradition — Indian Christians who trace their faith to Saint Thomas — continues.

## The Parsis — Refugees from Persia

The **Parsis** (also called Zoroastrians) are followers of an ancient Iranian religion founded by the prophet **Zarathustra (Zoroaster)** around 1500-1000 BCE.

For over 1,000 years, Zoroastrianism was the **state religion of Persia (Iran)**. It influenced Greek philosophy, Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.

But in the **7th century CE**, Arab Muslim armies conquered Persia. Many Zoroastrians converted to Islam (some forcibly). Others held on to their faith despite persecution.

### Arrival in India

Around the **8th-10th centuries CE**, a group of Persian Zoroastrians fled Iran by ship. After difficult journeys, they landed at **Sanjan** in Gujarat.

### The Famous Sugar Story

A beautiful legend describes their reception:

When the Parsi leader **Jadi Rana** asked the local Hindu king for refuge, the king sent him a bowl of milk filled to the brim — meaning: "Our country is already full. We have no place for more people."

The Parsi leader **stirred sugar into the milk**. The milk didn't overflow — the sugar dissolved and made the milk **sweeter**.

His message: "We will not displace anyone. We will dissolve into your society and add sweetness."

The king allowed them to settle. The Parsis kept their promise:
- Adopted Gujarati as their language
- Wore Indian clothes
- Followed Indian customs in social life
- But preserved Zoroastrian religion strictly

### Becoming Indian — and World-class

Over centuries, Parsis spread to Mumbai and other cities. Despite their small numbers (about **60,000-70,000** worldwide today, most in India), they have produced:

- **Industrialists**: The **Tata family** (TCS, Tata Motors, Tata Steel, etc.) — Jamsetji Tata, J.R.D. Tata, Ratan Tata
- **Other industrialists**: Godrej, Wadia (Bombay Dyeing), Mistry (Shapoorji Pallonji)
- **Politicians**: **Dadabhai Naoroji** (first Indian member of British Parliament), **Bhikaji Cama** (revolutionary), **Pherozeshah Mehta** (Congress founder)
- **Scientists**: **Homi Bhabha** (father of India's nuclear programme), **Homi Sethna**
- **Lawyers**: **Fali Nariman**, **Soli Sorabjee**
- **Conductors**: **Zubin Mehta** (world-famous orchestra conductor)
- **Cricketers**: many in early Indian cricket
- **Writers**: Bapsi Sidhwa, Rohinton Mistry, Cyrus Mistry

The Parsi contribution to modern India is **vastly disproportionate** to their numbers.

### Decline in Numbers

Parsis face a demographic challenge — strict rules against intermarriage, low birth rates, and emigration have caused their population to decline. The community is working to preserve its heritage.

## The Tibetans — Refugees of the 20th Century

A more recent group of refugees: **Tibetans** fleeing Chinese rule.

### Background

**Tibet** had been an independent or autonomous region for centuries, with its own Buddhist culture, Dalai Lamas, and traditions. In 1950, China invaded and occupied Tibet. By 1959, conditions had become impossible for many Tibetans.

In **March 1959**, the **14th Dalai Lama** (the religious and political leader of Tibet) fled to India along with tens of thousands of his followers.

### India's Response

India, led by Prime Minister Nehru, granted asylum to the Dalai Lama. He set up the **Tibetan Government in Exile** in **Dharamsala** (Himachal Pradesh). India provided land, settlements, schools, monasteries.

About **100,000 Tibetans** live in India today, in settlements in:
- **Dharamsala** (Himachal Pradesh) — the main centre
- **Bylakuppe** (Karnataka) — large settlement
- **Mundgod** (Karnataka)
- **Several others** across India

### Tibetan Contributions to India

The Tibetans have:
- Preserved Tibetan Buddhism (which was nearly destroyed in Tibet itself)
- Built beautiful monasteries in India
- Trained thousands of monks
- Spread Tibetan medicine and astrology
- Strengthened India's spiritual tourism
- Built schools and cultural centres

The **Dalai Lama** has been a global ambassador for peace, dialogue, and Buddhist values. He won the **Nobel Peace Prize** in 1989. He is one of the most respected spiritual figures in the world — and has lived in India for over 65 years.

### A Note of Caution

India's hosting of the Tibetans has cost diplomatically — China resents India's role. But India has maintained its commitment despite Chinese pressure. This shows that the Indian tradition of welcoming refugees continues into the modern era.

## Other Communities

India has welcomed many other communities over centuries:

### Sri Lankan Tamils
During Sri Lanka's civil war (1983-2009), about 100,000 Sri Lankan Tamils took refuge in Tamil Nadu. India provided camps, schooling, healthcare. Some have returned to Sri Lanka; many remain in India.

### Bangladeshis
After the 1971 war of liberation, India absorbed about 10 million Bangladeshi refugees (the largest single-event migration into India). Most returned after independence. Some remained.

### Afghans
Over the past 40+ years, waves of Afghans have fled war and Taliban rule, with significant numbers settling in Delhi and other cities.

### Burmese
Refugees from Myanmar's military repression have come to India, especially the Northeast and Delhi.

### Bahais
The Bahai Faith, founded in 19th-century Persia, faces persecution in Iran. India has welcomed Bahais; the famous **Lotus Temple** in Delhi (a Bahai house of worship) is one of India's most visited buildings.

### Armenians
Small but ancient Armenian Christian community in India (Kolkata, Surat, Chennai) since the 16th century — fleeing Ottoman persecution.

### Anglo-Indians
Mixed-heritage communities (British-Indian) — though not exactly refugees, they have been an important Indian community for over 200 years.

## What This Tells Us

India's history of welcoming refugees teaches several important things:

### 1. Indian Society Was Open

Throughout history, Indian society was generally open to outsiders who came peacefully. As long as new arrivals didn't try to conquer or impose, they were given space to live, work, worship.

This wasn't accidental. It reflects deep features of Indian thought:
- **Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam** — "The world is one family"
- **Sarve Bhavantu Sukhinah** — "May all be happy"
- **Religious pluralism** — many paths to truth
- **Hospitality (Atithi Devo Bhava)** — "The guest is god"

### 2. Refugees Enriched India

Every refugee community brought something to India:
- Jews — trade, scholarship, philanthropy
- Syrian Christians — religious tradition, distinctive culture
- Parsis — entrepreneurship, integrity, modern industry
- Tibetans — spiritual depth, Buddhist preservation
- Others — varied contributions

India was strengthened, not weakened, by these arrivals.

### 3. Integration Worked

Most refugee communities:
- Adopted local languages
- Adapted to Indian customs
- Contributed to local economies
- Lived peacefully with neighbours
- Preserved their own religion and culture
- Did not seek special privileges

The model: **integration without forced assimilation**. Be Indian in social life, retain your distinct religious/cultural identity.

### 4. Local Rulers Often Helped

Across history, local Indian rulers — Hindu kings, Muslim sultans, princely rajahs, modern governments — gave refuge to fleeing communities. This shows that hospitality was a **shared Indian value**, not just one religion's.

### 5. India Continues This Tradition

In the modern era, India has:
- Welcomed Tibetans (1959 onwards)
- Absorbed Bangladeshi refugees (1971)
- Hosted Sri Lankan Tamils (1983-2009)
- Provided shelter to Afghans, Burmese, Bahais
- Often supported the most vulnerable

India is one of the few countries that has consistently been open to refugees throughout its history.

### 6. The Limits

Not everything has been perfect:
- India hasn't signed the 1951 UN Refugee Convention
- Refugees sometimes face bureaucratic difficulties
- Anti-immigrant sentiment exists in some places
- Politics of citizenship has become contentious recently

But on the whole, India's record on accepting refugees is one of the most positive in human history.
