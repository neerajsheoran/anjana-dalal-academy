## Every Country Needs a Government — But Whose Government?

We saw in Class 6 that every group of humans, from a school to a country, needs **rules** and **someone to make and enforce them**. That "someone" — the system that runs a country — is the **government**.

But here is the deeper question: **who decides who governs?**

- In one country, the people vote to choose their leaders.
- In another, a king rules because his father was king.
- In a third, religious leaders make the laws.
- In a fourth, a single person rules by force.

These are different **types of government**. They are not equally good. They give very different lives to the people who live under them. Understanding them helps us appreciate why democracy — though imperfect — is precious.

## What Does a Government Actually Do?

Before comparing types, let us remember the **functions** of any government:

1. **Maintain law and order** — police, courts, prisons
2. **Defend the country** — armed forces, borders, foreign policy
3. **Deliver essential services** — schools, hospitals, roads, water
4. **Manage the economy** — currency, banks, trade
5. **Look after the welfare of the people** — pensions, support for the poor, disaster relief
6. **Manage relations with other countries** — embassies, treaties

To do all this, a government has **three organs** (Class 6 chapter):
- **Legislature** — makes laws
- **Executive** — carries out laws
- **Judiciary** — decides who has broken laws

Different types of government organise these three organs in different ways.

## What Makes Governments Different?

Four big questions tell us what kind of government a country has:

1. **Who decides this is the government?** (Where does its authority come from?)
2. **How is the government formed?** (Elections? Inheritance? Force?)
3. **How are the three organs structured?** (Are they independent, or controlled by one person?)
4. **What goals is the government working for?** (Equality? Religion? One family's power?)

Let us use these questions as we look at each type.

## Democracy — Rule of the People

The word **democracy** comes from Greek: **demos** (people) + **kratos** (power) = "rule of the people."

In a democracy:
- The **source of authority** is the people of the country
- The government is formed through **elections**
- The three organs are usually **separate** (separation of powers)
- The goal is the **welfare and equality** of all citizens

But democracy is not just one thing. It comes in several forms.

### Direct Democracy

In **direct democracy**, all citizens vote on every important decision themselves. There are no representatives.

This worked in **small ancient city-states** like some Greek polis. Today it still works in some Swiss cantons, where citizens vote frequently on local laws.

**The limit:** direct democracy is impossible at large scale. Imagine 140 crore Indians trying to vote on every law. We would never decide anything.

### Representative Democracy

In **representative democracy**, citizens elect representatives, and those representatives make laws and run the country on their behalf.

This is what India has. We elect:
- **Members of Parliament (MPs)** to the Lok Sabha
- **Members of Legislative Assembly (MLAs)** to state Vidhan Sabhas
- **Councillors** to municipal corporations

Most modern democracies are representative.

### Parliamentary Democracy

Within representative democracy, there are two main models. **Parliamentary democracy** is one.

In a parliamentary democracy:
- People vote for the **legislature** (Parliament).
- The **leader of the majority party** in the legislature becomes the **head of government** — called the Prime Minister.
- The PM and the Council of Ministers are also **members of the legislature**.
- The PM stays in power as long as the legislature has **confidence** in them. If the legislature votes "no confidence," the PM must resign.

**Examples:** India, UK, Australia, Canada, Germany.

In India, the PM is the real head of government. The President is the **nominal head** (a ceremonial position).

### Presidential Democracy

In a **presidential democracy**:
- People vote for the **legislature** separately.
- People also vote for the **President** directly.
- The President is the head of government and is NOT a member of the legislature.
- The President serves a fixed term (e.g., 4 years in the USA) regardless of legislative support.

**Examples:** USA, South Korea, Brazil.

This system separates legislative and executive powers more strictly. The advantage: clear separation. The disadvantage: deadlock is possible if the President and legislature disagree.

## Fundamental Principles of Democracy

Whatever the form, all true democracies share these principles:

- **Equality** — every person is equal before the law
- **Freedom** — people can speak, think, write, worship as they choose
- **Universal adult franchise** — every adult citizen has the right to vote, regardless of caste, gender, religion, or wealth
- **Free and fair elections** — held regularly, without rigging
- **Fundamental rights** — protected by the constitution
- **Independent judiciary** — courts not controlled by the government
- **Accountability** — the government must answer to the people

### A Note on Voting Rights

These principles took **centuries** to develop, even in modern democracies:
- **USA** — black men got voting rights in 1870 (in theory), but effective voting rights only in 1965
- **UK** — full universal suffrage only in 1928
- **Switzerland** — women got voting rights only in **1971**!
- **India** — gave universal adult suffrage to all citizens from the **very first election in 1951–52**, regardless of gender, caste, religion, or property. This was a huge achievement — many countries took 100+ years to do what India did from day one.

## Monarchy — Rule of a King or Queen

A **monarchy** is a government where a king or queen — a **monarch** — holds power. Monarchs are usually **hereditary** — they inherit the throne from their parents.

Monarchies were the most common form of government in history. Most ancient empires — Mauryan, Gupta, Chola, Mughal, Ottoman, Roman — were monarchies.

### Absolute Monarchy

In an **absolute monarchy**, the king has total power. He makes laws, enforces them, and decides court cases. There is no constitution limiting him.

**Modern examples:** Saudi Arabia, Eswatini (Swaziland), some Gulf states.

In Saudi Arabia, the king governs according to Islamic law. He appoints advisers but is not bound to follow their advice.

### Constitutional Monarchy

In a **constitutional monarchy**, the country still has a king or queen — but **the king has only ceremonial power**. Real power lies with the elected legislature and a Prime Minister.

**Modern examples:** United Kingdom, Japan, Spain, Sweden, Norway, Netherlands, Thailand.

King Charles III of the UK has no real power to make laws. The British Parliament and Prime Minister run the country. The king is a **symbol**, not a ruler. Constitutional monarchy combines tradition (the monarchy) with democracy (real power with the elected legislature).

### The Indian Tradition — Rajadharma

Indian kingdoms were monarchies, but they had an interesting feature: the king was bound by **rajadharma** — the duties of a king.

In the Mahabharata's Shanti Parva, the dying warrior Bhishma teaches Yudhishthira:
- A king is not just a ruler — the **welfare of his people** is his highest priority
- He must apply the law without bias
- He must not get attached to power, as it fosters ego and corruption
- He must seek guidance from wise advisers
- His authority is **temporary and bound by dharma**

This is profound: in classical India, kingship came with **moral responsibility**. A king who failed in rajadharma was considered illegitimate.

There is a story from the **Rajatarangini** (a 12th-century Kashmiri history) about King Chandrapida wanting to build a temple. A cobbler's hut stood in the way. The king ordered measurements taken, but the cobbler refused to allow it. The king's officers complained — but the king sided with the cobbler! He said his officers should have asked first. He himself went to the cobbler, asked respectfully, and paid for the land.

The message: **even a king cannot take what is not his**. Dharma applies to everyone — including the king. This idea, that **power is limited by morality**, is one of India's greatest political contributions.

## Theocracy — Rule by Religion

A **theocracy** is a government where religious law is the highest law, and religious leaders hold political power.

In a theocracy:
- The **constitution is based on religious texts**
- Religious leaders have **veto power** over laws
- Citizens are expected to follow religious rules
- Other religions may face restrictions

**Modern examples:**
- **Iran** — the Islamic Republic of Iran combines theocracy with limited democracy. There is an elected president and parliament, but the **Supreme Leader** (a senior religious cleric) has ultimate authority.
- **Afghanistan** — under the Taliban
- **Vatican City** — ruled by the Pope (a tiny city-state in Rome)

Theocracies can give a sense of moral order, but they often **restrict freedoms** — especially for women, religious minorities, and those who question religious orthodoxy.

## Dictatorship — Rule by One Person or a Small Group

A **dictatorship** is a government where a single person or a small group holds absolute power — without legal limits, without elections, without freedom for citizens.

In a dictatorship:
- Power is held by **force**, not consent
- There are **no real elections** (or elections are rigged)
- The **legislature is a rubber stamp** for the dictator
- The **judiciary is controlled** by the dictator
- **Media is censored**
- **Opposition is jailed or killed**

The 20th century saw terrible dictatorships:
- **Adolf Hitler** in Germany (1933–1945) — caused World War II and the Holocaust
- **Joseph Stalin** in the Soviet Union (1924–1953) — killed millions of his own people
- **Mao Zedong** in China (1949–1976) — caused famines that killed tens of millions
- **Pol Pot** in Cambodia (1975–1979) — killed about a quarter of Cambodia's population

These regimes show what happens when power is **not limited** by laws, elections, or independent institutions.

## India's Ancient Experiments

Long before modern democracy, India had its own experiments with non-monarchical government.

### Early Republics — The Vajji Mahajanapada

The **Vajji** (or **Vrijji**) mahajanapada in the 6th century BCE was a confederation of clans, including the **Lichchhavis**.

In the Vajji:
- Leaders were chosen **based on merit, not birth**
- Major decisions were made by an **assembly of representatives**
- Different clans sent their representatives to discuss issues
- This is one of the **earliest republics** in human history

Greek and Roman republics existed around the same time, but the Vajji shows that **republican governance was not invented only by the West**. India had its own version.

### The Uttaramerur Inscription — Chola Village Democracy

In the 10th century CE, the **Cholas** had something extraordinary: **detailed rules for village elections**, carved into temple walls.

The **Uttaramerur inscription** in Tamil Nadu (still readable today, on the Vaikuntha Perumal temple) describes:
- **Qualifications** for candidates (age, property, character)
- **Disqualifications** — corrupt people, people who hadn't filed accounts
- The **election process** — ballots were written on palm leaves and placed in pots (a kind of sealed ballot box)
- **A drawing of lots** to choose final members from qualified candidates
- **Specific duties** of elected representatives
- **Removal procedures** for those found guilty of corruption

This is **1,000 years before modern democracy**. The Cholas had figured out:
- Sealed ballots
- Anti-corruption rules
- Audited accounts
- Term limits

What's striking is that this was at the **village level** — democracy at the grassroots, in a kingdom that was politically a monarchy.

So India's tradition has multiple threads:
- **Monarchy** at the top, with kings bound by rajadharma
- **Republics** like the Vajji
- **Village democracy** like Uttaramerur
- **Council-based decision-making** even within kingdoms (the king's council of ministers, the sabha and samiti)

All this happened thousands of years before the modern democratic revolution.

## Why Democracy Matters

Looking at the alternatives — absolute monarchy, theocracy, dictatorship — we can see why democracy is precious.

In a democracy:
- **You can criticise the government** without going to jail
- **You can change the government** through elections, peacefully
- **Your daughter and son have equal rights**
- **Religious minorities are protected**
- **Courts can rule against the government** and the government has to obey
- **You have rights that even the Prime Minister cannot take away**

Democracy is messy. It is slow. It involves arguments, compromise, and frustration. But the alternative is far worse: rule by force, where ordinary people have no protection.

Winston Churchill once said: **"Democracy is the worst form of government — except for all the others."**

India is the **largest democracy in the world**. About **970 million Indians** are eligible to vote. Every five years (or sooner), they get to choose their representatives. That is a precious right — one Indians fought hard to win, and one we must guard for our children.

## Before We Close

- **Government** is the system that makes and enforces rules for a country.
- Governments differ in (1) who gives them authority, (2) how they are formed, (3) how the three organs are structured, (4) what goals they pursue.
- **Democracy** is "rule of the people" — direct in small communities, representative in larger ones.
- **Parliamentary democracies** (India, UK) — the head of government is from the legislature.
- **Presidential democracies** (USA) — head of government is elected separately.
- **Monarchy** is rule by a hereditary king/queen — can be absolute (Saudi Arabia) or constitutional (UK, Japan).
- The Indian tradition of **rajadharma** held kings to moral standards — power had to serve dharma.
- **Theocracy** is rule by religious law (Iran, Vatican City).
- **Dictatorship** is rule by a single person or small group without limits — 20th-century dictatorships caused some of history's worst crimes.
- India had its own ancient experiments: the **Vajji republic** (6th c. BCE) and **Chola village elections** (10th c. CE, Uttaramerur).
- Democracy is **imperfect** but offers the best protection for ordinary people's rights and freedoms.
