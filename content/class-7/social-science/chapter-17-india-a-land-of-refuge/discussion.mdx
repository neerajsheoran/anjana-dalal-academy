## Why Has India Welcomed So Many Refugees?

**Child:** Maa, India has welcomed so many groups over 2,000 years. Why? Most countries close their doors.

**Mother:** A thoughtful question. Several reasons, working together.

**1. Indian thought has been pluralist for thousands of years.**

The deepest Indian philosophy says: **truth is one, but called by many names**. The Rig Veda has the famous line: "Ekam sat, vipra bahudha vadanti" — "Truth is one, the wise call it by many names."

If you believe that, then **other religions are not enemies** — they are different ways to the same truth. So you can welcome them.

This is different from religions that believe theirs is the only true path. Such religions historically had more trouble accepting others.

**2. India is huge and diverse.**

India already had many languages, religions, ethnic groups, even within its own population. Hindus, Buddhists, Jains, tribal religions, regional languages, hundreds of castes and communities. Adding a few refugee groups didn't really change the fundamental diversity. It just added more variety to what was already a varied society.

Compare to small, homogeneous countries. A few thousand refugees can feel like a big disruption. In India, a few thousand refugees blend into the larger mosaic.

**3. Indian rulers benefited from refugees.**

Throughout history, refugee communities have brought:
- **Trading expertise** — Jews, Parsis, Armenians were known for commerce
- **Crafts and skills** — many communities had specialised abilities
- **Wealth** — refugees often arrived with some resources
- **New ideas** — different religious and intellectual traditions
- **International connections** — links to their countries of origin

Smart Indian rulers welcomed them because they **made the kingdom richer**. The Raja of Kochi giving land to Jews "as long as world, sun, and moon endure" — this wasn't just kindness. It was also smart governance. Jewish traders helped Kochi's economy.

**4. The Indian tradition of "atithi devo bhava" — the guest is god.**

This is an ancient Sanskrit teaching from the Taittiriya Upanishad. Indian children grew up hearing: when a guest comes, honour them. Feed them first. Don't ask too many questions about who they are or why they came.

This habit of hospitality, **scaled up to civilisation level**, becomes a policy of welcoming refugees.

**5. India was rarely a conqueror nation.**

Indian civilisation, for most of its history, didn't try to spread itself by force. India didn't have an "expansion policy" that needed to dominate others. So it didn't have the suspicion of outsiders that conquering nations often have.

When you're not trying to take over others, you can be more relaxed about others coming to you.

**6. Religious freedom was widely practised.**

In most Indian kingdoms, different religions coexisted. Hindu kings patronised Buddhist monks. Buddhist kings respected Hindu temples. Muslim sultans often supported Hindu institutions. There was friction, of course, but mostly **coexistence was the norm**.

For refugees from religiously intolerant places, India must have felt like paradise: you could practise your faith openly, build your places of worship, raise your children in your tradition.

**7. Geography helped.**

India is enormous. It had room for many communities to settle in different places. Jews in Mumbai and Kochi. Parsis in Gujarat. Tibetans in the Himalayas. Refugees didn't all crowd into one place — they spread out, found their niches.

**Was India always welcoming?**

Not always. There were:
- **Religious tensions** at various times (between Hindu and Buddhist, Hindu and Muslim, etc.)
- **Caste discrimination** that could affect new arrivals
- **Political conflicts** that displaced people
- **Some closed communities** that didn't integrate well

But on the whole, **the pattern was welcome**. Compare to many other regions of the world where minorities have been expelled, killed, or assimilated — India looks remarkably tolerant.

**Why does this matter today?**

In the 21st century, the world has more refugees than ever. Wars in Ukraine, Syria, Yemen, Afghanistan, Myanmar, Ethiopia, Sudan, Gaza. Climate change is creating new refugees. The numbers are in the tens of millions.

India's tradition has lessons:
- **Welcoming refugees can enrich a country**
- **Diversity can be a strength**
- **Different religions can coexist**
- **Integration works better than forced assimilation**
- **Generous policies build long-term goodwill**

India has not always lived up to its own ideals. There are debates today about refugee policy, citizenship laws, religious tolerance. But the **historical model** — of welcoming refugees and letting them become Indian while keeping their distinct identity — is one of India's gifts to the world.

When you grow up, however you act in your own life, remember: **kindness to strangers is one of India's deepest traditions**. Practise it. Be hospitable. Be open. The visitor at your door might bring a gift you never expected.

## What Is the Story of the Parsis?

**Child:** Maa, the Parsi story is interesting. Such a small community, but so important. Tell me more.

**Mother:** Yes — the Parsis are one of the most remarkable communities in human history. A small group of refugees became one of the most influential communities in modern India. Let me tell their story.

**Background: Zoroastrianism.**

The Parsis follow **Zoroastrianism**, founded by the prophet **Zarathustra (Zoroaster)** in ancient Persia (modern Iran) around 1500-1000 BCE. It is one of the world's oldest religions still practised.

Key Zoroastrian beliefs:
- **One supreme God** — **Ahura Mazda** ("Wise Lord")
- **Cosmic dualism** — eternal struggle between Truth (Asha) and Lie (Druj)
- **Free will** — humans must choose good
- **Final judgment** — souls judged after death
- **Resurrection** — eventual triumph of good over evil

Zoroastrian teachings influenced Judaism, Christianity, Islam, and Greek philosophy. Many ideas Western religions share — heaven and hell, angels, day of judgment — have Zoroastrian roots.

For over 1,000 years, Zoroastrianism was the **state religion of Persia** — the religion of great empires like the Achaemenids, Parthians, and Sasanians.

**The Arab Conquest.**

In the 7th century CE, Arab Muslim armies conquered Persia. Most Persians converted to Islam — some willingly, some under pressure. A minority refused. Over centuries, they faced increasing persecution.

**The Flight to India.**

Around the **8th-10th centuries CE**, a group of these Zoroastrians decided to leave Persia. They sailed across the Arabian Sea and landed at **Sanjan** (in Gujarat).

**The Sugar in Milk Story.**

A beautiful legend describes their welcome.

The Parsi leader, **Dastur** (priest), met the local Hindu king, **Jadi Rana**. He asked for refuge.

The king listened, then sent a bowl of **milk filled to the brim**. His message: "Our country is already full. We have no room for more people."

The Parsi leader thought for a moment. Then he stirred a spoonful of **sugar** into the milk. The milk didn't overflow. The sugar dissolved.

His message back: "We will not displace anyone. We will dissolve into your society. And like sugar in milk, we will make it **sweeter**."

The king was impressed. He granted them refuge — with conditions:
- Adopt Gujarati as their language
- Wear local clothes (saris for women)
- Hold weddings in the evening (not midnight, as in Iran)
- Lay down their weapons (they were no warriors here)
- Practise their religion privately

The Parsis agreed. They kept their promise.

**Becoming Indian.**

Over centuries, the Parsis:
- Learned Gujarati and made it their daily language
- Adopted Gujarati food, with their own distinctive twists (Parsi cuisine is unique — dishes like dhansak, patra ni macchi, sali boti)
- Wore saris (women), with their distinctive style
- Built **fire temples** (Atash Behrams, Agiaries) — places of worship where the sacred fire is kept eternally burning
- Lived in Parsi colonies — quiet, well-kept neighbourhoods

They preserved Zoroastrian religion strictly:
- Did not allow conversion in (you can only be born Parsi)
- Did not allow conversion out (Parsi couples raise Parsi children)
- Maintained their rituals carefully

**Moving to Mumbai.**

In the 17th-19th centuries, many Parsis moved to **Mumbai (Bombay)** when the British developed it as a major port. The Parsis took to:

- **Trade**: with Europeans, Arabs, Africans, Chinese
- **Banking**: building financial networks
- **Shipping**: owning many of Mumbai's shipping companies
- **Manufacturing**: starting Mumbai's textile and other industries
- **Education**: founding schools and colleges
- **Charity**: building hospitals, libraries, parks

**Famous Parsi families and individuals:**

**The Tatas.**

The Tata family is one of the most influential business families in India.

- **Jamsetji Nusserwanji Tata** (1839-1904) — founded the Tata Group. Started in trading, then textile mills, then envisioned the **Indian Institute of Science (IISc)** in Bangalore (built in 1909 after his death). Also built the **Taj Mahal Hotel** in Mumbai (1903) — Indians had been refused entry to British hotels; Tata built his own world-class hotel. After his death, his sons built **Tata Steel** in Jamshedpur — India's first major steel plant.

- **Dorabji Tata** — eldest son, continued the work; established several Tata charities.

- **J.R.D. Tata** (1904-1993) — visionary, ran Tata Group for decades; founded **Air India**; champion of Indian industry.

- **Ratan Tata** (1937-2024) — modernised the group; led Tata's globalisation; bought Jaguar Land Rover, Tetley, Corus.

Today the **Tata Group** has companies like TCS (software), Tata Motors (cars, trucks), Tata Steel, Tata Power, Tata Chemicals, Indian Hotels (Taj), Air India, and many more. It employs hundreds of thousands. It is owned mostly by **Tata Sons** (a holding company), which is in turn owned by **Tata Trusts** — charitable foundations.

So Tata wealth doesn't make a family rich; it funds Indian charity. This is a unique model in world business.

**Other Parsi families and figures:**

- **Godrej family** — Godrej & Boyce; Godrej Industries; locks, appliances, real estate
- **Wadia family** — Bombay Dyeing, Bombay Burmah
- **Mistry family** — Shapoorji Pallonji (construction giant)
- **Pesotan Wadia** — Indian shipping
- **Dadabhai Naoroji** (1825-1917) — "Grand Old Man of India"; first Indian elected to British Parliament; mentor to Gandhi; freedom fighter
- **Bhikaji Cama** (1861-1936) — revolutionary; hoisted India's flag in Germany in 1907
- **Pherozeshah Mehta** — founder of Indian National Congress
- **Sam Manekshaw** (1914-2008) — Field Marshal; led India to victory in 1971 war
- **Homi J. Bhabha** (1909-1966) — "Father of India's nuclear programme"; founded Tata Institute of Fundamental Research
- **Homi Sethna** — nuclear scientist
- **Zubin Mehta** (1936-) — world-famous conductor; led major orchestras in New York, Israel, Vienna
- **Fali Nariman** — eminent constitutional lawyer
- **Soli Sorabjee** — eminent legal figure
- **Bapsi Sidhwa**, **Rohinton Mistry**, **Cyrus Mistry** — writers
- **Adi Godrej**, **Anu Aga** — business leaders

This is an extraordinary list for a community of about 60,000 people in India (and perhaps 200,000 worldwide).

**Why have Parsis been so successful?**

Several factors:

1. **Education** — Parsis valued education from early on; both boys and girls were educated when most Indians (and most Westerners) didn't educate girls
2. **Honesty** — "Good thoughts, good words, good deeds" is Zoroastrian creed; the community has long been known for honest dealing in business
3. **Community support** — Parsis helped each other; Parsi trusts funded education, business loans, health care for community members
4. **Charity** — wealthy Parsis built schools, hospitals, libraries, parks — not just for Parsis but for all Indians
5. **International connections** — Parsis bridged Indian society with European traders, then later with global business
6. **Modern outlook** — they adapted to modern industry, science, technology
7. **Small community advantage** — being small, they didn't get caught in caste politics; they were treated as a separate respected group

**The Demographic Challenge.**

The Parsi community is **declining in numbers**:
- Strict rules against intermarriage (children of mixed marriages traditionally not considered Parsi)
- Late marriages, fewer children
- Emigration (especially to UK, USA, Australia)
- About 60,000 Parsis in India today; some predictions show further decline

The community is working to address this — encouraging marriage and children, sometimes allowing some intermarriage (rules vary).

**The Parsi Gift to India.**

The Parsis are a remarkable example of how a small refugee community can have enormous positive impact. They:
- Came as refugees
- Integrated peacefully
- Built businesses that employ millions
- Built schools and hospitals that serve all Indians
- Produced political, scientific, cultural leaders
- Maintained their distinct identity
- Gave back through charity

Their story is one of the great success stories of India's tradition of welcoming refugees.

When you visit Mumbai, you'll see Parsi colonies, Parsi schools, Parsi-built hospitals, Parsi-named streets. The Tata logo is everywhere. Each is a reminder of the bowl of milk into which sugar was stirred 1,200 years ago.

The promise was kept. The milk became sweeter. India is grateful.

## How Has India Helped Tibetan Refugees?

**Child:** Maa, the Tibetan refugees are still here, after 65+ years. Tell me more about them and India's role.

**Mother:** The Tibetan-Indian story is one of the great humanitarian achievements of modern India.

**Background: Tibet's Loss.**

Tibet was an independent or autonomous Buddhist kingdom for centuries, with its own culture, language, government, and Dalai Lama (the spiritual and political leader).

In 1950, China invaded Tibet. The young **14th Dalai Lama** tried to coexist with Chinese rule for several years. But conditions deteriorated:
- Monasteries closed
- Traditional culture suppressed
- Religious practices restricted
- Tibetan leaders arrested

In **March 1959**, a popular uprising in Lhasa was crushed by China. The Dalai Lama, then just 23 years old, fled across the Himalayas to India. About 80,000 Tibetans followed him over the following months.

**India's Response.**

Prime Minister **Jawaharlal Nehru** faced a difficult choice. Welcoming the Tibetans would:
- Anger China (with whom India was trying to maintain friendly relations)
- Cost India money and resources
- Create a permanent diaspora problem

But not welcoming them would:
- Be morally wrong — the refugees had nowhere else to go
- Betray Indian values of hospitality and refuge
- Hurt India's standing as a moral nation

Nehru chose to welcome them. India:
- Granted asylum to the Dalai Lama
- Allowed him to set up the **Tibetan Government in Exile** in Dharamsala
- Provided land for settlements
- Allowed schools, monasteries, hospitals to be built
- Did not require refugees to assimilate

The decision turned out to be a fateful one. In 1962, China-India relations broke down completely, partly over this issue. India and China fought a war that India lost.

But the moral choice was made — and India has held to it for over 65 years.

**Tibetan Settlements in India.**

Today, about **100,000 Tibetans** live in India. Major settlements:

(1) **Dharamsala** (Himachal Pradesh): The main centre. **McLeodganj** in Dharamsala is the residence of the Dalai Lama and the seat of the Tibetan Government in Exile.

(2) **Bylakuppe** (Karnataka): The largest Tibetan settlement in India. Houses major monasteries like **Sera, Tashi Lhunpo, Drepung, Gaden** — the great Gelugpa monasteries that were destroyed in Tibet and rebuilt here.

(3) **Mundgod** (Karnataka): Another large Buddhist monastic complex.

(4) **Several other settlements** across Himachal, Uttarakhand, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Sikkim, and other states.

**What India Has Provided.**

Over 65+ years:
- **Land** for settlements
- **Limited self-governance** (Tibetans run their own schools, hospitals, monasteries)
- **Education** — Tibetan schools with Tibetan curriculum
- **Cultural protection** — freedom to practise Buddhism, learn Tibetan language, preserve traditional arts
- **Medical care** including Tibetan medicine (Sowa Rigpa) institutes
- **Religious freedom** — no restrictions on monasteries
- **Travel documents** — special "Identity Certificates" for Tibetans (most are not Indian citizens, but can live and work)
- **Protection from China** — India does not allow Chinese government activity targeting Tibetans

**What the Tibetans Have Done.**

The Tibetans have not just survived — they have **rebuilt their culture in exile**:

(1) **Preserved Tibetan Buddhism.** Many Buddhist traditions that were destroyed or weakened in Tibet have been preserved in India. Today, the most authentic Tibetan Buddhist scholarship happens in India.

(2) **Built monasteries.** Gigantic monastic complexes have been built — sometimes housing 3,000+ monks each. These are training centres for Buddhist monks from across the world.

(3) **Trained scholars.** Geshe (PhD-equivalent) and Khenpo training programmes produce learned Buddhist teachers.

(4) **Preserved language.** Tibetan schools teach Tibetan to children born in India.

(5) **Cultural arts.** Tibetan music, dance, art, thangka painting — all kept alive.

(6) **Tibetan medicine.** The Men-Tsee-Khang in Dharamsala teaches and practises Tibetan medicine; provides care to thousands.

(7) **Books and scholarship.** Tibetan publishing houses in India have produced thousands of books, dictionaries, translations.

(8) **The Dalai Lama's Teachings.** The 14th Dalai Lama has become one of the most respected spiritual figures in the world. His teachings on compassion, mindfulness, and Buddhist philosophy have inspired millions. He won the **Nobel Peace Prize in 1989**.

(9) **Sand mandalas and spiritual ceremonies** open to all visitors, including from around the world.

(10) **Indian-Tibetan cultural exchange.** Tibetan monks have learned Indian languages; Indians have learned Tibetan Buddhism. Connections deepen.

**Costs and Tensions.**

Hosting Tibetans has not been free of cost or controversy:

(1) **Chinese opposition.** China sees the Dalai Lama as a separatist. Every visit by world leaders to Dharamsala creates diplomatic friction.

(2) **Limited rights.** Tibetan refugees are not Indian citizens (with some exceptions). They have limited property rights, employment options, political voice.

(3) **Stateless feeling.** Many young Tibetans, born in India, have no homeland to return to. Tibet is closed to them.

(4) **Future uncertainty.** The 14th Dalai Lama is now over 90 years old. When he dies, the succession is contested — China claims it will choose the next Dalai Lama; Tibetans say only Tibetan tradition can.

(5) **Economic burden.** India has provided land, infrastructure, services for decades.

**Why India Should Be Proud.**

Despite the costs, India has done something extraordinary: **preserved a civilisation**.

Without India's welcome:
- Tibetan Buddhism might have been destroyed
- The Dalai Lama and his teachings would not have reached the world
- Thousands of monks would have been killed or scattered
- Tibetan language, art, medicine would have suffered worse

Tibet's tragedy is real. But the Tibetan culture is alive — and India is the reason.

**A Modern Lesson.**

In a 21st century where many countries are turning away refugees, India's Tibetan policy is a powerful example. It shows:

(1) **Moral choices have costs but also benefits.** India paid diplomatically. But India also gained moral standing, gained the Dalai Lama's deep gratitude (he has consistently honoured India), and gained a beautiful cultural treasure on Indian soil.

(2) **Cultures can survive in exile if given space.** Tibet might be reborn one day if conditions change. Even if not, Tibetan civilisation lives.

(3) **Hospitality can be national policy.** India has done what individual hospitable families do — opened the door, found room, supported integration.

**A Personal Note.**

The 14th Dalai Lama has often said: **"India is my guru."** He learned much from Indian Buddhist traditions, from Mahatma Gandhi's non-violence, from Indian philosophical thought. India gave him refuge; he gave the world his teachings.

It is a beautiful exchange. India sheltered a refugee. The refugee became a global figure of peace. Both have benefited.

When you grow up, you may visit Dharamsala, hear the Dalai Lama if he is still teaching, see the monasteries in Bylakuppe, see Tibetan culture alive on Indian soil.

Remember: this is what your country has done, with grace, over 65 years. It is one of India's quiet achievements.

## What Does It Mean to Be a "Sweetness in the Milk"?

**Child:** Maa, the Parsi "sugar in milk" story is beautiful. Is that what immigration should ideally look like?

**Mother:** Yes — and the story has deeper meaning than just immigration. Let me explain.

**What did the Parsi leader say?**

When the king said the bowl is full, the Parsi leader didn't reply with: "Then take less of your country and give us part." He didn't reply with: "We need our own land and rights." He didn't reply with: "We will fight to be here."

He said: "**We will dissolve into your society. We will not displace anyone. And we will make the whole thing sweeter.**"

This is a profound philosophy. Let me unpack it.

**Three things the metaphor says:**

**1. "We will dissolve."**

Means: We will integrate. We will learn your language, adopt your customs, follow your way of life. We will not stay separate. We will become Indian.

In practice, the Parsis kept their promise. They:
- Adopted Gujarati
- Wore saris (women)
- Lived in Indian cities
- Followed Indian social customs
- Were not visibly "different"

This is integration — becoming part of the host society in social terms.

**2. "We will not displace anyone."**

Means: We will not take anyone else's space, job, or resources. We will find our own niche.

The Parsis found niches in trade, banking, manufacturing, education. They didn't compete unfairly with existing communities. They built new wealth.

This is non-displacement — adding to the society rather than taking from it.

**3. "We will make it sweeter."**

Means: We will contribute. We will give back. Our presence will make the whole better, not worse.

The Parsis built hospitals (used by all Indians), schools (educating all), industries (employing all), foundations (helping all). The Tata family is the most striking example — most of Tata wealth is owned by charitable trusts that benefit India.

This is contribution — being a net positive for the host society.

**The Three-Part Test.**

Let's call this the **"Sugar in Milk Test"** for refugee or immigrant integration. A community succeeds when:

1. **Integrate** — learn the language, adopt social customs, become part of daily life
2. **Don't displace** — find your own niche, don't crowd out existing communities
3. **Contribute** — give more than you take

Many of India's refugee communities have followed this pattern:
- **Jews** — integrated as Marathi/Malayalam speakers, found trade niches, contributed to commerce
- **Syriac Christians** — integrated into Kerala society, contributed to trade and learning
- **Parsis** — the original "sugar in milk" example
- **Tibetans** — partial test: integrated less fully (they aim to return), but contribute culturally
- **Armenians** — integrated, contributed to trade
- **Bahais** — integrated, contributed (Lotus Temple welcomes all)

**The opposite of the test.**

What happens when communities don't pass the test?

- **No integration**: Communities that stay completely separate cause tensions. They don't understand the host society; the host doesn't understand them.
- **Displacement**: When new arrivals push out existing communities (whether through numbers, economic pressure, or politics), tensions rise.
- **No contribution**: Communities that only take (welfare, public services) and don't give back create resentment.

India's history has examples of both successful and unsuccessful integrations. The successful ones follow the Sugar in Milk model. The unsuccessful ones don't.

**Does this apply to all immigration?**

Mostly yes. Whether you are:
- An Indian student going to study in the USA
- An Italian family moving to Mumbai
- A Bangladeshi worker in Bengaluru
- A Tibetan family in Dharamsala

The same principles apply:
- Learn the local language
- Respect the local customs
- Don't try to take what others have built
- Contribute through your work and life
- Earn your place

If you do this, you will be welcomed — almost anywhere in the world. If you don't, you will struggle even in the most generous host society.

**Personal application.**

You can apply the Sugar in Milk principle in your own life:

When you join a new school, new sports team, new neighbourhood, new workplace:
- **Integrate**: learn the culture, the unwritten rules, the people
- **Don't displace**: find your own role, don't push others out
- **Contribute**: bring your skills, your kindness, your effort

This is how good people behave in any community.

**The deeper teaching.**

The Sugar in Milk story is more than an immigration metaphor. It is a teaching about **how to live in the world**.

Wherever you are:
- You arrived later than someone
- You exist in a community that came before you
- You will leave one day, others will come after

In that time, **what kind of presence will you be**?

A loud noise that disrupts? A burden that takes from others? A demand for your own way?

Or a sweetness in the milk — quiet, integrated, contributive, leaving everything a little better than you found it?

The Indian sages knew the answer. The Parsi leader 1,200 years ago knew the answer. It is the answer for all of us, in every interaction, every day.

**Be the sugar in the milk.**

That is one of the most beautiful Indian teachings — given by a refugee, to a king, who was wise enough to hear it.

And the milk got sweeter. The country grew richer. Everyone benefited. Everyone won.

That is what generosity and integration can build, together.
