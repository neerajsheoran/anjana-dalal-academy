## Why Were Mahmud's Raids So Devastating?

**Child:** Maa, you said Mahmud of Ghazni raided India 17 times. Why couldn't Indian kingdoms stop him?

**Mother:** This is one of the most important questions in Indian history. The answer is painful but important.

**1. India was politically fragmented.**

In the year 1000 CE, when Mahmud started his raids, India had no central government. Dozens of kingdoms — Rajputs in the west, Cholas in the south, Palas in the east, Pratiharas, Chalukyas, Paramaras, and others — all ruled their own territories. They were often at war with each other.

When Mahmud attacked the Hindu Shahis (in present-day Pakistan/Afghanistan), the other Indian kingdoms didn't help. When he raided Mathura, the kingdoms further south didn't unite. Each Indian king thought: "It's not my problem, let the neighbour handle it."

This was a fatal mistake. Mahmud could pick off Indian kingdoms one by one.

**2. Different military style.**

Indian armies of this period:
- Used elephants prominently (slow, easily panicked)
- Followed traditional rules of warfare (announced battles in advance, didn't fight at night, spared retreating enemies)
- Relied on heavy infantry formations
- Were composed mostly of warriors, with limited cavalry

Mahmud's army:
- Used **fast cavalry** as the main force
- Used **surprise attacks** — including raids deep into territory before defenders could organise
- Used **psychological warfare** (terror against civilians to demoralise opponents)
- Was **highly mobile** — could appear and disappear quickly
- Used **archers on horseback** — Central Asian style — devastating at range

In open battle, fast horseman could outmanoeuvre slow elephant-infantry forces. Indian kings hadn't fought such enemies before.

**3. Wealth made temples targets.**

Indian temples had accumulated **enormous wealth** over centuries. Gifts from kings, devotees, merchants piled up:
- Gold and silver idols
- Jewels
- Bronze and copper plates
- Manuscripts in valuable bindings
- Treasures in vaults

Somnath alone is said to have had vast wealth — pounds of gold, hundreds of jewels, treasures from many kings. For Mahmud, the temples were like banks waiting to be robbed.

**4. Religious motivation.**

Mahmud was a Sunni Muslim ruler. He claimed religious justification for attacking "idolaters" — though scholars today think the **religious motivation was secondary to greed**. He robbed temples for wealth, not faith. But the religious framing helped him recruit warriors and justify destruction.

**5. Indian chivalry.**

Indian rulers — especially Rajputs — followed strict codes of honour. They fought during the day, gave warning before attacks, spared the wounded, kept their word. These codes were noble but **dangerous against an enemy who didn't share them**.

Prithviraj III is the classic example. He defeated Muhammad of Ghor in the First Battle of Tarain (1191) and let him escape. Muhammad returned with a bigger army the next year and killed Prithviraj. Indian chivalry was exploited by enemies who fought by different rules.

**6. The pattern repeated.**

Mahmud raided 17 times. Each time, Indian kingdoms suffered but didn't unite. After he died (1030 CE), Indian kingdoms thought the threat was over. They returned to fighting each other.

A century later, Muhammad of Ghor showed up. Same pattern of disunity. Same defeat at the Second Battle of Tarain. This time, the invaders **stayed and ruled**.

**7. What was the result?**

By 1200 CE, much of north India was under the Delhi Sultanate. This would mark a new chapter of Indian history — about 600 years of Muslim rule (Sultanate → Mughal Empire) until the British arrived.

**8. The deeper lesson.**

The history of Mahmud's raids — and the later Turkish conquests — teaches several lessons that India has had to relearn:

(1) **Disunity has terrible cost.** Indian kingdoms that fought each other while ignoring outside threats lost their independence. The same lesson applies to modern India — political quarrels at home weaken the country externally.

(2) **Military readiness matters.** A civilisation cannot afford to be technologically or tactically behind. Indian armies should have adapted to fight cavalry; they didn't, and paid the price.

(3) **Wealth must be defended.** Resources concentrated in vulnerable places (temples) without strong defence became targets. Modern lesson: nations must protect their wealth (banks, technology, infrastructure).

(4) **Cultural pride alone is not enough.** Indian civilisation in 1000 CE was richer than its invaders culturally — but lost militarily. Cultural achievements need to be backed by political and military strength.

(5) **Chivalry against unchivalrous enemies is suicidal.** Indian codes of honour are admirable in peacetime. In war against ruthless enemies, they have to be balanced with strategic realism.

(6) **No empire is forever — but how it falls matters.** Empires rise and fall. The question is whether civilisation continues. Indian civilisation continued, but at huge cost. Better preparation could have reduced the cost.

These are lessons that India has had to relearn at various points in history. The 21st century continues to require them.

**Child:** Why couldn't Indian kingdoms learn after the first time?

**Mother:** They tried — but slowly. The Rajputs improved their cavalry. Indian kingdoms started forming alliances. But change came too late. Multiple generations of disunity had hollowed out India's strategic position.

This is why the history of this period is taught — not to dwell on defeat, but to draw lessons that future generations can apply. The wisest students of history are not those who memorise dates, but those who **understand patterns** — and try to avoid repeating mistakes.

## What Was Lost When Nalanda Burned?

**Child:** Maa, you said Nalanda's library burned for months. What was lost?

**Mother:** This is one of the most painful subjects in Indian history. Nalanda was one of humanity's great achievements, and its destruction was one of humanity's great losses.

**What was Nalanda?**

Nalanda was a Buddhist monastic university in what is now Bihar. It operated for about **800 years** (5th to 13th century CE), making it one of the longest-running educational institutions in human history.

At its peak (under the Palas), Nalanda had:
- **10,000+ students** living on campus
- **2,000+ teachers**
- **300 lecture halls**
- Multi-story buildings, sometimes 9 stories tall
- A huge **multi-story library complex** called **Dharmaganja** ("Mountain of Truth")
- Dormitories, kitchens, gardens, water systems
- Strict admission tests — only the best students got in
- Students from China, Korea, Japan, Tibet, Mongolia, Sri Lanka, Persia, Indonesia

**What did Nalanda teach?**

Nalanda was a comprehensive university:
- **Buddhist philosophy** (multiple schools)
- **Hindu philosophy** (Vedanta, Samkhya, Nyaya)
- **Jain philosophy**
- **Sanskrit and Pali grammar**
- **Mathematics** (algebra, geometry, arithmetic)
- **Astronomy** (planetary motions, eclipses)
- **Medicine** (Ayurveda, surgery)
- **Logic** (formal reasoning, debate)
- **Linguistics** (Sanskrit, Tibetan, Chinese, Pali, etc.)
- **Arts** (poetry, music, painting)
- **Crafts** (some practical training)

Some scholars compare Nalanda to **modern research universities** — multidisciplinary, internationalised, intensely intellectual.

**Famous people connected to Nalanda:**

- **Xuanzang** (7th century) studied here for years
- **Yi Jing** (7th century) also studied here
- **Aryabhata II** (mathematician)
- **Atisha** (11th century) — Tibetan Buddhism's great teacher; lived and taught here before going to Tibet
- **Naropa** — Buddhist master
- **Shantarakshita** — went from Nalanda to Tibet, founding Tibetan Buddhism

**The library — Dharmaganja.**

This was the heart of Nalanda. Estimates of the collection vary, but it likely held:
- **Hundreds of thousands of manuscripts** in Sanskrit, Pali, Tibetan, Chinese, possibly Persian
- Buddhist texts (Hinayana, Mahayana, Vajrayana)
- Hindu texts (Vedas, Upanishads, philosophy)
- Jain texts
- Scientific treatises
- Medical manuals
- Astronomical works
- Logic and grammar texts
- Histories and biographies
- Poetry and literature
- Some texts that existed nowhere else

**What burned in 1200 CE?**

Around 1200 CE, the Turkish general **Bakhtiyar Khilji**, leading an army from Muhammad of Ghor's territory, attacked Bihar.

He targeted Nalanda. The buildings were burned. The library, according to Persian historian **Minhaj-i-Siraj** (writing about 50 years later), burned for **six months** — so vast was the collection.

Most monks were killed. Those who escaped fled to:
- **Tibet** (carrying many texts; Tibetan Buddhism preserved much that Indian Buddhism lost)
- **Nepal**
- **Southern India** (where Buddhism survived longer)
- **Southeast Asia**

**What was lost forever?**

We will never know exactly. But likely:
- **Many Buddhist texts** — Mahayana sutras, tantras, commentaries
- **Hindu texts** — some philosophical works
- **Scientific works** — mathematics, astronomy, medicine
- **Historical records** — biographies, dynasty records
- **Translations** — Sanskrit texts translated to other languages
- **Original works** — texts composed at Nalanda over 800 years
- **Living tradition of certain schools** — Vajrayana Buddhism nearly died in India

Some texts survived because copies existed in Tibetan or Chinese libraries. Modern scholars have **back-translated** Tibetan texts into Sanskrit to recover some lost works. But much is gone forever.

**Why this matters.**

The destruction of Nalanda was not just a building going down. It was the **interruption of a living intellectual tradition**. Generations of scholarship were destroyed in months.

Compare to:
- The **Library of Alexandria** (Egypt) — destroyed multiple times in antiquity, with similar tragic loss
- The **Mongol destruction of Baghdad** (1258 CE) — they threw books into the Tigris River; it ran black with ink
- **Aztec libraries** burned by the Spanish (16th century)

Each of these is a tragedy. Each interrupted human knowledge. Nalanda ranks among the worst.

**What India lost as a civilisation.**

For centuries, India had been the **intellectual centre of Asia**. Students came from across the continent to study at Nalanda, Vikramashila, and other universities. India produced texts that influenced China, Tibet, Southeast Asia, the Arab world.

After Nalanda's destruction:
- Indian Buddhism nearly disappeared
- Many philosophical traditions weakened
- The university tradition broke
- India never again had the same intellectual centrality

When the British arrived 600 years later, India had no universities in the modern sense — they had to build new ones. The continuous tradition that Nalanda represented was gone.

**Modern attempts to revive Nalanda.**

Nalanda's destruction haunted Indian intellectuals for centuries. In **2014**, the **Nalanda University** was reopened — a modern institution funded by India and several Asian countries (China, Japan, Singapore, Australia, etc.). It is built near the ancient site in Bihar.

Modern Nalanda offers:
- Postgraduate programmes
- Buddhist studies
- History
- Ecology and environment
- Sustainable development
- International relations

It is symbolic — the **revival of a tradition broken 800 years ago**. Whether it can recreate Nalanda's old glory is uncertain. But the attempt itself matters.

**The deeper lesson.**

Nalanda teaches several painful lessons:

(1) **Knowledge needs protection.** Libraries, universities, archives are precious. They can be destroyed. Civilisations must defend them.

(2) **Distributed knowledge is safer than concentrated.** When all texts were in one library, one destruction wiped them out. Modern internet, distributed across the world, is safer.

(3) **Translation and copying are insurance.** Tibetan Buddhists copied Sanskrit texts in Tibetan. Some of those texts survive only because of this. Translation can save knowledge.

(4) **Cultural destruction is not "collateral damage."** It is sometimes deliberate. Tyrants and invaders have always understood that destroying a people's knowledge weakens them.

(5) **Civilisations recover, but with loss.** India continued after Nalanda. But what could have been built on its foundations? We will never know.

**Child:** So Nalanda's loss is still felt today?

**Mother:** In subtle ways, yes. India is rebuilding its educational greatness over decades. The IITs, IIMs, AIIMS, IISc, and many other institutions are world-class today. The Indian student community is among the largest in the world. The intellectual tradition continues.

But there is still the awareness that **something was lost** in 1200 CE that cannot be fully recovered. Modern Nalanda University is a tribute to that awareness — and an attempt to begin again.

When you grow up, you may visit the **old Nalanda ruins** (now an archaeological site). The remains of brick buildings, dormitories, lecture halls are still there. Walk among them. Imagine 10,000 students, 2,000 teachers, lectures in dozens of subjects, languages, traditions. Imagine the library — manuscripts everywhere, monks copying, debating, teaching.

And then remember what was lost. Remember it not to be sad, but to be **determined**. The Indian tradition of learning, of debate, of inquiry — Nalanda's true legacy — is yours to inherit and carry forward.

## Who Was Bhaskaracharya and Why Should We Know Him?

**Child:** Maa, you mentioned Bhaskaracharya. Was he as important as Aryabhata?

**Mother:** Yes — and in some ways, he was even more advanced. **Bhaskaracharya** (also called Bhaskara II, 1114-1185 CE) is one of the greatest mathematicians and astronomers India ever produced — and one of the great scientists in world history.

**His life.**

Bhaskara was born in a village near Bijapur in Karnataka. He studied under his father, a mathematician. He worked from **Ujjain** — the great Indian centre of astronomy and mathematics, where Aryabhata and Brahmagupta had also worked centuries earlier.

He was head of the astronomical observatory at Ujjain — one of the most prestigious scientific positions in the medieval world.

**His three major works.**

**1. Lilavati (1150 CE).**

Named after his daughter (according to legend). A textbook on arithmetic. Some of the most charming math ever written.

Bhaskara presented problems in **verse form**, often using imagery from daily life:
- Bees buzzing around lotus flowers
- Cooking utensils
- Travel times for elephants
- Mathematical puzzles set as folk stories

For example, a famous problem:
> "Out of a swarm of bees, one-fifth settled on a kadamba blossom, one-third on a silindhri flower, three times the difference between these two went to a kutaja, and one bee, attracted by the perfume of a jasmine bloom, remained flying in the air. Tell me, lovely girl, how many bees were there?"

(The answer is 15. Two-thirds of 15 is 10 settled bees, plus the wandering bee plus the kutaja-attracted ones.)

The Lilavati covers:
- Arithmetic
- Algebra
- Geometry
- Trigonometry
- Practical applications (interest, partnerships, commercial math)

It was used as a textbook in India for **centuries**. It was translated into Persian (under Akbar's order) and later into many other languages.

**2. Bijaganita (Algebra, 1150 CE).**

A more advanced work on algebra. It covers:
- Solving linear and quadratic equations
- Methods for indeterminate equations
- Negative numbers (Bhaskara accepted them more naturally than even later European mathematicians)
- Working with unknowns

Bhaskara realised that **a number x^2 can have TWO solutions** (positive and negative). He wrote it down, while European mathematicians were still ignoring negative roots 400 years later.

**3. Siddhanta Shiromani (Crown Jewel of Treatises, 1150 CE).**

A massive astronomical work in four parts:
- **Lilavati** — arithmetic
- **Bijaganita** — algebra
- **Goladhyaya** — sphere (astronomy)
- **Ganitadhyaya** — math for astronomy

In the astronomical parts, Bhaskara:
- Calculated planetary motions
- Worked out eclipse predictions
- Described instruments for observation
- Calculated the **sidereal year** (time Earth takes to orbit the sun once) at **365.2588 days** — accurate to within **3 minutes** of the modern value!
- Anticipated **differential calculus** — used a kind of derivative for calculating instantaneous planetary speeds. This is 500 years before Newton and Leibniz formalised calculus.

**Specific breakthroughs.**

Bhaskara is credited with several major mathematical achievements:

(1) **Mean Value Theorem (Rolle's Theorem).** Anticipated by 600+ years.

(2) **Differential calculus concepts.** He used the idea of an "instantaneous rate of change" for planetary motion. The full development came later in Europe (Newton, Leibniz).

(3) **Division by zero.** Bhaskara discussed division by zero. He said the result was infinite. This was sophisticated mathematical thinking for the time. (Modern math says division by zero is undefined, but Bhaskara's intuition was on the right track.)

(4) **The Chakravala method.** For solving certain hard equations (Pell's equations). European mathematicians took centuries to develop similar methods.

(5) **Trigonometric identities.** Several modern trigonometric formulas were known to Bhaskara.

(6) **Pi.** He computed pi accurately to many decimal places.

(7) **Surface area and volume of sphere.** Anticipating later formulas.

**The Lilavati legend.**

A charming legend about Bhaskara's daughter Lilavati:

Lilavati was very intelligent. Bhaskara, by astrology, calculated the exact moment when she should marry — for her happiness. He made a special water clock with a small hole in a cup floating in water. When the cup sank, that would be the auspicious moment.

But just before the moment, Lilavati was peering curiously at the clock. A pearl from her nose ring fell into the cup and blocked the hole. The water didn't enter, the cup didn't sink, and the auspicious moment passed. Lilavati never married.

To comfort her, Bhaskara dedicated his mathematics book to her — promising that her name would be remembered as long as people studied math.

The legend may or may not be true. But the dedication is real. And his promise has been kept — 900 years later, Lilavati's name is still spoken whenever Indian mathematics is discussed.

**His influence.**

- Bhaskara's works were studied in India for centuries.
- His texts were translated into Persian during the Mughal period.
- Persian translations reached Europe by the 16th-17th centuries.
- European mathematicians borrowed Indian methods (often without credit).
- The decimal system, zero, place-value, modern arithmetic — all owe a major debt to Indian mathematics, including Bhaskara.

**Why he matters today.**

(1) **Indian science is older and deeper than commonly recognised.** Modern Indian students often think science is "Western." But Indian mathematics, astronomy, medicine, metallurgy were world-leading for centuries.

(2) **Mathematics can be beautiful.** Bhaskara's verse problems show that math need not be dry. The Indian tradition combined math with poetry.

(3) **Education built on tradition.** Bhaskara learned from his father, studied at Ujjain — a tradition going back to Aryabhata. Indian science was passed down through generations.

(4) **Discoveries happen globally, in many places.** Some of what is called "calculus" in textbooks was anticipated by Bhaskara 500 years before Newton. Many "European" discoveries had Indian precursors. History should reflect this.

(5) **National pride is grounded in reality.** Indian students should know about Bhaskara, Aryabhata, Brahmagupta, Madhava, and others. These were real, world-class scientists. India has nothing to apologise for.

**What we can learn.**

Bhaskara's life teaches us:

- **Pursue excellence** — wherever you start, you can become world-class
- **Honour teachers** — Bhaskara honoured his father and his teachers
- **Combine subjects** — math, astronomy, poetry, philosophy worked together for him
- **Make work beautiful** — Lilavati's verse problems are delightful
- **Push beyond your time** — Bhaskara anticipated ideas that came centuries later
- **Build on what came before** — he used Aryabhata's and Brahmagupta's work
- **Continue the chain** — his work was passed to many generations

In modern India, many science institutes, schools, prizes are named after Bhaskaracharya. A space satellite is named **Bhaskara**. His statue stands at his birthplace.

**One more interesting fact.**

Bhaskara's **Bijaganita** contained an early version of what we now call the **proof of the Pythagorean theorem**. He drew a single diagram and wrote one word: **"Behold!"** (in Sanskrit). The proof was visual — geometric — elegant.

This "Behold!" proof is sometimes still shown to students today. Pure mathematics, written 900 years ago, still teaches.

**Child:** Why don't we hear more about him?

**Mother:** Several reasons:
- The British dominated Indian education and emphasised European achievements
- Indian textbooks haven't always prioritised Indian scientists
- Sanskrit, his language, is no longer commonly taught
- His works were translated into Persian, then Arabic, then Latin — by the time European mathematicians used his ideas, Bhaskara was often forgotten

But this is changing. Indian universities, NCERT, ISRO, and many others now actively highlight Indian scientific heritage. You should too — read about Bhaskara, mention his name, share his story.

He earned his place in human history through brilliant work 900 years ago. The least we can do is remember him.
