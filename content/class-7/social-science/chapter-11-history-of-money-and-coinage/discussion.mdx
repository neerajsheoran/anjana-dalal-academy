## What If Money Suddenly Disappeared?

**Child:** Maa, what if money suddenly disappeared? What would happen?

**Mother:** Wonderful question. Let's think through it.

**Day 1.** You wake up. You want bread. The baker says: "Money has stopped working. Bring something to barter."
- You go home. What do you have to trade? You're 12 years old. You have a school bag, some books, clothes.
- You take an extra pencil. The baker says: "Pencils? I have enough." You go to three other shops. Same answer.
- You return home hungry.

**Day 2.** Your mother tries to go to office. The taxi driver wants to be paid in something tangible. She offers a small gold earring. He thinks about it. She arrives late. Her office wants employees, but how will they pay? Hospital? Same problem. School? Teachers won't show up.

**Within a week**, the city shuts down. Not because of any natural disaster — just because the **invisible tool that made everything work** has vanished.

**Within a month**, society collapses. People start farming, fishing, hunting, gathering. Cities empty out. Economic activity falls 90%. Hospitals struggle. Schools close.

**This is what money does**, even when we don't notice it. It is the invisible network that connects every transaction in every market in every city across the country and the world.

**Child:** So money is more important than I realised?

**Mother:** Far more important. Money is one of the **greatest inventions** of human civilisation. It made possible:
- **Cities** — where people specialise in different jobs and trade with each other
- **Long-distance trade** — silk from China, spices from India, gold from Africa, all coordinated by money
- **Large enterprises** — factories, railways, hospitals, schools — all requiring money to pay many specialised workers
- **Savings** — people can plan for the future, save for old age, invest in education
- **Loans** — start a business, buy a home, get medical care

Without money, none of these are possible at large scale. Money is what allows millions of strangers to cooperate without trusting each other personally.

**Child:** So banks and ATMs and UPI — these are all built on the idea of money?

**Mother:** All of them. They are just **more efficient ways to use money**. The basic invention — agreeing that this piece of metal or paper or number has value — was the real breakthrough. Everything else is improvement on that.

**Child:** Was money invented by one person?

**Mother:** No — like fire, language, and farming, money was invented many times by many cultures. The Chinese had cowrie shells. The Lydians had electrum (gold-silver) coins. The Indians had karshapanas. Each culture, facing the limits of barter, came up with similar solutions.

The interesting thing is that **even today**, when humans land in remote situations — prisons, war zones, isolated communities — they invent new money. Cigarettes have served as money in many prisons. Bottle caps. Tide detergent. Even in our school playground, sometimes children create their own currency for games.

The instinct to invent money is deeply human. Without it, modern civilisation would not exist.

## Why Did Indian Civilisation Need Coins So Early?

**Child:** Maa, India had coins by 600 BCE. Why so early?

**Mother:** Because India was already a **very advanced trading civilisation** by then.

Think about what India had by 600 BCE:
- **Many cities** — Pataliputra, Varanasi, Ujjain, Taxila, Madurai, and many more
- **Multiple kingdoms (mahajanapadas)** — 16 large states, each with its own economy
- **Active trade routes** — connecting the mahajanapadas, and reaching to Persia, Central Asia, and beyond
- **Specialised crafts** — carnelian beads, fine cotton, iron tools, ivory work
- **Surplus agriculture** — farmers producing more than their families needed

All this required exchange. Barter could only get you so far. With so many cities, so many goods, so many traders — you needed **money**.

So the Indian mahajanapadas — and similar civilisations around the world — invented coins.

**Child:** Were Indian coins just like Greek or Roman ones?

**Mother:** Different. Greek and Roman coins typically had a king's portrait. Indian coins, especially the earliest ones, were **punch-marked** — small symbols (sun, mountain, animals) stamped on flat metal pieces. The symbols showed who issued the coin, when, and where.

Later Indian coins had:
- **Animals** (elephant, tiger, bull, horse)
- **Deities** (Lakshmi, Vishnu)
- **Plants** (lotus, bodhi tree)
- **Kings and queens** (especially in Gupta era)
- **Calligraphy** (Mughal era — beautiful Persian script)

Indian coin art is some of the most beautiful in the world. Each region developed its own style.

**Child:** And Indian coins reached far?

**Mother:** Very far. Indian gold and silver coins have been found:
- In Arabia
- In Central Asia
- In South East Asia (Sri Lanka, Indonesia, Thailand)
- In China
- In East Africa

And foreign coins have been found in India:
- Roman gold coins (in Tamil Nadu and Kerala)
- Persian and Greek coins (in the northwest)
- Chinese coins (in eastern India)

This tells us India was a **major hub of global trade**. Coins were the language of that trade. A merchant from Egypt could come to Madurai, hand over Roman gold coins, get Indian pepper, and head home. Coins translated value across cultures.

**Child:** Why did people accept foreign coins?

**Mother:** Because **gold is gold** and **silver is silver**. The metal had value regardless of who minted it. A Roman gold coin had the same gold in it whether you spent it in Rome or in Pudukkottai.

Modern paper money is different — it has value only because the government backs it. But gold coins were intrinsically valuable.

Today we still see this: gold is a **global currency**. People buy gold as a store of value, and gold prices are similar everywhere in the world. This continues a 2,500-year-old tradition.

## How Did UPI Change India?

**Child:** Maa, UPI is everywhere in India now. Why is it such a big deal?

**Mother:** UPI (Unified Payments Interface) is **one of the most important Indian inventions of the 21st century**. Let me explain why.

**Before UPI (before 2016):**
- To send money to someone, you needed their bank account number, IFSC code, name, etc.
- You had to log into your bank's website or app
- It took hours or sometimes days to transfer
- Each bank had its own system
- Small payments (₹10, ₹50) were impossible — fees made it not worth it
- Most small shops took only cash
- Cash was hard to carry and risky

**With UPI:**
- Just need someone's phone number or scan their QR code
- Money moves in **seconds**
- Works across all banks
- **Free** for most transactions
- Works for any amount — ₹1, ₹1,00,000, anything
- A vegetable vendor with no bank training can accept payments

**Child:** Why is this such a big deal?

**Mother:** Several reasons.

**1. It brought millions into the financial system.**
Before UPI, hundreds of millions of poor Indians had no real way to use bank accounts. The bank was far, paperwork was confusing, fees were high. UPI made banking accessible from a basic smartphone. A street vendor in Bihar can now receive payments from customers, save in a bank, even take small loans.

**2. It made small payments possible.**
You can pay ₹10 to a paanwala or ₹5 to a chai shop using UPI. Before, this was unthinkable. Small transactions were stuck in cash. UPI freed them.

**3. It cut transaction costs.**
A typical UPI transaction costs essentially zero. Compare to credit card payments which cost shops 2-3% of every transaction. UPI gave India one of the cheapest payment systems in the world.

**4. It enabled new businesses.**
Many new Indian startups exist only because UPI exists — delivery services, mobile ordering, ride sharing, online education. They all rely on tiny, instant, cheap payments that UPI makes possible.

**5. It is a global model.**
Many countries are now studying UPI. India has offered to help build similar systems in Singapore, UAE, France, and others. UPI is becoming an Indian export — not goods, but **technology**.

**6. It scales massively.**
In 2024, UPI processed about **150 billion transactions in a year** — more than the credit card networks of the entire world combined. India is the world's leader in real-time digital payments.

**Child:** How did India do this when richer countries didn't?

**Mother:** Several factors helped:

(1) **Open standards** — UPI is run by NPCI (National Payments Corporation of India) as a public utility, not a private company. All banks must support it. No company can dominate it.

(2) **Aadhaar** — India's digital ID system gave every Indian a unique identifier. This made bank accounts and KYC (Know Your Customer) much easier.

(3) **Smartphone explosion** — cheap smartphones (₹5,000-15,000) reached every Indian village around 2015-18. Internet became cheap. Suddenly, hundreds of millions could use UPI.

(4) **Demonetisation push (2016)** — when high-value notes were withdrawn, UPI suddenly looked very attractive. Many people who would have stayed cash-only learned digital payments.

(5) **No legacy systems** — richer countries already had credit cards. They had no urgent need for UPI. India was leapfrogging — going from cash directly to mobile payments, skipping the credit card era.

(6) **Government support** — successive governments backed UPI as national policy.

**Child:** Are there risks?

**Mother:** Yes — UPI has some real risks we should know.

- **Fraud** — scammers trick people into approving fake payments. You should never share your UPI PIN with anyone.
- **Privacy** — your payment data is collected and analysed. Who sees this data is a real concern.
- **Dependency on phone** — if your phone is lost or stolen, you may be locked out.
- **Internet outages** — without internet, UPI doesn't work.
- **Concentration risk** — if NPCI's systems go down, the country's payments freeze.

These are real issues that India is working to address. But on balance, UPI has been an enormous gift.

**Child:** Will UPI replace all cash?

**Mother:** Probably not entirely. Cash is still useful for:
- Emergencies when electronics fail
- Privacy (cash leaves no trail)
- People who don't have smartphones
- Very small transactions in remote areas

But cash will keep shrinking. UPI will keep growing. By the time you start earning, you may rarely touch cash at all.

That said — knowing how money works, not just how to use UPI, is important. The basics of value, savings, and budgeting matter whether you pay in cash, cards, or UPI.

## What Makes Currency Valuable?

**Child:** Maa, paper money is just paper. Why is a ₹500 note worth ₹500?

**Mother:** This is one of the deepest questions in economics. The short answer: **because people trust it.**

Let me explain.

**In ancient times**, money was made of valuable materials — gold, silver, copper. A gold coin was worth its weight in gold regardless of which kingdom made it. The coin had **intrinsic value** — the metal itself was worth something.

**Today**, paper money has no intrinsic value. A ₹500 note costs maybe ₹3 to print. The paper, ink, and security features cost roughly that. So how does it become ₹500?

**Answer: because we all agree.**

The ₹500 note is what economists call **fiat money** — money that has value because the government declares it so AND because society accepts it.

**Three pillars hold paper money up:**

**1. The government accepts it for taxes.**
This is huge. When the Indian government says "you must pay taxes in rupees," every Indian needs rupees. Rupees suddenly have demand. This gives them value.

**2. Other people accept it as payment.**
Even if the government accepted rupees, that wouldn't matter much for daily life. But because shops, employers, and friends accept rupees for goods and services, the rupee works everywhere.

**3. The Reserve Bank of India backs it.**
The RBI is the central bank. It guarantees the rupee's value, manages the money supply, and prevents both inflation (too much money) and deflation (too little).

**Child:** What happens if trust breaks?

**Mother:** Disasters happen.

**Example 1: Hyperinflation in Germany (1920s).**
After World War I, Germany printed too much money to pay its debts. Money lost value rapidly. A loaf of bread cost a few marks in 1918, millions of marks by 1923. People needed wheelbarrows of cash to buy basics. Families burnt currency for warmth because the paper was worth more as fuel than as money.

**Example 2: Zimbabwe (2007-2009).**
Zimbabwe printed money to pay government bills. Inflation hit **89.7 sextillion percent**. They issued **100-trillion-dollar notes**. People stopped trusting the currency. Eventually, Zimbabwe abandoned its dollar entirely.

**Example 3: Venezuela (recent years).**
Similar story — government printed too much money, currency collapsed, citizens turned to US dollars or barter.

**Child:** Why does inflation destroy money?

**Mother:** Because money's value comes from trust that it will be roughly the same value next year. If you can't trust that, you don't accept it. If no one accepts it, it isn't money.

**Child:** Can India's rupee crash like that?

**Mother:** In theory yes, in practice unlikely — because RBI carefully manages the money supply. India has had inflation (sometimes 10-15%) but never hyperinflation. The RBI's job is to keep inflation stable, usually targeting around 4%.

India's rupee has lost value over time — what cost ₹1 in 1947 might cost ₹100 now — but slowly, manageably. This is **normal inflation**, which most countries have.

**Child:** So money is a kind of agreement?

**Mother:** A grand social agreement. **The most successful agreement in human history.**

Imagine: 140 crore Indians, plus everyone abroad who deals with India, ALL agreeing that little pieces of paper or numbers on a screen have specific values. Trillions of transactions every year, all working smoothly because of this shared trust.

If you stop and think about it, it's almost a miracle. Money is the **invisible foundation** of modern civilisation. Every time you pay for chai, you are participating in this ancient, vast, complicated agreement.

The lesson: **protect the trust**. When governments print too much money, when banks lie about reserves, when shopkeepers refuse to give change — they all damage the trust that makes money work.

A wise saying: "**Bad money drives out good**" (Gresham's Law). When currency loses trust, it gets replaced by something else — gold, foreign currency, even cigarettes. So all societies have to work to keep their money trustworthy.

And as Indians, we should be proud — our rupee has held up across 77+ years of independence, despite wars, droughts, oil crises, and pandemics. Not perfect, but resilient. That is a quiet achievement worth celebrating.
