## A Country That Lives by Its Fields

You probably bought your rice, your wheat, your vegetables from a shop. But before any of them reached the shop, **someone grew them**. A farmer planted a seed, watered it, weeded it, watched the sky for rain or storms, and harvested when the time was right.

That someone — multiplied by **140 million farming households** — is the silent foundation of Indian life. Without farming, there is no food. Without food, there is no civilisation.

In India, farming is not just an industry. It is:
- A **livelihood** for nearly half of all Indians
- A **culture** older than any written history
- A **tie to the land** that goes back tens of thousands of years
- A **rhythm of seasons** that shapes festivals, songs, marriages, and prayers

This chapter is about how India farms — and why understanding it matters even if you grow up in a city.

## How Much of India's Life Is Farming?

The numbers tell a story:

- **18% of India's GDP** comes from agriculture and allied activities (2022-23)
- **About 46% of India's working population** works in farming (2022-23)
- **140 million farming households** — roughly 600+ million people directly dependent on farming
- **Over 75% of agricultural workers** in rural India are women (2025)

So when you imagine "an Indian farmer," do you picture a man? Many Indians do — but the reality is that **most farmworkers are women**. They sow, transplant, weed, harvest, thresh. Women do most of the unseen daily labour of Indian farming.

India is a country where, even in 2027, more people work the land than do anything else. That makes farming central to who we are.

## What Is Farming?

The word **agriculture** comes from Latin: **agri** (field) + **culture** (to cultivate) = "cultivating fields."

But farming in India is broader than just growing crops. The Government of India uses the term **"agriculture and allied activities,"** which includes:

- **Crop cultivation** — growing rice, wheat, vegetables, fruits
- **Animal husbandry** — raising cows, buffaloes, sheep, goats, poultry
- **Forestry** — managing forests, growing timber
- **Horticulture** — growing fruits, flowers, vegetables intensively
- **Pisciculture** — fish farming
- **Apiculture** — beekeeping
- **Sericulture** — raising silkworms for silk
- **Fibre production** — cotton, jute, hemp

Together, these make up Indian agriculture. Every Indian meal you eat — rice, dal, vegetables, milk, paneer, sweets, chai — comes from some combination of these activities.

## How Old Is Indian Farming?

Very old. Among the oldest in the world.

### Mehrgarh — 9,000 Years Ago

At **Mehrgarh** (in present-day Balochistan, then part of the area that would later become the Indus-Saraswati Civilisation), archaeologists have found evidence of **barley and millet cultivation from the 7th millennium BCE** — about 9,000 years ago.

This makes it among the **oldest farming sites in South Asia**, and one of the earliest in the world.

### The Indus-Saraswati Civilisation

By around **2500 BCE**, the Harappans were:
- Growing **wheat** and **barley** as staple crops
- Growing **rice** in some regions
- Cultivating **cotton** (the world's earliest evidence of cotton use)
- Practising **intercropping** — growing two or more crops together in the same field, an advanced technique. Excavated furrows at **Kalibangan** (Rajasthan) show perpendicular intercropping patterns from 2800 BCE — the same technique is used by Indian farmers today, 4,800 years later!

### Vedic and Classical Periods

The **Vedas** mention:
- **Yava** (barley)
- **Godhuma** (wheat)
- **Vrihi** (rice)
- **Sesame**, black gram, various pulses

By classical times, India had ancient farming textbooks. Some of them include:
- **Kautilya's Arthashastra** — detailed rules for state-supported farming
- **Sangam literature** — Tamil poetry describing farming, fishing, and rural life
- **Brihatsamhita** by Varahamihira — astronomical and agricultural knowledge
- **Krishi Parashara** — a complete farming manual
- **Surapala's Vrikshayurveda** — Ayurveda for trees and plants

Some of these texts have remarkable detail. **Surapala** recommended:
- **Seed preparation**: "Seeds sprinkled with milk, rubbed with cow dung, dried and smeared with honey and false black pepper will definitely sprout."
- **Watering newly planted trees**: "Every morning and evening for fifteen days until the soil is fully soaked."
- **Grafting techniques** — still used today

These ancient writings show that Indian farmers were **scientific observers**, learning from every season, sharing knowledge across generations.

## Climate and Crops — What Grows Where

India has **seven different climate types**:

| Climate | Regions |
|---------|---------|
| **Alpine** | High Himalayas |
| **Temperate** | Lower Himalayas |
| **Subtropical** | Northern plains |
| **Arid** | Thar Desert (Rajasthan) |
| **Tropical wet** | Western coastal strip (Kerala, Konkan) |
| **Semi-arid** | Central Deccan Plateau |
| **Tropical** | Eastern and southern peninsula |

Each climate suits different crops:

- **Wheat** — northern plains (Punjab, Haryana, UP)
- **Rice** — eastern and southern India (Bengal, Odisha, Andhra, Tamil Nadu, Kerala) plus wet north (UP, Bihar)
- **Cotton** — Maharashtra, Gujarat, Punjab (semi-arid black soil)
- **Tea** — Assam, Darjeeling, Nilgiris (cool, wet hills)
- **Coffee** — Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu (Western Ghats hills)
- **Spices** — Kerala (pepper, cardamom), Gujarat (cumin), Rajasthan
- **Sugarcane** — UP, Maharashtra (warm, plenty of water)
- **Apples** — Himachal, Kashmir (cool temperate hills)
- **Mangoes** — across most of India, especially UP, AP, Maharashtra
- **Saffron** — only Kashmir (specific cool, dry climate)

The 7th-century Chinese pilgrim **Xuanzang**, who travelled in India, observed:

> "The climate and the quality of the soil being different according to situation, the produce of the land is various in its character. The flowers and plants, the fruits and trees are different kinds, and have distinct names."

He understood India's climate diversity 1,400 years ago.

### Agro-Climatic Zones

For modern planning, India is divided into **15 agro-climatic zones** based on climate, soil, terrain, and vegetation. Examples:
- Upper Gangetic Plain
- Southern Plateau and Hills
- Western Dry Region (Rajasthan deserts)
- East Coast Plains
- West Coast Plains

This helps the government plan: which crops, what seeds, what irrigation, what subsidies are right for each zone.

## The Monsoons Decide Everything

Indian agriculture is heavily dependent on **monsoons** — the seasonal rains.

### The Two Monsoons

1. **Southwest Monsoon** (June to September)
   - Brings rain to most of India
   - Critical for kharif (monsoon) crops
   - When this fails, India faces drought

2. **Northeast Monsoon** (October to December)
   - Brings rain mainly to southeast (Tamil Nadu, eastern AP)
   - Critical for second crops in the south

A failure of the monsoon can mean:
- Crop failure
- Hunger
- Farmer suicides
- Higher food prices for everyone

A strong monsoon can mean:
- Bumper harvests
- Lower prices
- Joyous festivals (Onam in Kerala, Pongal in Tamil Nadu, Lohri in Punjab — all monsoon-linked)

This is why **monsoon forecasting** is a major Indian science. The India Meteorological Department (IMD) tracks weather constantly. Farmers, traders, and governments all watch the monsoon news closely.

## The Three Cropping Seasons

Indian farming has **three main seasons**:

### 1. Kharif (Monsoon, June–October)

- Sown in June with the monsoon
- Harvested in September-October
- **Major crops**: rice (especially in the east), maize, jowar, bajra, sugarcane, groundnut, cotton, soybean, tur

These are crops that **love water and warmth**.

### 2. Rabi (Winter, October–March)

- Sown in October-November
- Harvested in March-April
- **Major crops**: wheat (the big one), barley, peas, mustard, gram (chana), masoor

These are crops that grow in **cool, less water** conditions.

### 3. Zaid (Summer, March–June)

- Short summer season between rabi and kharif
- **Major crops**: watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber, pumpkin, some vegetables

These need **summer heat** and **irrigation**.

### Names from Mughal Times

The words **kharif** and **rabi** are **Arabic** in origin, coming into India with the Mughals. Before that, Sanskrit-origin words were used:
- **Kedara** for wet crops
- **Haimana** for winter crops
- **Graishmika** for summer crops

## The Major Indian Crops

Let us look at the staples:

### Rice

India is the **world's second-largest rice producer** (after China). Rice is grown in:
- West Bengal (#1 producer)
- Uttar Pradesh
- Punjab
- Andhra Pradesh
- Tamil Nadu
- Odisha
- Bihar

Indians eat over **70 kg of rice per person per year** on average. Many southern and eastern Indians eat rice three times a day.

India also **exports basmati rice** — a long-grain aromatic variety prized worldwide. India is the world's largest exporter of basmati.

### Wheat

India is the **world's second-largest wheat producer** (also after China). Most wheat comes from:
- Uttar Pradesh
- Punjab
- Madhya Pradesh
- Haryana
- Rajasthan
- Bihar

Wheat is the staple of north India — chapati, naan, paratha, roti, daliya.

### Pulses (Dal)

India is the **world's largest producer and consumer of pulses** — chana, tur (arhar), urad, moong, masoor. Most pulses come from Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Karnataka, UP.

But India still **imports pulses** because demand exceeds supply. Improving pulse production is a national priority.

### Sugarcane

India is the **world's largest sugarcane producer** (#1). UP and Maharashtra dominate. Sugar mills, jaggery (gur) makers, alcohol producers, and bio-fuel makers all depend on sugarcane.

### Cotton

India is the **world's largest cotton producer** (recently overtook China). Major producing states: Gujarat, Maharashtra, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Punjab, Haryana. Cotton supports India's huge textile industry.

### Tea

India is the **world's second-largest tea producer** (after China). Assam, Darjeeling, Nilgiris are the famous growing regions. Indian tea is consumed at home and exported globally.

### Spices

India is the **world's largest producer and exporter of spices** — turmeric, ginger, cardamom, pepper, cumin, coriander, chillies. The Spice Coast (Kerala) has exported pepper for over 2,000 years.

### Milk

India is the **world's largest milk producer** — about 22-23% of global milk production! The **White Revolution** under Verghese Kurien (the "Father of the White Revolution") transformed India from a milk-deficient country to the world leader through cooperatives like Amul.

## How Indian Farming Has Changed

Let us trace the transformation:

### Traditional Farming (until the 1950s)
- Mostly subsistence — farmers grew enough to feed their family, with little surplus
- Used local seeds passed down generations
- Animal-drawn ploughs, manual labour
- Dependent entirely on monsoon
- Yields were low
- Famines occurred when monsoons failed

### Green Revolution (1960s-70s)

A massive transformation, led by **Dr. M.S. Swaminathan** ("Father of India's Green Revolution") and others:

- **High-yielding variety (HYV) seeds** — wheat and rice varieties that produced far more grain per acre
- **Chemical fertilisers** — urea, DAP
- **Pesticides** — to kill insects damaging crops
- **Irrigation** — tube wells, canals
- **Tractors** — replacing bullocks
- **Government support** — minimum support prices, subsidies

**Results**:
- India went from being a food-importing nation to **self-sufficient in food**
- Wheat production nearly **quadrupled** in 30 years
- Famines stopped occurring

**Problems**:
- Heavy chemical use damaged soil and water
- Groundwater levels fell as more pumps ran
- Punjab and Haryana became dependent on a few crops
- Diabetes and cancer rates rose in heavily-farmed regions
- Small farmers fell into debt to buy seeds, fertilisers, pesticides

So the Green Revolution was a double-edged sword — it solved hunger but created new problems.

### Modern Farming (2000s onwards)

Now India is trying to combine the best of old and new:

- **Drip irrigation** — saving water
- **Solar pumps** — saving electricity
- **Organic farming** — restoring soils
- **Direct-to-consumer apps** — farmers selling without middlemen
- **Mobile-based crop advice** — weather, prices, pest warnings
- **Crop diversification** — moving away from rice/wheat monocultures
- **GM crops** — controversial, but Bt cotton is widely used

### Animal Husbandry Revolution

Beyond crops:
- **White Revolution** — milk
- **Blue Revolution** — fisheries
- **Pink Revolution** — meat and poultry
- **Yellow Revolution** — edible oils
- **Golden Revolution** — fruits and honey
- **Silver Revolution** — eggs

Each has transformed parts of Indian rural life.

## Problems Indian Farmers Face

Indian farming has many achievements, but also serious problems:

1. **Small landholdings** — most farms are tiny (1-2 hectares), making it hard to be profitable
2. **Monsoon dependency** — only about 50% of farmland has irrigation
3. **Soil degradation** — overuse of chemicals
4. **Falling groundwater** — pumps draining aquifers
5. **Low prices** — farmers often can't earn enough to live well
6. **Debt** — many small farmers borrow at high interest from informal lenders
7. **Farmer suicides** — tragically, debt-related suicides are common in some areas
8. **Climate change** — unpredictable monsoons, extreme heat, floods
9. **Pest resistance** — pests evolving to resist pesticides
10. **Lack of insurance** — most farmers have no protection against crop failure

These are not minor issues. Indian agriculture supports nearly half our population. Solving these problems is one of the most important national challenges.

## What India Is Doing

The government and farmers are working on many fronts:
- **PM-KISAN** — direct cash support to farmers
- **Crop insurance** — Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana
- **eNAM** — online national agricultural market
- **Soil Health Cards** — testing and improving soils
- **Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs)** — small farmers banding together
- **Promoting millets** — declared 2023 International Year of Millets
- **Organic farming zones** — Sikkim is 100% organic; others following
- **Solar pumps**, **drip irrigation**, **precision farming** — saving water and energy

The vision: Indian farming that is **productive, profitable, sustainable, and dignified** for the millions who work in it.

## Before We Close

- **Agriculture** has been practised in India for **9,000+ years** (Mehrgarh, 7th millennium BCE).
- About **46% of Indians work in farming**, contributing **18% of GDP**.
- **Over 75% of farmworkers in rural India are women**.
- India has **seven climate types** and **15 agro-climatic zones** — leading to vast crop diversity.
- The **southwest** (Jun-Sep) and **northeast** (Oct-Dec) **monsoons** are critical for Indian agriculture.
- Three cropping seasons: **kharif** (monsoon), **rabi** (winter), **zaid** (summer).
- India is the world's **largest producer of milk, pulses, sugarcane, cotton, and spices**, and **second largest** in rice, wheat, and tea.
- The **Green Revolution** (1960s-70s) made India food-self-sufficient but caused new environmental problems.
- Modern Indian farming combines tradition and technology — drip irrigation, organic methods, FPOs, mobile advisories.
- Indian farmers face serious challenges: small farms, monsoon risk, debt, climate change.
- Solving Indian agriculture's problems is one of the most important national priorities.
