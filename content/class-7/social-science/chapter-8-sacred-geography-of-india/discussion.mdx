## Why Did Indians Make the Whole Country Sacred?

**Child:** Maa, this is unusual. Most cultures have a few holy places. India seems to have made the whole country sacred. Why?

**Mother:** It happened over thousands of years, slowly. Indians didn't sit down and decide "let us make all of India sacred." It evolved.

But the result is striking. By the time of the Guptas (around 1,500 years ago), the entire subcontinent was already covered with sacred sites — temples, rivers, mountains, forests — all connected by pilgrimage routes.

**Child:** What was the use of this?

**Mother:** Many things at once. First, it gave Indians a **sense of one country**. A villager in Tamil Nadu who had never seen the north still knew of Kashi (Varanasi), Haridwar, Badrinath. A villager in Punjab knew of Rameswaram, Madurai, Kanyakumari. Even before maps, before railways, before nations — Indians had a **mental map of India** because of these sacred networks.

Second, it **moved people**. Pilgrims walked across India in huge numbers for centuries. Along the way, they exchanged goods, learned languages, ate new foods, heard new music. Cultures mixed.

Third — and this is wise — it **protected Nature**. When the river is your mother, you don't pollute her. When the forest is the abode of gods, you don't cut it down. When the cow is your mother, you don't kill her carelessly. Whether or not the religious beliefs are literally true, the **effect** was conservation.

**Child:** So sacredness was a kind of glue that held India together?

**Mother:** Yes — and a kind of insurance policy for the environment. Modern societies have to pass laws to protect rivers, forests, animals. Indians had a culture that protected them naturally, through reverence.

**Child:** Did political rulers create this network?

**Mother:** No — that is the interesting part. **No king ordered this**. There was never an emperor who said "let us build pilgrimage networks across India." It grew **organically**, through saints, devotees, traders, and ordinary people, over thousands of years.

This is what makes Indian civilisation unusual. Many great things in India were not built by states — they were built by **society itself**, through tradition, through faith, through habit. Sacred geography is one of these gifts.

## How Did Pilgrimage Networks Help India Stay United?

**Child:** Maa, I want to understand the unity part better. India has so many languages and so many cultures. How could pilgrimage create unity?

**Mother:** Let me give you a thought experiment. Imagine you live in a village in Kerala, 800 years ago. You speak Malayalam. You eat rice and fish. You worship the local goddess. You have never been more than 20 km from your village.

One day, your father announces: we are going on tirthayatra. He has saved for years. We will visit Kashi (Varanasi), 2,500 km away.

The journey takes 5 months. On the way, you cross:
- Tamil Nadu — Tamil-speaking
- Andhra and Karnataka — Telugu and Kannada
- Maharashtra — Marathi
- Madhya Pradesh — Hindi dialects
- Uttar Pradesh — Hindi

You see different clothing styles, different farming, different food. You hear hundreds of songs in dozens of dialects.

But — and this is the key — **everywhere you go, people recognise the names**. When you say "we are going to Kashi," the Marathi family that hosts you says "ah, Kashi! We also want to go someday." When you reach Kashi, you find pilgrims from Punjab, Gujarat, Bengal, Assam — all in the same place.

**Child:** So they all knew the same sacred sites?

**Mother:** Exactly. Even though languages differed, **the sacred sites were shared**. Everyone had heard of Kashi, Prayag, Mathura, Vrindavan, Dwarka, Rameswaram, Puri, Badrinath. These were like landmarks in a shared imagination.

After your journey, you go home to Kerala. Now you have:
- **Met Indians from 10 regions** — and know they are like you in deep ways
- **Heard stories** that you carry back to your village
- **Bought small souvenirs or items** from places you visited
- **Picked up a few words** of other languages
- A sense of **being part of something bigger** than your village

Now multiply this by **millions of pilgrims over thousands of years**.

**Child:** That's a lot of mixing.

**Mother:** Yes. This is why Indians, despite their incredible diversity, felt themselves to be **one civilisation**. They didn't need a king to tell them — they had walked the country themselves, or heard the stories from those who had.

**Child:** So pilgrimage was kind of like... a national network?

**Mother:** A spiritual one, yes. Modern countries unify through television, internet, airlines, common school systems. Ancient India unified through **shared sacred geography**. Different method, same result.

## Why Are Rivers Worshipped in India?

**Child:** Maa, why are rivers worshipped as goddesses? Other cultures don't usually do this.

**Mother:** Think about what rivers do for India. Without rivers, there would be no Indian civilisation.

- **Drinking water** — rivers were the only source of clean water for most communities for thousands of years
- **Agriculture** — rivers irrigated fields; without irrigation, no food
- **Transport** — before roads and trains, rivers carried boats with people and goods
- **Fishing** — rivers gave protein to many communities
- **Bathing and ritual** — rivers were the daily bath, the wedding bath, the funeral pyre
- **Festivals** — many festivals are tied to rivers (Chhath Puja, Ganga Aarti, Pushkaram)
- **Borders** — many states and ancient kingdoms were defined by rivers

The river was not just water. The river was **life itself**.

**Child:** So calling it a goddess was natural?

**Mother:** Yes — but more than natural, it was **wise**. Calling the river a goddess meant:
1. You don't pollute her — that is sin
2. You don't take water carelessly — that is greed
3. Your children learn to respect rivers from their first words
4. The river enters religious rituals and stays present in daily life

Compare this to many modern countries where rivers were polluted to death because they were seen as just "resources" to be used. When rivers are seen as just water, people pollute them. When rivers are seen as goddesses, people protect them.

**Child:** But aren't Indian rivers polluted now?

**Mother:** Sadly, yes. The Ganga, despite being India's most sacred river, is heavily polluted in many places. This is a tragic gap between **what Indians say** and **what Indians do**.

The reasons are modern: factories releasing chemicals, untreated sewage, plastic, religious offerings that don't decompose (flowers, oil lamps, plastic items). When India became modern and industrial, the old reverence weakened.

**Child:** So the old way was actually better at protecting rivers?

**Mother:** In one sense, yes. Traditional Indian society, even when poor, did less damage to rivers than modern industrial India. This is humbling. Modern science is now trying to clean rivers using laws and technology — but **culture is still the deepest protection**. If every Indian child grows up genuinely seeing the Ganga as Maa Ganga, no factory owner would dare pollute her.

That is what the Namami Gange and other government cleanup programmes also need — not just rules, but **revival of reverence**.

**Child:** So worshipping rivers was actually a kind of environmental policy?

**Mother:** A profound one. India's ancient sages may not have known modern chemistry, but they knew human nature. They knew that **people protect what they love**. So they made the rivers loveable — gave them names, stories, personalities, motherhood. And for thousands of years, this kept India's rivers cleaner than they would otherwise have been.

Today, we have to combine the old reverence with the new science — pollution control, water treatment, plastic bans, plus the deep cultural respect that says "the river is my mother." Both together can save India's rivers.

## What Are Sacred Groves and Why Do They Matter?

**Child:** Maa, you mentioned sacred groves. What are they?

**Mother:** Across India — especially in tribal and rural areas — there are small patches of forest that have been **left untouched for hundreds of years** because they are believed to be the home of a local deity. Cutting trees there is forbidden. Hunting is forbidden. Even taking dead wood is sometimes forbidden.

These are **sacred groves**.

There are estimated to be **over 100,000 sacred groves** across India. Some examples:
- **Mawphlang** sacred grove in Meghalaya — preserved by the Khasi people
- **Devarakadu** groves in Karnataka — preserved by villagers
- **Orans** in Rajasthan — sacred deserts where the Bishnoi community protects trees and animals
- **Sarna** groves in Jharkhand and Odisha — sacred to many tribal communities

**Child:** So these are forests preserved by religion?

**Mother:** Yes. And modern scientists have discovered something remarkable: **sacred groves are biodiversity treasures**. Because they have been untouched for centuries, they contain:
- **Rare plants** that have disappeared from surrounding farmland
- **Medicinal plants** known only to local healers
- **Old-growth trees** hundreds of years old
- **Wildlife** that has lost habitat elsewhere
- **Water sources** — small streams that wouldn't survive in cleared land

In effect, these communities — without ecology degrees, without scientific training — created **nature reserves** thousands of years before the modern world invented the concept.

**Child:** That's amazing. So traditional knowledge was actually scientific?

**Mother:** In its effects, yes. The communities believed the gods would punish anyone who cut trees in the grove. We can debate the theology, but the **practical effect** was perfect conservation.

This is one of the lessons of India: **scientific wisdom can take many forms**. The Bishnoi community in Rajasthan has been protecting trees and blackbucks for 500 years — because of their religion. The Khasi people of Meghalaya have protected groves for centuries. Their methods are different from modern environmental science, but their results are sometimes better.

**Child:** Are sacred groves being protected today?

**Mother:** Some are. The Indian government and many NGOs are working to recognise sacred groves as protected areas. State governments like Sikkim and Maharashtra have officially recognised many such sites.

But many sacred groves have also been lost — to roads, mining, agriculture, modern development. Some communities have weakened their traditions because of religious change, urbanisation, or pressure from outside.

This is a race between **preservation and destruction**. Every sacred grove lost is a small library of nature destroyed. Every one saved is a victory.

**Child:** What can ordinary people do?

**Mother:** Several things:
1. **Learn about local sacred groves** — they exist in most Indian states
2. **Support tribal and rural communities** who protect them — they are doing what governments cannot
3. **Visit respectfully** — without disturbing, without polluting
4. **Tell others** — most urban Indians don't know these exist
5. **Plant trees** in your own areas — keep the tradition alive
6. **Question development plans** that threaten such groves

These groves are India's hidden inheritance. They belong to all of us, but only some communities have protected them. If India is to retain its rich biodiversity, the rest of us have to help.

## What's the Difference Between Worship and Religion Here?

**Child:** Maa, sometimes I'm confused. Indians worship rivers, mountains, trees, animals. Is this really religion or is this something else?

**Mother:** Excellent question. It's something else — or rather, it's **wider than religion as Western thought defines it**.

When people in some Western traditions hear "worship," they think of bowing before a single god. The idea of bowing before a river or a tree sometimes strikes them as primitive or superstitious.

But Indian thought has always been different. Indian sages saw **divinity present in everything**. The river is divine because the divine is in everything. The mountain is divine for the same reason. The tree, the cow, the snake, the rock, the human — all divine. Not because they are gods, but because **god is in them**.

**Child:** So everything has god in it?

**Mother:** Yes — this is a deep idea in Indian philosophy called **panentheism** (god is in all things). It's not the same as polytheism (many gods) or monotheism (one god) — it's a third view: one ultimate reality that manifests as everything we see.

The Upanishads say: **"Ekam sat, vipra bahudha vadanti"** — "Truth is one, the wise call it by many names."

So when an Indian bows before a river, she is not bowing to the water — she is bowing to the **divine that is present in the water**. When she touches the feet of an elder, she is bowing to the divine in that person. When she circles a holy tree, she is honouring the divine in the tree.

**Child:** That's a different way of looking at religion.

**Mother:** Yes. It also explains why Indian culture has been so **inclusive**. If god is everywhere, then no one religion has the monopoly on truth. The Buddha, Mahavira, Guru Nanak, Jesus, Muhammad — all could be seen as different teachers of the same truth.

This is why religions have coexisted in India for so long. Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism — all born in India. Christianity, Islam, Zoroastrianism — all welcomed in India. There were conflicts, yes, but the underlying culture said: **truth has many names**.

**Child:** Maa, so this isn't really primitive at all. It's actually quite sophisticated.

**Mother:** Some of the deepest philosophy in human history is hidden in what looks like simple ritual. A village grandmother lighting a lamp before a tulsi plant is enacting a thousand-year-old philosophical insight — that the divine is everywhere, even in the small plant in her courtyard.

The form is simple. The meaning is profound. This is one of India's gifts to the world: **religion that doesn't divide people, that doesn't insist on one name for god, that sees the holy in everything**.

When the rest of the world is learning to respect environments, animals, and other religions — they are catching up with what Indian villagers have known for 3,000 years.
