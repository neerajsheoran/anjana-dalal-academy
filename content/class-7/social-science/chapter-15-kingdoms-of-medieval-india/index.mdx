## A New Era After the Guptas

The Gupta Empire had unified north India for two centuries (~320–550 CE), producing one of India's great cultural ages. But empires don't last forever. After the Guptas, India entered a different phase — **no single empire**, but **many strong regional kingdoms**.

This chapter covers approximately **600 to 1000 CE** — four centuries when India was politically fragmented but culturally vibrant. Some historians call this period "post-classical" or "early medieval." But the names matter less than understanding what was happening.

Imagine yourself as a traveller in this India:
- **In the north**, you visit **Kannauj**, a city where King **Harshavardhana** hosts poets and scholars.
- **In the Deccan**, the **Chalukyas** rule a kingdom of grand temples and cities.
- **In the deep south**, the **Pallavas** carve entire temples out of rock at **Mamallapuram**.
- **In Bengal**, the **Palas** support universities like **Vikramashila** and continue the legacy of **Nalanda**.

You see no large empire — but you see incredible **artistic, religious, and intellectual creativity**. This is the time when many of India's greatest temples are built. When the Bhakti movement begins. When Indian Buddhism spreads to Tibet and Southeast Asia. When Arab traders arrive on India's coasts.

So is this the **end** of an era of empires — or the **dawn** of something new? Let us see.

## Harshavardhana — The Last Great Northern Emperor (606–647 CE)

In the early 7th century, a brilliant young king named **Harshavardhana** rose in North India.

### Background

Harsha was the son of Prabhakaravardhana, king of Thanesar (in modern Haryana). After his elder brother was assassinated, Harsha became king at just **16 years old**.

In a few years, he built one of the most extensive kingdoms in post-Gupta North India:
- Ruled from **Kannauj** (his new capital)
- Controlled much of Punjab, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Bengal, Odisha, Rajasthan
- Tried to expand south but was checked by the Chalukyas at the Narmada

### Harsha as Patron

Harsha was a great patron of learning and the arts:
- He himself wrote three Sanskrit plays — **Ratnavali**, **Priyadarshika**, and **Nagananda**
- He hosted a **grand religious assembly at Kannauj** that lasted weeks
- Held similar assemblies at **Prayag** every 5 years where he gave away vast wealth
- Patronised Buddhists, Hindus, and Jains equally
- Supported the great university of **Nalanda**

### Xuanzang's Visit

The Chinese pilgrim **Xuanzang** (Hsuan Tsang) visited India during Harsha's reign (630–644 CE) and spent **17 years** in India.

Xuanzang's accounts give us priceless details:
- He studied at **Nalanda** for years
- Met Harsha personally; the king honoured him
- Described India's cities, climate, customs, religions
- Took back over **600 Sanskrit texts** to China
- His translations and writings became major sources for Chinese Buddhism

Xuanzang's travels are one of the great cultural bridges in world history. The friendship between Harsha and Xuanzang shows India's continuing role as a global centre of learning.

### After Harsha

Harsha died in 647 CE without an heir. His kingdom dissolved. North India returned to fragmentation. No major emperor emerged in the north for centuries.

## The Tripartite Struggle (8th–9th Centuries)

After Harsha, three regional powers competed for control of **Kannauj** — which remained the most prestigious throne in north India:

1. **The Gurjara-Pratiharas** — from western India (Rajasthan, Gujarat)
2. **The Palas** — from eastern India (Bengal, Bihar)
3. **The Rashtrakutas** — from the Deccan (Maharashtra)

Each tried multiple times to take and hold Kannauj. None succeeded for long. This **Tripartite Struggle** lasted nearly two centuries.

### The Pratiharas (Gurjara-Pratiharas)

The Pratiharas dominated north and west India from about 730 to 1000 CE.

- King **Nagabhata I** repelled an Arab invasion in the 8th century
- King **Mihira Bhoja** (9th century) was their greatest ruler — controlled much of north India
- They were great patrons of art — built the **Khajuraho** temples (in Madhya Pradesh, UNESCO World Heritage)
- Were skilled cavalry warriors

### The Palas

The Palas ruled Bengal and Bihar from about 750 to 1150 CE — one of the longest dynasties in Indian history.

- Founded by **Gopala**, who was elected (an interesting democratic origin!)
- Reached their peak under **Dharmapala** (8th-9th century) and **Devapala**
- Great patrons of **Buddhism** — founded the universities of **Vikramashila** and **Somapura**
- Supported **Nalanda University**
- Through their support, Buddhism spread to Tibet, Nepal, Southeast Asia, China

### The Rashtrakutas

The Rashtrakutas ruled the Deccan from about 750 to 973 CE.

- Their greatest king was **Krishna I** (8th century)
- He built the astonishing **Kailasa Temple at Ellora** — carved out of a single rock! It is one of the most amazing architectural achievements anywhere
- Patronised Sanskrit and regional literature
- Held influence over much of South India

The Tripartite Struggle weakened all three, which is one reason why later Turkish invasions found India fragmented and unable to mount unified defence.

## The Pallavas — South India's Innovators (3rd–9th Centuries)

The **Pallavas** ruled parts of Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh from about the 3rd to 9th centuries. Their capital was **Kanchipuram**, one of India's seven sacred cities.

### Their Achievements

1. **Mamallapuram (Mahabalipuram)** — a coastal town near Chennai. The Pallavas built astonishing rock-cut and stone temples here, including:
   - The **Shore Temple** (8th century, UNESCO World Heritage)
   - The **Pancha Rathas** — five chariot-shaped temples carved from single rocks
   - The **Descent of the Ganga** — massive bas-relief carving on a rock face
   - Many cave temples

2. **Pioneered South Indian temple architecture** — laid foundations for the great Dravidian temple style.

3. **Patronised Tamil literature** — the Pallava period saw great Tamil and Sanskrit poets.

4. **Bhakti tradition** — many of the early Tamil Bhakti saints (Alvars and Nayanars) lived and composed under Pallava patronage.

5. **Maritime trade** — connected India with Southeast Asia.

6. **King Narasimhavarman II** built much of Mamallapuram.

7. **Music and dance** — the Pallavas patronised the development of Bharatanatyam dance.

## The Chalukyas — Lords of the Deccan

The **Chalukyas** were a major Deccan dynasty (6th–12th centuries).

### Western Chalukyas (6th–8th centuries)

- Capital at **Vatapi (Badami)** in Karnataka
- Greatest king: **Pulakeshin II** (early 7th century), who defeated even Harshavardhana
- Built magnificent **rock-cut and structural temples** at Badami, Aihole, Pattadakal
- **Aihole and Pattadakal** are UNESCO World Heritage Sites — early examples of South Indian temple architecture

### Eastern Chalukyas

- Ruled coastal Andhra
- Capital at Vengi

### Later Chalukyas

- Different branches of the family ruled various parts of the Deccan into the 12th century
- Continued to patronise temple-building and Sanskrit/Kannada literature

## The Cholas — South India's Greatest Empire

The **Cholas** are one of the longest-ruling dynasties in world history. They have ancient roots (mentioned even in Ashoka's inscriptions), but rose to greatness from the 9th century onwards.

### The Imperial Cholas (850–1250 CE)

- **Vijayalaya Chola** (mid-9th century) restored the dynasty after centuries of decline
- **Rajaraja Chola I** (985–1014 CE) — one of India's greatest emperors. Built the **Brihadeeswara Temple at Thanjavur** — one of the most magnificent in India, UNESCO World Heritage.
- **Rajendra Chola I** (1014–1044 CE) — son of Rajaraja. Conquered as far as the Ganga in the north (he brought back Ganga water to consecrate his new capital), and led naval expeditions to **Sri Lanka, Maldives, and Southeast Asia** (reached Srivijaya, in present-day Indonesia).

### Why the Cholas Were Special

1. **Naval power** — the only South Asian empire ever to mount naval expeditions across the Indian Ocean.

2. **Temple architecture** — Brihadeeswara is one of the world's great buildings.

3. **Bronze sculpture** — Chola bronzes (especially the Dancing Shiva or **Nataraja**) are among the greatest art ever made.

4. **Administration** — sophisticated revenue and judicial systems.

5. **Village democracy** — the **Uttaramerur inscriptions** (10th century) describe local elections with sealed ballots and anti-corruption rules — 1,000 years before modern democracy. Already covered in Chapter 9.

6. **Trade** — Chola merchants reached Southeast Asia, China, the Arab world.

7. **Literature** — Tamil and Sanskrit flourished.

The Cholas were arguably the most successful Indian dynasty during this period.

## Arab Invasions and Arrivals

While the Indian kingdoms were busy with internal struggles, a new force was rising in West Asia — **Islam** and the **Arab Caliphate**.

### Early Arab Encounters

- **637 CE** — first Arab naval attack on Thana (near Mumbai), but was repulsed.
- **712 CE** — Arabs under **Muhammad bin Qasim** conquered **Sindh** (in present-day Pakistan). This was the first lasting Muslim presence in South Asia.

### Arab Influence

The Arabs in Sindh:
- Ruled for a few centuries
- Traded extensively
- Brought new ideas and technologies
- Initially encountered Indian astronomy, mathematics, medicine — and translated many Indian texts into Arabic
- This is how **Indian numerals** (with zero) reached the Arab world, and from there to Europe

### Later Turkish Invasions

The Arabs in Sindh remained a regional power. The big invasions that would reshape India came later — Turkish raids from Afghanistan, especially:
- **Mahmud of Ghazni** — 17 raids on India between 1000-1027 CE; mostly looted wealth, didn't establish lasting rule
- **Muhammad of Ghor** — late 12th century, established the Delhi Sultanate

These come in the next chapter.

## The Rise of Bhakti

While politics fragmented, something extraordinary was happening in religion — the **Bhakti movement**.

Bhakti means **personal devotion to god**. Bhakti poets and saints offered a radical alternative to ritualistic religion:
- Direct personal connection with the divine
- Songs and poems in **local languages** (Tamil, then later Marathi, Kannada, Hindi, Bengali)
- Open to **all castes and genders**
- Rejection of empty ritual
- Universalism

### Tamil Bhakti

The Bhakti movement began in Tamil Nadu around the **6th-7th centuries**. Two streams:

- **Alvars** — 12 Vaishnavite saints who composed devotional poems to Vishnu. Most famous: **Andal**, the only female Alvar.
- **Nayanars** — 63 Shaivite saints who composed devotional poems to Shiva. Most famous: **Appar, Sundarar, Sambandar, Manikkavachakar**.

Their poems are some of the most powerful devotional literature in world history. They were composed in Tamil, accessible to common people.

### Spread of Bhakti

In the centuries that followed, Bhakti spread northward:
- **Adi Shankaracharya** (8th century) revived Advaita Vedanta — though more philosophical than purely Bhakti
- **Ramanujacharya** (11th-12th century) — systematised Vishishtadvaita and Vaishnavism
- **Madhvacharya** (13th century) — Dvaita philosophy
- Later: Kabir, Mirabai, Tulsidas, Surdas, Tukaram, Namdev, Guru Nanak, Chaitanya Mahaprabhu, and many more

Bhakti would transform Indian spiritual life for centuries to come.

## Cultural Vitality During Political Fragmentation

What is remarkable about this period is that **politics fragmented, but culture flourished**.

### Temple Architecture
- Pallavas at Mamallapuram
- Chalukyas at Badami, Aihole, Pattadakal
- Rashtrakutas at Ellora (especially Kailasa Temple)
- Cholas at Thanjavur
- Pratiharas at Khajuraho
- Hoysalas later in Karnataka
- And countless regional temples

### Sculpture
- Bronze casting (Chola Nataraja statues)
- Rock-cut sculpture (Ellora caves)
- Stone temple sculpture (Khajuraho)

### Literature
- Sanskrit continued — Bharavi, Magha, Kalidasa's successors
- Tamil literature reached new heights
- Regional literatures began (Kannada, Telugu, Marathi)

### Education
- Nalanda continued and reached its peak under the Palas
- New universities: Vikramashila, Somapura, Odantapuri
- Indian scholars went to Tibet, China, Southeast Asia

### Trade
- Pallavas, Cholas traded with Southeast Asia
- Pratiharas connected with West Asia
- Indian Ocean trade flourished

### Science
- Mathematics continued — Brahmagupta (7th century) systematised rules for zero
- Astronomy developed
- Medicine continued through Ayurveda

So this was a period of **decentralised brilliance**. Without imperial unity, regional creativity exploded.

## Religious Coexistence

Hindus, Buddhists, Jains, and now Muslims all coexisted in India during this period:

- **Hinduism** was the largest religion, but had many internal variations (Shaivism, Vaishnavism, Shakta, local cults)
- **Buddhism** was strong in the east (Pala kingdom) and in Sri Lanka, Tibet, Southeast Asia
- **Jainism** was strong in western India (Rajasthan, Gujarat, Karnataka)
- **Islam** had arrived in Sindh (712 CE) and was practised by Arab merchants on the coasts
- **Christianity** had been in Kerala since the 1st century CE (Saint Thomas tradition)
- **Judaism** had small communities in Kerala
- **Zoroastrianism** — Parsis began arriving from Persia around the 8th-10th century

This religious pluralism is one of the great features of Indian history.

## Before We Close

- The period **600–1000 CE** was post-Gupta — no single empire, but many strong regional kingdoms.
- **Harshavardhana** (606–647 CE) ruled a large North Indian kingdom from **Kannauj**; hosted **Xuanzang** (Chinese pilgrim).
- After Harsha, the **Tripartite Struggle** between **Pratiharas, Palas, and Rashtrakutas** dominated north Indian politics.
- The **Pratiharas** built Khajuraho temples; the **Palas** supported Nalanda, Vikramashila, and Buddhism's spread; the **Rashtrakutas** built the **Kailasa Temple at Ellora**.
- The **Pallavas** of South India built **Mamallapuram** and pioneered Dravidian temple architecture.
- The **Chalukyas** built temples at Badami, Aihole, Pattadakal.
- The **Cholas** rose from the 9th century; **Rajaraja Chola I** built **Brihadeeswara Temple**; **Rajendra Chola I** launched naval expeditions to Southeast Asia.
- The **Bhakti movement** began in Tamil Nadu (6th-7th c.) and would transform Indian religion.
- **Arabs conquered Sindh in 712 CE** — the first lasting Muslim presence in South Asia.
- Despite political fragmentation, this was an era of **cultural brilliance** — temples, sculpture, literature, education, trade — all flourishing across regions.
- Religious pluralism — Hindus, Buddhists, Jains, Muslims, Christians, Zoroastrians, Jews — all present in India by 1000 CE.
