## A Time of Two Stories

The 11th and 12th centuries CE were a period of profound transition in Indian history. Two parallel stories were unfolding at the same time.

**Story 1: Foreign invasions.** Turkic warriors from Central Asia — first **Mahmud of Ghazni**, then **Muhammad of Ghor** — invaded India repeatedly. The 712 CE Arab conquest of Sindh had been limited; these new invasions reached far deeper. By 1200 CE, foreign rule would be established over Delhi — beginning a new chapter in Indian history.

**Story 2: Indian flourishing.** While the north was attacked, India elsewhere continued to flourish. The **Cholas** were at their peak. Magnificent temples were built. **Bhaskaracharya** wrote the **Lilavati**, one of the great mathematical works of all time. The philosopher **Ramanuja** founded **Vishishtadvaita Vedanta**. Indian poets, saints, and scientists produced extraordinary work.

This chapter tells both stories — the invasions that changed India's political destiny, and the resilient civilisation that continued to create even under threat.

## Mahmud of Ghazni — The Raider

**Mahmud of Ghazni** (971–1030 CE) was the Sultan of Ghazni (in present-day Afghanistan). He led **seventeen raids on India** between 1000 and 1027 CE.

### Who Was Mahmud?

Mahmud was a Turkic warrior-king from Central Asia. His empire was based in Ghazni and included parts of Persia, Afghanistan, and Central Asia. He was a brilliant military leader and a patron of the arts (his court included the great Persian poet **Firdausi**, who wrote the **Shahnameh**, and the scholar **Al-Biruni**, who wrote about India).

### His Raids on India

Between **1000 and 1027 CE**, Mahmud raided India seventeen times. His pattern:
- Strike fast with cavalry
- Defeat the Hindu Shahis (in present-day Pakistan and Afghanistan)
- Loot temples and cities for their wealth
- Carry treasures back to Ghazni
- Repeat

Major targets included **Mathura, Kannauj, Thanesar**, and most famously **Somnath**.

### The Destruction of Somnath (1026 CE)

In **1026 CE**, Mahmud attacked **Somnath**, one of the most sacred Shiva temples in India, located on the Gujarat coast. He smashed the famous **Shivalinga**, killed thousands of defenders, and carried away enormous wealth.

Somnath's destruction became symbolic of:
- The vulnerability of Indian kingdoms
- The fanaticism of some early invaders
- The fragmentation that allowed such attacks to succeed
- The depth of cultural memory — Somnath has been **rebuilt seven times**, most recently after independence under Sardar Patel

### Why Did Indian Kingdoms Fail to Stop Him?

Several reasons:
- **Disunity**: Indian rulers fought each other rather than uniting
- **Military style**: Indian armies relied on elephants, slow tactical formations, and traditional rules of warfare. Mahmud used fast cavalry and surprise attacks.
- **Wealth made temples targets**: Indian temples accumulated huge treasure over centuries, making them irresistible prizes
- **No early-warning system**: communication between kingdoms was slow

### What Mahmud Did NOT Do

Importantly, Mahmud was a **raider**, not a conqueror. He didn't try to settle in India or establish lasting rule. After each raid, he returned to Ghazni with loot. His ambitions were in Central Asia, not South Asia.

So while his raids caused great destruction, they did not yet create a Muslim empire in India. That would come a century later.

### Al-Biruni — The Scholar Who Came with Mahmud

In Mahmud's court was a remarkable scholar: **Abu Rayhan al-Biruni** (973–1048 CE), born in present-day Uzbekistan.

Al-Biruni was a polymath — astronomer, mathematician, geographer, anthropologist, philosopher. He travelled with Mahmud's campaigns and **spent years in India**.

He wrote **Kitab al-Hind** ("The Book of India") — one of the most detailed accounts of medieval India by a foreign observer. He:
- Learned **Sanskrit**
- Studied Indian sciences (astronomy, mathematics, medicine)
- Translated Indian texts into Arabic
- Described Indian society, religion, customs, philosophy
- Compared Indian and Greek thought

His account is invaluable because it shows medieval India through outside eyes. He admired Indian science and philosophy, while also criticising aspects he found problematic.

So even amid destruction, intellectual exchange continued.

## Muhammad of Ghor — The Conqueror

A century after Mahmud, another Turkic ruler came to India — but this one came to **stay**.

**Muhammad of Ghor** (also called Muhammad Ghori or Shihab-ud-din) ruled the Ghurid dynasty from Afghanistan in the late 12th century. Unlike Mahmud, he was interested in **conquest and settlement**, not just raids.

### His Indian Campaigns

Muhammad of Ghor invaded India from the 1170s onwards. His campaigns culminated in two crucial battles against the great Rajput king **Prithviraj III**, ruler of the Chauhan dynasty based in Delhi and Ajmer.

#### First Battle of Tarain (1191 CE)

Muhammad Ghor's army met Prithviraj III's army at Tarain (near present-day Karnal, Haryana). **Prithviraj won decisively**. Muhammad Ghor barely escaped with his life.

This victory should have ended Muhammad Ghor's Indian ambitions. But Prithviraj — perhaps believing in Indian codes of chivalry — let Muhammad Ghor go, instead of pursuing or killing him.

#### Second Battle of Tarain (1192 CE)

Muhammad Ghor returned the next year with a larger, better-prepared army. The **Second Battle of Tarain** was different. The Ghurids:
- Brought 120,000 troops
- Used faster cavalry with better tactics
- Attacked at dawn while Indian troops were unprepared
- Used psychological warfare

**Prithviraj III was defeated** and killed (or captured and killed). His army was destroyed.

This battle is one of the **turning points of Indian history**. The defeat at Tarain meant:
- Delhi and much of north India fell to Muhammad Ghor's general **Qutb-ud-din Aibak**
- Lasting Muslim rule was established in India
- The Delhi Sultanate emerged (1206 CE)
- Indian civilisation would be transformed over the next 500 years

### Prithviraj III — The Tragic Hero

**Prithviraj III** (1166–1192 CE) is one of the great tragic figures of Indian history. He was:
- King of the Chauhan dynasty (Ajmer and Delhi)
- Famous for his bravery and chivalry
- Skilled in archery, swordsmanship, languages
- Subject of the epic poem **Prithviraj Raso** by his court poet **Chand Bardai**

His story is still told in Rajasthan — his victories in the First Battle of Tarain, his defeat in the Second, his death, and his legend. He represents both the brilliance of Rajput valour and the cost of disunity among Indian rulers.

### Qutb-ud-din Aibak — Founder of Delhi Sultanate

After the Second Battle of Tarain, Muhammad Ghor returned to Afghanistan, leaving his Turkish general **Qutb-ud-din Aibak** in charge of India.

In 1206 CE, Aibak founded the **Delhi Sultanate** — the first lasting Muslim state in India. The Sultanate would last over 300 years before being absorbed into the Mughal Empire.

Aibak also built the famous **Qutb Minar** in Delhi — one of the tallest brick minarets in the world, still standing today.

This was the beginning of a new era. The next 600 years of Indian history would be shaped by the interaction (sometimes conflict, sometimes cooperation, sometimes synthesis) between indigenous Indian traditions and Islamic civilisation.

## Bakhtiyar Khilji and the Destruction of Nalanda

Among Muhammad Ghor's generals was a man named **Bakhtiyar Khilji**. In the early 13th century, he led campaigns into eastern India.

In around 1200 CE, Bakhtiyar Khilji destroyed **Nalanda University** — one of the greatest centres of learning in human history.

### Nalanda — A Crown Jewel Lost

**Nalanda** had operated continuously for **800 years** (5th to 13th century CE). It was:
- A Buddhist university with rooms for 10,000 students and 2,000 teachers
- An international centre — students came from China, Korea, Japan, Tibet, Sri Lanka, Indonesia, Persia, Greece
- A repository of Buddhist, Hindu, and Jain texts
- An advanced institution teaching logic, philosophy, medicine, astronomy, mathematics, art

The destruction was catastrophic:
- **Buildings burned** — Nalanda's massive multi-story structures, painstakingly built over centuries
- **Library destroyed** — it is said the library was so vast it burned for **months** (some accounts say six months)
- **Monks killed or fled** — many escaped to Tibet, carrying knowledge with them
- **Centuries of scholarship erased** — texts in Sanskrit, Pali, Tibetan, Chinese, all lost

This is one of the greatest cultural losses in human history. We can never fully know what we lost. Modern Nalanda University, opened in 2014, attempts to revive the tradition in a small way.

Other Buddhist universities — **Vikramashila, Somapura, Odantapuri** — were also destroyed during this period.

The destruction of Nalanda is often cited as a turning point: India had been the world's intellectual centre for centuries; after this period, it never quite reclaimed that role.

### Bakhtiyar Khilji's Later Fate

After destroying Nalanda and Bihar's Buddhist heritage, Bakhtiyar Khilji invaded Bengal and then Assam (Kamrupa). In Kamrupa, he encountered fierce resistance from the **Ahom** and **Koch** kingdoms. His army was defeated in 1206 CE. He retreated, ill and humiliated, and died shortly after.

This shows that not all of India fell easily. Northeast India successfully resisted Turkish conquest for centuries longer.

## The Chola Apex and Decline

While the north was being invaded, **South India was at the peak of its glory**.

### Rajendra Chola I (1014–1044 CE)

Son of the great Rajaraja Chola. Among his achievements:
- **Northern expedition**: marched all the way to the Ganga; brought back Ganga water to consecrate his new capital
- **Naval expedition (1025 CE)**: launched a fleet that attacked the **Srivijaya Empire** in Southeast Asia. Captured the Srivijaya king.
- **New capital**: built **Gangaikonda Cholapuram** ("the city of the Chola who brought the Ganga") with a temple to rival his father's at Thanjavur.
- **Empire**: at its peak, the Chola empire stretched from Sri Lanka and the Maldives to Bengal, with influence over Southeast Asia.

### Chola Decline

After Rajendra, the Cholas declined slowly over the 12th century. By the late 13th century, the empire had broken up. Successor dynasties (Pandyas, Pallavas, others) inherited parts. But Chola achievements lived on in temples, bronzes, literature, and Tamil identity.

## Other Indian Kingdoms in This Period

### The Paramaras

The Paramaras ruled western Madhya Pradesh from around 800 to 1300 CE. Their greatest king was **Bhoja** (reigned 1010-1055 CE).

**King Bhoja** was extraordinary:
- A great warrior who defeated several enemies
- A scholar who wrote (or sponsored) **84 books** on subjects from architecture to grammar to yoga to astronomy
- A patron of poets and scientists
- Built **Bhojpur** (with a massive Shiva linga and a planned but unfinished temple)
- His capital **Dhar** became a centre of Sanskrit learning

Bhoja is one of the few Indian kings remembered for **learning** rather than just military success. He shows that Indian kingship at its best combined warrior virtue with scholarly excellence.

### The Kakatiyas

The Kakatiyas of **Warangal** (present-day Telangana) rose in the 12th century. They:
- Built magnificent temples (the famous **Thousand Pillar Temple** at Warangal, the **Ramappa Temple** — UNESCO World Heritage)
- Promoted Telugu language
- Built sophisticated irrigation tanks
- Ruled until conquered by the Delhi Sultanate in the 14th century

### The Hoysalas

The Hoysalas of Karnataka (11th-14th centuries) built some of India's most exquisitely sculpted temples:
- **Belur** — Chennakeshava Temple
- **Halebidu** — Hoysaleshwara Temple
- **Somnathpur** — Keshava Temple

Hoysala temples have **star-shaped platforms** and are covered with incredibly detailed sculptures — every figure, every animal, every leaf carved with extraordinary precision. The Halebidu/Belur complex is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

### The Pala Decline and Sena Rise

In eastern India, the long Pala dynasty (which had ruled Bengal for 400 years) declined. The **Sena dynasty** took over in the late 11th century. The Senas:
- Were great patrons of Sanskrit literature
- Promoted Hinduism (the Palas had favoured Buddhism)
- Built temples
- Fell to Bakhtiyar Khilji in the early 13th century

## Great Indian Scientists of This Period

Despite political turmoil, Indian science continued to advance.

### Bhaskaracharya (1114–1185 CE)

**Bhaskaracharya** (Bhaskara II) was one of the greatest mathematicians and astronomers in world history. He worked from Ujjain (Madhya Pradesh).

His major works:
- **Lilavati** — a textbook on arithmetic, named after his daughter. Full of charming verses and practical problems.
- **Bijaganita** — on algebra
- **Siddhanta Shiromani** — a comprehensive astronomical treatise

His achievements:
- Proved **negative numbers** could be solutions of equations
- Developed methods for **quadratic, cubic, and quartic equations**
- Anticipated calculus by hundreds of years (rules for derivatives)
- Calculated the **sidereal year** (time Earth takes to orbit the sun) to 365.2588 days — accurate to within 3 minutes of the modern value
- Worked on **trigonometry**, calendar calculations, planetary motions

Bhaskaracharya's works were translated into Arabic and Persian and influenced mathematics across the Islamic world and eventually Europe. He is sometimes called **"the lion of mathematicians."**

### Ramanujacharya (1017–1137 CE)

**Ramanujacharya** was one of India's greatest philosophers. He founded **Vishishtadvaita Vedanta** — "qualified non-dualism."

His ideas:
- The Supreme Reality (Brahman) is **personal** — has qualities, can be loved
- The world and individual souls are real (not just illusion)
- The path to liberation is **devotion (bhakti)** combined with knowledge
- All beings, regardless of caste, can attain liberation through bhakti

Ramanuja's philosophy challenged the older Advaita (non-dualism) of Shankaracharya. His Vishishtadvaita became the basis of the great Sri Vaishnava tradition. Temples like **Srirangam** (Tamil Nadu) and many others trace their lineage to him.

### Other Notable Figures

- **Hemachandra** (1089–1173) — Jain scholar and polymath; advisor to Chalukya king Kumarapala of Gujarat
- **Jayadeva** (12th century) — Sanskrit poet, author of **Gita Govinda** (the love-songs of Krishna and Radha)
- **Kalhana** (12th century) — Kashmiri historian, wrote **Rajatarangini** ("River of Kings") — one of the earliest Indian historical chronicles
- **Sayana** and **Madhava** (14th century, slightly later) — would carry the tradition forward

## Building Continues — and Beyond

Even during invasions, temple-building continued in India. Beyond what we have already mentioned:

- **Konark Sun Temple** in Odisha (early 13th century, built by Narasimhadeva I of the Eastern Ganga dynasty)
- **Jagannath Temple at Puri** (12th century)
- **Khajuraho temples** (under the Chandelas, peaked 10th-12th c.)
- **Modhera Sun Temple** in Gujarat (11th century, Solankis)
- **Lingaraja Temple** in Bhubaneswar (11th century)

Meanwhile, abroad:
- **Angkor Wat** in Cambodia (12th century) — the largest religious monument ever built. Originally Hindu (dedicated to Vishnu), built by King Suryavarman II. Shows how far Indian temple-building influence had spread.

## A Civilisation Under Strain — But Not Broken

By 1200 CE:
- Foreign rule was established in Delhi
- Some great Indian institutions (Nalanda) were destroyed
- Many temples were damaged
- Indian kingdoms had been weakened
- A new chapter — the Delhi Sultanate — was beginning

Yet:
- Most of India remained under Hindu rulers (the Sultanate would expand only over time)
- Indian civilisation continued to produce scientists, philosophers, poets, architects
- Religious life continued — and the Bhakti movement, already started, would soon transform Indian spirituality
- Trade continued
- Cultural influence abroad continued
- The Cholas were still strong in the south
- Northeast India had repelled invasions

So the 11th-12th centuries were a time of **turning** — old patterns gave way, new patterns emerged. Indian civilisation showed great **resilience** even as it was challenged. The next centuries would see continued struggle, but also the emergence of one of India's greatest periods of cultural fusion — the Mughal era, with its synthesis of Indian and Persian-Islamic traditions.
