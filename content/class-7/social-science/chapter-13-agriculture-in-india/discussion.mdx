## Why Are Indian Farmers Often Poor When India Is a Major Food Producer?

**Child:** Maa, India produces so much food — milk, rice, wheat, sugarcane. Why are so many Indian farmers poor?

**Mother:** This is one of the deepest paradoxes of India. We are an **agricultural superpower** in terms of production, but our farmers are often the **poorest section** of society. Let me explain.

**1. Small landholdings.**

The average Indian farm is about **1-2 hectares** (2.5-5 acres). For comparison:
- A typical US farm: **180 hectares**
- A typical Brazilian farm: **70 hectares**
- A typical EU farm: **17 hectares**

When you have only 2 acres, even a perfect harvest gives you limited income. You can't afford modern equipment. You can't experiment with new crops. You can't compete on scale.

Why so small? Because Indian farms are **divided across generations**. A grandfather had 20 acres. His three sons inherited about 7 each. Their children inherited 2 each. The next generation may have only 0.5 acres. This continuous division has reduced farm sizes over generations.

**2. Low prices for produce.**

A farmer who grows rice spends 6 months tending it. He may earn ₹15,000-25,000 from one acre of rice — that's about ₹3,000-5,000 per month from his hard work. Hardly enough to live with dignity.

Why are prices low?
- Many farmers grow the same crops — supply is high
- Farmers have **weak bargaining power** — they need to sell quickly (perishables) and can't wait for better prices
- **Middlemen** (commission agents, wholesalers, retailers) take a big share of the final price
- For example: a farmer sells tomatoes at ₹5/kg; by the time they reach you, they cost ₹40/kg. The farmer got ₹5; everyone else split ₹35.

**3. High costs of inputs.**

Modern farming requires expensive inputs:
- Seeds (especially patented hybrid seeds)
- Chemical fertilisers
- Pesticides
- Diesel for tractors and pumps
- Electricity
- Labour (during harvest)

These have risen faster than crop prices. So a farmer earning ₹50,000 from one acre may have spent ₹35,000 on inputs — net income only ₹15,000.

**4. Risk concentration.**

A factory owner can lose money in one product and make up in another. A farmer's **everything is in one crop**. If the monsoon fails, the crop fails. If pests attack, the crop fails. If prices crash at harvest, all that work is lost.

**5. Debt.**

To buy seeds, fertilisers, fuel — farmers borrow. Often from informal moneylenders charging **20-30% interest** (banks lend cheaper but require paperwork and collateral many farmers don't have). When a crop fails, debt piles up. Many farmers end up in debt traps that last generations.

**6. No safety net.**

If a factory worker loses a job, he can claim some unemployment benefit. If a farmer has a crop failure, traditionally there's been no real safety net. Crop insurance schemes exist but are imperfect.

**7. Suicide tragedy.**

Tragically, **debt-related suicide** has been a heartbreaking pattern in some Indian states (Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra). When a farmer can't repay debt, when next year's crop looks doubtful, when there's no way out — some take their lives. This is a national shame.

**Child:** So the farmer feeds India but can't feed himself well?

**Mother:** That is the painful truth. The Indian who grows your rice may not have enough rice. The Indian who produces your milk may have stopped drinking milk to sell more.

**What is being done?**

Several efforts are working:

1. **Minimum Support Prices (MSP)** — government guarantees a floor price for some crops
2. **PM-KISAN** — direct cash transfer to farmers (₹6,000/year)
3. **Crop insurance** — Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana
4. **Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs)** — small farmers banding together, getting better prices
5. **eNAM** — online national market to bypass middlemen
6. **Kisan Credit Card** — formal bank lending at lower rates
7. **PDS (Public Distribution System)** — selling cheap food to poor people, including farming families
8. **Diversification** — growing fruits, flowers, dairy alongside crops

But these are incomplete fixes. The fundamental challenge — small landholdings, low prices, high costs — remains.

**What needs to change?**

Long-term solutions include:
- **Better organisation of farmers** — they need more collective bargaining power
- **Crop diversification** — moving from rice/wheat monoculture
- **Value addition** — turning produce into products (juice, jam, dried foods) that fetch higher prices
- **Direct selling** — apps and platforms that connect farmers to consumers
- **Climate-resilient farming** — drought-resistant seeds, water-saving methods
- **Non-farm jobs** — many farmers may need to leave farming over time; jobs in factories, services, construction must be created
- **Recognition of women farmers** — many women do the work but lack land rights, credit access, or training

This is one of the most important national challenges. When you grow up, however you choose to live, please remember the Indian farmer. Pay fair prices when you can. Avoid waste. Buy from local farmers. Support policies that help them. Your meals come from their hands.

## What Was the Green Revolution and Was It Good?

**Child:** Maa, you mentioned the Green Revolution. What was it? Was it good for India?

**Mother:** Excellent question. The Green Revolution is one of the most important — and most debated — events in modern Indian history.

**The Background — 1950s and early 1960s.**

After independence, India was a food-deficit country. We grew enough only in good years. Famines were a regular fear:
- The Bengal famine of 1943 had killed an estimated 2-3 million people
- The mid-1960s saw two consecutive bad monsoons
- India had to import millions of tonnes of wheat from the USA under PL-480 (a humiliating dependence)
- Some Indians had to eat **less** because there wasn't enough food

The leadership — Lal Bahadur Shastri, then Indira Gandhi — knew India had to become food-self-sufficient or remain dependent on foreign aid.

**Enter Dr. M.S. Swaminathan and Dr. Norman Borlaug.**

**Dr. Norman Borlaug** was an American agricultural scientist who had developed **high-yielding wheat varieties** in Mexico — dwarf wheat plants that produced much more grain. He was invited to India.

**Dr. M.S. Swaminathan** was an Indian agricultural scientist who became the architect of India's agricultural transformation. He led the adaptation of Borlaug's wheat varieties for Indian conditions.

**What the Green Revolution did (1960s-70s):**

1. **High-yielding variety (HYV) seeds** — wheat varieties that produced **3-4 times more grain per acre** than traditional Indian varieties. Later, similar HYV rice was developed (especially IR8 from the Philippines).

2. **Chemical fertilisers** — urea, DAP, potash — applied in large quantities

3. **Pesticides** — to protect the high-yielding crops which were more vulnerable to pests

4. **Irrigation** — massive expansion through tube wells, canals, pumps. The government and farmers invested heavily.

5. **Tractors and machines** — replacing animal power

6. **Government support** — minimum support prices, subsidies for fertilisers, free or cheap electricity for pumps, government procurement of grain

7. **Concentrated in Punjab, Haryana, western UP** — these states became the rice/wheat bowls

**The Good Results:**

- India became **food-self-sufficient** by the early 1970s
- Wheat production: **6 million tonnes (1960) → 26 million tonnes (1980)** → over 100 million tonnes today
- Famines essentially **stopped**
- India today produces **more food than its population needs**
- We even **export** rice and wheat

This is one of the great success stories of post-independence India. Hundreds of millions of Indians eat better because of the Green Revolution. Lives were saved.

**The Bad Results:**

But the Green Revolution had serious side effects:

1. **Groundwater depletion** — millions of tube wells pumping water from underground. In Punjab, the water table has dropped over 30 meters in 50 years. Some areas may run dry within decades.

2. **Soil degradation** — heavy fertiliser use altered soil chemistry. Some Punjab soils are losing organic matter, becoming less productive.

3. **Pesticide poisoning** — pesticides built up in soil, water, food, and human bodies. Punjab's "Cancer Train" — a daily train from Bhatinda to Bikaner full of cancer patients — became a tragic symbol of pesticide exposure.

4. **Monoculture** — farmers focused on wheat and rice only, losing diversity. Pulses, oilseeds, millets, local vegetables declined.

5. **Loss of local seed varieties** — Indian farmers had developed thousands of rice varieties over millennia, suited to local conditions. Many were lost as HYV seeds took over.

6. **Inequality among farmers** — large farmers benefited more (they could afford tubewells, tractors). Small farmers were left behind.

7. **Regional inequality** — Punjab, Haryana, and western UP boomed. Bihar, Odisha, eastern UP, parts of Maharashtra didn't catch up.

8. **Energy dependence** — modern farming requires diesel (for pumps and tractors), electricity, chemical inputs derived from fossil fuels. Indian agriculture became dependent on imported oil.

9. **Methane emissions** — flooded paddy fields emit methane (a powerful greenhouse gas). India is a major emitter from agriculture.

10. **Climate vulnerability** — monocultures are vulnerable to climate change. Heat waves can wipe out wheat in days; droughts can devastate rice.

**Was it worth it?**

Most agricultural experts say YES — the alternative was hunger, dependence, possibly famine. The Green Revolution bought India time.

But many also say: we now need a **Second Green Revolution** — one that:
- Uses **less water** (drip irrigation, drought-resistant varieties)
- Uses **fewer chemicals** (integrated pest management, organic methods)
- Promotes **diverse crops** (millets, pulses, vegetables, fruits)
- Reaches **eastern India** which is still poor in productivity
- Restores **soil health**
- Adapts to **climate change**

This is sometimes called the **"Evergreen Revolution"** — a phrase coined by Dr. Swaminathan himself.

So the Green Revolution was both a triumph and a warning. It saved India from hunger. It also damaged India's soil, water, and rural health. The lesson: technological solutions to one problem (hunger) can create new problems (environmental damage). The wise approach is to **keep learning**, **keep adjusting**, **keep balancing** different goals.

When you grow up, you'll see the next chapter of Indian agriculture. Hopefully it will combine the Green Revolution's productivity with much better environmental and social wisdom.

## Why Are So Many Indian Farmworkers Women, but Few "Farmers"?

**Child:** Maa, you said over 75% of farmworkers are women. But when we hear "Indian farmer," we picture a man. Why?

**Mother:** This is one of the great paradoxes of Indian agriculture — and a deep injustice.

Let me explain what is really happening on Indian farms.

**The Work Women Do:**

In rural India, women are involved in nearly every step of farming:
- **Sowing** seeds (often by hand, very labour-intensive)
- **Transplanting** rice seedlings (most rice in India is transplanted, mostly by women)
- **Weeding** (hours of bending in the sun)
- **Harvesting** crops by hand
- **Threshing** — separating grain from straw
- **Winnowing** — using the wind to separate
- **Drying** grain on courtyards or roads
- **Storing** in granaries (traditional women's domain)
- **Tending livestock** — milking, feeding, cleaning
- **Carrying water** (for crops and animals)
- **Selling** vegetables in local markets (often)
- **Processing** — making pickles, papad, dried foods
- **Preserving traditional seed knowledge** — which seed for which season, how to store

Studies show women contribute **60-80% of farm labour hours** in rural India.

**Why Are They Not Recognised as "Farmers"?**

Several reasons:

**1. Land ownership.**
In most Indian families, **land is owned by men**. Even when a woman does most of the farming, the land legally belongs to her father, husband, or brother. Without land, you are not legally a "farmer" — you are a "worker" or "labourer" or "helping hand."

In Hindu inheritance law, daughters historically did not get equal rights to family land (this was changed in 2005, but practice often lags). In many regions, women still don't inherit family farms in practice.

**2. Government records.**
Land records list men as owners. Farmer schemes (PM-KISAN, crop loans, MSP) flow to landowners. So even if women do the work, the **money goes to men**.

**3. Cultural framing.**
For thousands of years, public roles (going to mandis, taking loans, negotiating prices, dealing with officials) have been associated with men. Women work but stay at home or on the farm. They don't appear in the public face of "farming."

**4. Photographs and media.**
News stories show male farmers. Government posters show male farmers. School textbooks (until recently) showed male farmers. So the **image** of a farmer is male, even when the reality is mostly female.

**5. Decision-making.**
Even on the farm, decisions about what to grow, when to sell, what price to accept are often made by male family members. Women do the work but don't decide.

**Why This Matters:**

When women's contribution is invisible:
- **Training programmes** target men. Women miss out on modern agricultural knowledge.
- **Loans and credit** go to men. Women can't easily borrow to improve their farms or start new ventures.
- **Extension services** (government advice to farmers) reach men. Women don't get the advice that would help.
- **Policy attention** focuses on male-coded issues (price support, mandi access) and neglects female-coded issues (water carrying, kitchen gardens, drudgery reduction).
- **Recognition** never comes. A woman who has farmed for 40 years can be invisible.

**What Is Changing:**

Slowly, the picture is shifting:

1. **Land ownership reforms** — many states now help register joint ownership of family farms.

2. **Women's self-help groups (SHGs)** — millions of rural women have organised into SHGs that pool savings, take loans together, and start agri-businesses.

3. **Mahila Kisan Sashaktikaran Pariyojana** — government scheme specifically for women farmers.

4. **FPOs with women** — Farmer Producer Organisations are now actively recruiting women members.

5. **Recognition campaigns** — media, government, and NGOs are slowly correcting the male-only image of farming.

6. **Women's leadership** — some rural women now serve as panchayat presidents, agriculture officers, and FPO directors.

**Stories of women farmers:**

- **Daulatdevi** of Tilonia (Rajasthan), at 70 years old, runs a solar engineering training centre and has trained thousands of rural women, including in farming techniques.
- **Subhasini Mistry** of West Bengal, born poor and widowed, worked as a vegetable seller for decades and saved every rupee to build a hospital for the poor in her village — funded by her vegetable-farming earnings.
- **Lakshmi Kutty** of Kerala, a tribal woman, holds knowledge of hundreds of medicinal plants — knowledge that universities are now formally recognising.

These are extraordinary women. But there are millions of ordinary women farmers whose stories will never be in newspapers — yet whose work feeds India.

**Child:** So farming is mostly women's work but credited to men?

**Mother:** In large part, yes. This is a quiet injustice. The next generation of Indians — especially you — should help change this. When you write about farmers, write about women too. When you imagine a farmer, see a woman too. When policy is made, ask: does this help women farmers? When you buy vegetables, remember: women probably picked them, sorted them, brought them to market.

Real progress in Indian agriculture requires putting **women at the centre, not the margins**. The country has finally started moving in this direction. Your generation can push it further.

## Should Indians Eat More Millets?

**Child:** Maa, I heard the government is promoting millets. Should we eat more of them?

**Mother:** Yes — and this is one of the more interesting modern stories in Indian food.

**What are millets?**

Millets are a group of **small-grain cereals** that have been grown in India for thousands of years. They include:
- **Ragi** (finger millet) — popular in Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra
- **Bajra** (pearl millet) — Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Gujarat
- **Jowar** (sorghum) — Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra
- **Kodo, Kutki, Sama, Kangani** — smaller millets across India

**Why were millets important?**

Before the Green Revolution, millets were **everyday food** for hundreds of millions of Indians, especially in semi-arid regions. They were:
- **Drought-resistant** — grown with very little water
- **Nutrient-rich** — more fibre, more minerals, more protein than rice
- **Hardy** — withstood pests, poor soil
- **Cheap** — accessible to even very poor families
- **Low glycemic index** — better for blood sugar control

**Why did millets decline?**

After the Green Revolution, India focused on rice and wheat. Several factors made millets retreat:

1. **PDS (Public Distribution System)** — government distributed cheap rice and wheat through ration shops. Poor families switched from millets to rice/wheat because the latter was nearly free.

2. **Status** — rice and wheat became seen as "modern" food. Millets were stigmatised as "poor person's food."

3. **Easier processing** — rice and wheat are easier to cook quickly. Millets need overnight soaking, careful cooking. As urbanisation rose, convenience won.

4. **Government policy** — MSPs supported rice and wheat. Millets had no MSP for decades.

5. **Marketing** — TV ads promoted modern processed foods (Maggi, bread, etc.). No one advertised ragi mudde.

Result: millet consumption fell **dramatically**. From being 40% of cereals consumed in 1960, millets dropped to under 10% by 2000.

**Why are millets coming back?**

Several reasons make millets attractive again:

**1. Health awareness.**
Diabetes is exploding in India — about 100 million Indians have it. Rice and wheat (especially refined) are high glycemic — they spike blood sugar. Millets are low glycemic. Doctors now advise diabetics to eat millets.

Other health benefits:
- High fibre (helps digestion, weight control)
- High mineral content (iron, calcium, magnesium)
- Higher protein than rice
- Antioxidants
- Gluten-free (good for those with gluten sensitivity)

**2. Climate change.**
As India faces more droughts, hotter summers, less predictable monsoons — **drought-resistant crops** become vital. Millets need a fraction of the water that rice needs. They can grow in poor soils. They survive heat waves.

A 2-week dry spell that would kill rice may not affect millets. As climate becomes more unpredictable, this matters more.

**3. Soil and water.**
Millets are gentle on soil — they don't deplete it like rice/wheat monocultures. They use little water. They can rebuild degraded land.

**4. Government push.**
- In 2018, the Indian government declared **2018 the National Year of Millets**
- India pushed the **UN to declare 2023 the International Year of Millets**
- Millets were added to **PDS** in some states
- **MSP** introduced for some millets
- **Awareness campaigns** with celebrities

**5. Health-conscious urban Indians.**
Wealthy urban Indians have rediscovered millets. Millet products (ragi cookies, jowar rotis, bajra dosas) are now in supermarkets. Restaurants serve millet thalis. Influencers post millet recipes.

**The current state:**

Millet production is rising again — about 16 million tonnes in 2022, projected to grow significantly. But it's still well below the historical peak.

**Should we eat millets?**

Yes, for several reasons:

1. **Health** — replacing some rice/wheat with millets improves nutrition, especially for blood sugar control.

2. **Environment** — millets need less water and chemicals. Eating them supports sustainable farming.

3. **Cultural heritage** — millets are an Indian food. They were the food of our ancestors. Eating them connects us to that heritage.

4. **Supporting small farmers** — millets are often grown by poor farmers on rain-fed land. Buying millets supports them.

5. **Variety** — eating just rice and wheat is monotonous. Adding millets opens new flavours, textures.

**How to start:**

- Start with **ragi mudde** or **ragi roti** in Karnataka/Tamil style
- Try **bajra roti** with sarson saag (Punjab winter favourite)
- Try **jowar bhakri** in Maharashtra style
- Replace one rice meal a week with millet (ragi balls, jowar khichdi, millet upma)
- Try millet flour mixed with wheat flour for chapatis (50/50 is a good start)
- Try millet-based snacks (ragi cookies, jowar puffs)
- Substitute oats for ragi/foxtail millet porridge

**The bigger lesson:**

The story of millets shows something important. **Modernisation and globalisation can lose us things that are actually good.** Our ancestors had wisdom — they knew which foods worked for our climate, soil, and health. When we abandoned that for "modern" choices, we lost some valuable things.

The wise approach: don't reject everything traditional, don't accept everything modern. **Combine the best of both**. Eat traditional millets AND modern medicine. Use traditional water-harvesting AND modern drip irrigation. Honour ancient farming texts AND modern agricultural science.

This is one of India's strengths — our long civilisational memory, combined with modern openness. The millet revival is a small example. Bigger ones may follow as India rediscovers its ancient wisdom for modern problems.
