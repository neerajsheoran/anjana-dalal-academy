## Why Is It Difficult to Be a Big Country in a Small Neighbourhood?

**Child:** Maa, why does India have trouble with so many of its neighbours? Are we doing something wrong?

**Mother:** This is a deep question. It's not really about doing something wrong — it's about the **structural challenge** of being the giant in your neighbourhood.

Let me explain.

**India is much bigger than all its neighbours combined.**

Look at the numbers:
- India: 1.4 billion people, $3.5 trillion GDP
- Pakistan: 240 million people, $360 billion GDP
- Bangladesh: 170 million, $460 billion GDP
- Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, Maldives: combined under 100 million

If you add up ALL of India's neighbours (excluding China), India is still **more populous than all of them combined**.

This creates an unusual situation. Imagine being the biggest child in a class — you didn't choose it, but your size affects how others treat you and how you treat others.

**The challenge for India:**
- Small neighbours fear domination
- They look for outside friends to balance India
- China happily steps in as that balance
- Anything India does (build a dam, raise tariffs, change policy) affects neighbours
- Even helpful actions can feel overbearing

**The challenge for neighbours:**
- They can't compete with India economically
- They need access to Indian markets
- They depend on Indian ports, electricity, transit
- But they don't want to be dependent
- So they often act prickly even when they want help

**Compare with the USA.**

The US is the biggest economy in its neighbourhood (Mexico, Canada). But the US has only 2 land neighbours. India has 7.

**Compare with China.**

China is huge but mostly has smaller neighbours (Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia, North Korea, Mongolia). China is generally more assertive in dealing with them.

India has taken a softer approach — what was called the **Gujral Doctrine** (1996-97): give more to neighbours than you expect in return; don't insist on reciprocity. This was generous. But it didn't always work — some neighbours took the help and still leaned away from India.

**What India is trying now:**

India has shifted to **"Neighbourhood First"** policy — special priority for South Asia, but with more clear-eyed pragmatism. The idea: be generous, but ensure the relationship is **fair and mutually beneficial**.

**Some success stories:**

- **Bhutan** — extremely close partnership, hydropower, defence, training
- **Bangladesh** — large trade, connectivity, energy projects, settled border issues
- **Sri Lanka** — major economic help during their 2022 crisis
- **Nepal** — open border maintained, major aid during earthquakes

**Some struggles:**

- **Pakistan** — bilateral relations very limited
- **China** — strategic rivalry across the Himalayas
- **Maldives** — political shifts sometimes create tensions
- **Myanmar** — instability after the 2021 coup

**The lesson:**

Being the biggest in your neighbourhood is a permanent challenge. India can't change its size. What it can do:
1. **Be patient** — relationships take generations
2. **Be generous** — small gestures matter
3. **Be clear** — about red lines and core interests
4. **Be inclusive** — let neighbours benefit too
5. **Be culturally sensitive** — recognise pride and dignity of smaller nations
6. **Compete fairly with China** — without making neighbours feel forced to choose

**Child:** So India can't really avoid these tensions?

**Mother:** Not fully. But India can manage them better. The goal isn't perfect neighbours — it's **stable relationships that help everyone**. We are not there yet, but moving in the right direction.

When you grow up, you might visit Nepal for trekking, Bhutan for spiritual retreats, Bangladesh for relatives, Sri Lanka for beaches. Indians and our neighbours have far more in common than what divides us. Our job is to keep working on the relationships, generation by generation.

## Why Was Partition So Painful?

**Child:** Maa, you said Partition was one of the bloodiest events of the 20th century. What happened?

**Mother:** This is a difficult chapter of our history. But you should know it, because Partition still shapes India and Pakistan today, 80 years later.

**Background — Why Partition Happened.**

By the 1940s, the independence movement had built up support across India. But there was a disagreement about what kind of country India should be:

- The **Indian National Congress** (Gandhi, Nehru, Patel) wanted a **united, secular India** where Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, Christians, and others lived as equal citizens.

- The **Muslim League** (Muhammad Ali Jinnah) increasingly argued that **Muslims needed a separate country** — what they called "Pakistan" (literally "land of the pure"). They feared being a permanent minority in a Hindu-majority India.

The British, who had ruled India for nearly 200 years, were leaving. They couldn't (and didn't want to) hold both sides together. So they decided: India will be partitioned. Two countries — India and Pakistan — would emerge on August 14-15, 1947.

**The line was drawn.**

A British lawyer named **Cyril Radcliffe** — who had never visited India — was given **5 weeks** to draw the border between India and Pakistan. He divided Punjab, Bengal, and other regions based on which districts had majority Hindu or Muslim populations. The border was announced **after** independence was declared — meaning people only learned where they "belonged" after it was too late.

**The result was chaos.**

About 15 million people had to choose: stay in their home and live in a country with the "wrong" majority, or flee to the country of their religion. Most chose to move.

- Muslims from Punjab, UP, Bihar fled to Pakistan
- Hindus and Sikhs from West Punjab, Sindh fled to India
- Hindus from East Bengal fled to West Bengal
- Muslims from West Bengal fled to East Bengal

**The bloodshed:**

The movements were not peaceful. Communal violence broke out across the subcontinent:
- **Trains** carrying refugees were attacked. Whole trains arrived with everyone dead.
- **Villages** were torched. Women were kidnapped, raped, killed.
- **Families were separated** — children lost forever from their parents, brothers from sisters.
- **Property was looted**. Centuries-old homes abandoned.

**The toll:**
- About **1-2 million people killed**
- About **10-20 million displaced** — the largest migration in human history
- **Tens of thousands of women** abducted, raped, never recovered
- **Generations of trauma** that still affects families today

**Stories survive.**

There are countless Partition stories. Some examples:

- **Saadat Hasan Manto** — a Muslim writer whose family fled from India to Pakistan. His stories ("Toba Tek Singh," "Khol Do," "Thanda Gosht") are among the most powerful Partition literature ever written.

- **The Train to Pakistan** — Khushwant Singh's novel about a Punjabi village where Hindus, Sikhs, and Muslims had lived peacefully for generations, then turned on each other.

- **The Begums of Bhopal** — a family that had ruled a princely state for generations, divided across both countries.

- **Mahatma Gandhi** — the freedom movement leader who had fought non-violently for independence, was deeply broken by Partition violence. He fasted to stop the killings. He was assassinated in January 1948 — a victim of the hatreds Partition unleashed.

**Why it still hurts.**

Eighty years later, Partition's wounds remain:

1. **Families separated** — many Indians have relatives in Pakistan they have never met. Sometimes they meet at international events or through online connections. But many never reunite.

2. **Property lost** — entire generations lost the homes, farms, businesses their families had built over centuries.

3. **Cultural loss** — many Punjabi, Sindhi, Bengali traditions were broken. The Sindhi language and culture especially suffered.

4. **Mental scars** — survivors carried the trauma. They passed it to their children. The story of Partition is still raw in many Indian families.

5. **Geopolitical consequences** — India and Pakistan have fought 4 wars since. Both spend huge sums on militaries. Kashmir, Sir Creek, water sharing — all unresolved disputes trace back to Partition.

6. **Communal tensions** — Hindu-Muslim relations within India are still affected by Partition's memory.

**Could it have been avoided?**

Historians still debate this. Some say Partition was inevitable given how Hindu-Muslim politics had developed by the 1940s. Others say better leadership could have prevented it. Mahatma Gandhi tried desperately to keep India united but was overruled.

What we know:
- The British rushed the process
- The political leaders failed to prevent communal violence
- The Radcliffe line was drawn carelessly
- Local communities turned on each other in ways no one had predicted
- All sides — Hindu, Muslim, Sikh, British — share blame for what happened

**Why you should know this.**

1. **It explains modern South Asia** — why India and Pakistan struggle, why Kashmir is contested, why communal tensions persist.

2. **It teaches the cost of religious hatred** — when communities turn on each other, ordinary people pay the price.

3. **It honours the victims** — millions died in 1947-48. They deserve to be remembered.

4. **It values our Constitution** — India's secular, equal-rights Constitution was a direct response to Partition. The founders said: never again will religion decide who is a "real Indian."

5. **It motivates us to do better** — your generation has a chance to build a more peaceful South Asia. That starts with understanding what went wrong.

**Child:** Why don't we talk about Partition more?

**Mother:** Because it is painful. Because the wounds are still fresh in many families. Because India and Pakistan still have tense relations. Because there is fear that revisiting Partition might re-open communal divisions.

But silence is not the answer. Partition needs to be remembered — honestly, completely. Museums like the **Partition Museum in Amritsar** are now documenting personal stories. Authors, filmmakers, and historians are bringing the period back to public memory.

Knowing about Partition makes us more careful. It makes us value the unity, secularism, and peace we have now. It makes us work harder to ensure such tragedies never recur — anywhere in South Asia, anywhere in the world.

## What Is Special About Bhutan?

**Child:** Maa, you mentioned Bhutan measures Gross National Happiness. What is that, and is it real?

**Mother:** Bhutan is a fascinating small country, and yes, **Gross National Happiness (GNH)** is real and serious there.

**What is GNH?**

Most countries measure their success by **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** — the total value of goods and services produced. Bigger GDP = "successful" country.

But the **King of Bhutan, Jigme Singye Wangchuck**, famously said in the 1970s:
> "Gross National Happiness is more important than Gross National Product."

He argued that simply growing the economy doesn't make people happy. A country can have rising GDP but falling environment, broken families, mental health crises, lost culture.

So Bhutan developed an alternative measure: **Gross National Happiness**. It tracks:

1. **Living standards** (income, jobs, housing)
2. **Health** (life expectancy, healthcare access)
3. **Education** (literacy, quality)
4. **Environment** (forest cover, biodiversity, climate)
5. **Cultural diversity** (preserving Bhutanese culture, language, arts)
6. **Good governance** (clean politics, citizen voice)
7. **Time use** (work-life balance, family time)
8. **Psychological wellbeing** (happiness, stress)
9. **Community vitality** (social bonds, trust)

The Bhutanese government **actually surveys citizens** about these dimensions, calculates a GNH score, and uses it to guide policy. If a new dam would boost GDP but harm the environment and culture, it might be rejected for hurting GNH.

**Is it just a slogan?**

No — Bhutan has actually shaped its country around GNH:

1. **Environment.**
   - Bhutan's constitution requires that **at least 60% of the country remain forested**.
   - Today, **70%+ of Bhutan is forest**.
   - Bhutan is **carbon-negative** — it absorbs more CO2 than it emits. One of the very few countries in the world to achieve this.

2. **Tourism.**
   - Bhutan limits the number of tourists allowed
   - Tourists must pay a "Sustainable Development Fee" (around $100/day)
   - This prevents over-tourism, protects culture and environment
   - Tourism funds the country without overwhelming it

3. **Culture preserved.**
   - Bhutanese architecture, dress, language are preserved
   - Traditional medicine taught alongside modern
   - Festivals, monasteries, prayer flags everywhere

4. **Modest growth.**
   - Bhutan's economy is growing — but slowly, deliberately
   - The country has avoided the pollution and inequality of fast-growing neighbours

5. **High wellbeing reported.**
   - Bhutanese citizens generally report higher happiness and satisfaction than people in much richer countries
   - Health outcomes are good for the country's income level

**Is everything perfect?**

No — Bhutan has problems too:
- **Limited job opportunities** (young people sometimes move abroad)
- **Slow modernisation** (some traditions resist change)
- **Refugee issues** (in the 1990s, many ethnic Nepalis were expelled — a stain on Bhutan's record)
- **Small economy** (limited choices for diverse careers)
- **Climate change vulnerability** (glaciers melting in the Himalayas)

So Bhutan is not a utopia. But it has done something most countries fail to do: **prioritise wellbeing over wealth**.

**Why this matters globally.**

The world is starting to take GNH seriously. The **UN passed a resolution** in 2011 supporting the idea of measuring happiness alongside GDP. Countries like France, Britain, and New Zealand now produce "wellbeing reports" alongside economic statistics.

Bhutan, a tiny country, has influenced global thinking about what "development" really means.

**What can India learn?**

India faces a question Bhutan has already grappled with: do we want to be rich-but-polluted, or sustainably-prosperous?

GDP growth is essential — millions still need to come out of poverty. But India's current path includes:
- Air pollution (Delhi often has the world's worst air quality)
- Water depletion (over half of India's groundwater stressed)
- Mental health crisis (suicide rates among the world's highest in some groups)
- Soil degradation
- Cultural homogenisation (regional cultures shrinking)
- Family stress (urban migration breaking traditional support systems)

Could India learn from Bhutan?
- Measure happiness and wellbeing, not just GDP
- Protect forests as core constitutional duty
- Prioritise traditional culture alongside modernisation
- Tax tourism to prevent over-tourism in fragile places
- Limit pollution before it kills

Some of this is happening — India has emission targets, forest preservation laws, mental health programs. But not at Bhutan's level of intentionality.

**Bhutan's lesson:**

A country doesn't have to be rich to be wise. A country doesn't have to follow the West's path. A country can define its own goals.

For India, Bhutan is a friendly Himalayan neighbour, but also a kind of mirror. It asks us: **what do we ultimately want our country to be?**

A bigger economy? Yes.
A cleaner environment? Yes.
A happier people? Most importantly, yes.

The wisest answer combines all three. That's what Bhutan tries to do — and what India, with much more complexity, is also trying to figure out.

## How Does India Connect to Southeast Asia?

**Child:** Maa, you said India's "wider neighbourhood" includes Southeast Asian countries. How are we connected?

**Mother:** This is a beautiful and underappreciated story. India and Southeast Asia have been deeply connected for over **2,000 years**.

**Ancient connections.**

In the first centuries CE, Indian merchants, monks, and travellers sailed eastward to what is now Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, Cambodia, Vietnam. They brought:
- **Hinduism** — Hindu kingdoms ruled in Java, Sumatra, Bali for centuries
- **Buddhism** — spread across mainland Southeast Asia
- **Sanskrit** — the language of learning and religion
- **Indian art and architecture** — temple styles inspired local versions
- **Indian epics** — the Ramayana and Mahabharata became local stories
- **Indian script** — many Southeast Asian scripts (Thai, Khmer, Lao, Burmese) evolved from Indian scripts

**The grand monuments.**

This Indian cultural influence produced some of the **greatest monuments in the world**:

- **Angkor Wat (Cambodia)** — the largest religious monument on Earth. Built in the 12th century by the Khmer Empire. Originally a Hindu temple dedicated to Vishnu, later Buddhist. Sculptures of Ramayana scenes cover its walls.

- **Borobudur (Indonesia, Java)** — a massive 9th-century Buddhist stupa. One of the world's greatest Buddhist monuments.

- **Prambanan (Indonesia, Java)** — a 9th-century Hindu temple complex dedicated to Trimurti (Brahma, Vishnu, Shiva).

- **Bali (Indonesia)** — the only Hindu-majority island in Indonesia today. Their Hinduism is unique, blending Hindu beliefs with local traditions.

- **Ayutthaya (Thailand)** — old Thai capital. The Thai name comes from "Ayodhya" — Rama's city.

- **Sri Lanka, Burma, Cambodia, Laos, Thailand** — all have Buddhist traditions traced back to India.

**The Ramayana lives in Southeast Asia.**

The Ramayana has versions in many Southeast Asian cultures:
- **Thai Ramakien** — the national epic of Thailand
- **Lao Phra Lak Phra Lam** — the Lao version
- **Cambodian Reamker** — the Khmer version
- **Indonesian Kakawin Ramayana** — the Javanese version
- **Malay Hikayat Seri Rama** — the Malay version

In all these countries, Ramayana stories are performed in shadow puppet theatre, classical dance, paintings. King Rama is honoured. Ravana plays the villain. The story we tell in India is told there too — with local twists.

**Modern India in Southeast Asia.**

In modern times, India launched **"Look East"** policy in 1991, later upgraded to **"Act East"** policy in 2014. Goals:
- Strengthen ties with ASEAN (Association of Southeast Asian Nations — 10 countries)
- Boost trade
- Increase tourism
- Cooperate on defence and security
- Connect with Southeast Asia through infrastructure

**ASEAN countries:**
1. Indonesia
2. Malaysia
3. Thailand
4. Philippines
5. Singapore
6. Vietnam
7. Myanmar
8. Cambodia
9. Laos
10. Brunei

India is an **ASEAN Dialogue Partner** and has signed a free trade agreement with the region. Trade is rising.

**Cultural connections continue.**

- Indian films are popular in many Southeast Asian countries
- Yoga has spread widely
- Bollywood music plays
- Indian restaurants are everywhere
- Many Southeast Asians visit India for religious tourism (Buddhists going to Bodh Gaya, Hindus to Tirupati, etc.)
- Tens of millions of people of Indian origin live in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand

**Why this matters.**

Southeast Asia is one of the world's fastest-growing regions — over 660 million people, growing economies. For India:
- **Trade and investment opportunities**
- **Strategic balance** in the Indo-Pacific
- **Cultural cousin region** — shared heritage means relations have a warm base
- **Diaspora** — many Indians of Tamil, Punjabi, Gujarati origin live in Southeast Asia for generations
- **Alternative to China** — many Southeast Asian countries also want a counterweight to Chinese dominance

**Indonesia is special.**

Indonesia is the world's largest Muslim-majority country (about 280 million Muslims). Yet:
- Its national language **Bahasa Indonesia** has thousands of Sanskrit-derived words
- Many Indonesian names are Sanskrit (Indra, Wijaya, Sukarno, Megawati, Sutomo)
- Its currency is the **rupiah** (related to Sanskrit rupya)
- Its airline is **Garuda Indonesia** (Garuda from Hindu mythology)
- Its national philosophy **Pancasila** has Sanskrit name
- Its army calls itself **Tentara Nasional Indonesia** with motto "**Tri Dharma Eka Karma**"

So Indonesia, while Islamic, retains deep Hindu-Buddhist cultural memory.

**A Note on Mauritius.**

Mauritius — an island in the Indian Ocean — is essentially an Indian cultural outpost. About 70% of its population is of Indian origin. Hindi is widely spoken. Indian festivals are national holidays. Indian movies dominate. India has very close ties with Mauritius.

**The big picture.**

When historians look back at the world's great cultural zones, they speak of:
- **The Mediterranean world** (Greek-Roman-European)
- **The Sinosphere** (Chinese-influenced East Asia)
- **The Islamic world** (Arabia outward)
- **The Indosphere** — India and the cultures it shaped

The Indosphere stretches from Afghanistan through India to Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia, Laos, Indonesia, Malaysia. About 2 billion people live in the broader Indosphere — over a quarter of humanity.

This is one of the world's great cultural achievements. It happened without conquest (unlike the Roman or Mongol empires) — through **trade, travel, ideas, and inspiration**. Indian merchants and monks sailed east; local kings and people willingly adopted what they found appealing.

When you grow up, you might visit Angkor Wat, see the Ramakien performed in Bangkok, walk through Borobudur, dance the Kecak in Bali. In all these places, you'll meet your own civilisation in different forms.

**Child:** So India has neighbours far beyond just the seven on its borders?

**Mother:** Yes — culturally, India's "neighbourhood" extends across half the world. The challenge is to **maintain and develop these old ties** in modern, mutually respectful ways. Trade, technology, tourism, education, climate cooperation — these are the modern languages of the old connections.

India's neighbourhood is a treasure. Most countries don't have this kind of civilisational network spanning oceans. Learning to use it wisely will be one of India's great tasks in the 21st century.
