## A Thousand Years Without Cities

In Class 6 we learnt about India's **first civilisation** — the **Indus-Sarasvati (Harappan) civilisation** — which flourished from about 2600 BCE to 1900 BCE. It had great cities like Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, and Dholavira.

Around **1900 BCE**, this civilisation declined. Cities were abandoned. People returned to **village life**. For nearly **a thousand years**, the Indian Subcontinent had **no major cities** — though small towns existed here and there.

But around the **1st millennium BCE** (1000 BCE onwards), something remarkable began. **Cities started rising again** — not in the Indus region this time, but in the **Ganga plains** and surrounding areas.

This new period is called the **Second Urbanisation of India**. And it has been going strong ever since — over 2,500 years of continuous Indian urban civilisation!

## How Do We Know About This Period?

Two main sources:

1. **Archaeology** — excavated remains of ancient cities, walls, pottery, coins, tools
2. **Ancient literature** — late Vedic texts, Buddhist scriptures (Jataka tales, Sutta-nipata), and Jain writings — all describe these urban centres in detail

When archaeology and literature agree, we have strong evidence. When they disagree, scholars investigate further. This is how history is built.

## Janapadas — Early Indian States

As clans of people settled down in particular regions and developed common languages and customs, each clan came to be associated with its own territory — called a **janapada**.

The word **janapada** in Sanskrit means:
- **jana** = people
- **pada** = foot, footprint

So janapada literally means **"the land where the people have set foot"** — the land where a community settled.

Each janapada was led by a **raja** (king or chief). But — and this is important — the raja did **not** rule arbitrarily. He had to consult assemblies called:

- **Sabha** — a council, usually of senior or respected members
- **Samiti** — a larger gathering of all the people

These words **first appear in the Vedas**, India's most ancient texts. So the idea of **collective decision-making** is one of the oldest features of Indian governance.

## Mahajanapadas — Bigger States

By the **8th-7th centuries BCE**, some of the smaller janapadas merged together to form larger states called **mahajanapadas** ("great janapadas").

Ancient texts list about **16 mahajanapadas**. The most famous ones include:

| Mahajanapada | Capital | Modern location |
|---|---|---|
| **Magadha** | Rajagriha (then Pataliputra) | Bihar |
| **Kosala** | Shravasti | Eastern UP |

| **Avanti** | Ujjayini (Ujjain) | Madhya Pradesh |
| **Kuru** | Indraprastha | Near Delhi |
| **Panchala** | Ahichchhatra | UP |
| **Vajji** | Vaishali | Bihar |
| **Anga** | Champa | Bhagalpur, Bihar |
| **Malla** | Kushinara | Eastern UP |
| **Gandhara** | Takshashila (Taxila) | Today's Pakistan |

| **Vrijji/Licchavi** | Vaishali | Bihar |

Notice: **most mahajanapadas were in the Ganga plains**. This was no accident. The Ganga plains had:
- **Fertile soil** for abundant crops
- **River transport** for trade and movement
- **Iron ore** in nearby hills — used for tools and weapons
- **Growing population** — both cause and effect of urbanisation

## What Did These Cities Look Like?

The mahajanapadas' capitals were not just larger villages. They were **planned urban centres** with:

- **Massive walls and ramparts** — for defence
- **Moats** — deep, water-filled ditches outside the walls, an extra layer of protection
- **Narrow gateways** — to control who entered and left
- **Markets** — for trade in cloth, metals, grain, animals
- **Royal palaces and administrative buildings**
- **Streets and water systems**
- **Specialised workshops** — for metals, pottery, weaving, jewellery
- **Religious centres** — temples, stupas (later, with Buddhism)

### Living Cities Today

Remarkably, **many of these ancient capitals are still living cities today**:
- **Rajagriha** → modern **Rajgir** in Bihar
- **Pataliputra** → modern **Patna**
- **Kaushambi** → archaeological site near Allahabad
- **Mathura** → modern **Mathura**, still important
- **Ujjayini** → modern **Ujjain**, still sacred
- **Takshashila (Taxila)** → in modern Pakistan, a UNESCO site
- **Vaishali** → in Bihar, important Buddhist site

When you walk through Patna or Ujjain or Mathura, you're walking through cities **older than 2,500 years**. Few cities in the world have such a long continuous history.

## Why Did Cities Return in This Period?

Several factors came together:

### 1. Agricultural Surplus

The **Ganga plains** are some of the most fertile land on Earth. Once Iron Age tools (axes, ploughs) made clearing forests easier, more land could be cultivated. Surplus food meant cities could be fed.

### 2. Iron Technology

Around 1000 BCE, **iron tools** became common in India. Iron axes cleared forests faster than bronze. Iron ploughs broke heavier soils. Iron weapons gave kingdoms military power.

Some scholars consider this the most important technological revolution in early India.

### 3. Trade Networks

Rivers connected cities. Roads connected regions. Long-distance trade brought goods, ideas, and people together. Some merchants became extremely wealthy and powerful.

### 4. Coinage

For the first time in India, **coins** were minted in this period — initially as **punch-marked silver pieces**. Coins made trade easier than barter. They let wealth be stored and accumulated.

### 5. Specialisation

In a city, you don't need to grow your own food, weave your own cloth, or make your own tools. You can specialise — become only a potter, only a weaver, only a smith — and trade with others. Specialisation creates **higher-quality work** and **more variety** in society.

## Early Democratic Traditions — The Gana-sanghas

Most mahajanapadas were ruled by **monarchies** (kings). But some had a different system — the **gana-sangha** (also called gana-rajya) — which was an **early form of republic or oligarchy**.

In a gana-sangha:
- A **council of senior members** (often heads of leading families) ruled together
- Decisions were made by **discussion and majority vote** in the council
- No single king had absolute power
- The most famous gana-sangha was the **Vajji confederacy** in Bihar (around Vaishali), of which the **Licchavi** clan was prominent

This was an **early experiment in collective rule** — long before similar systems developed in Greece or Rome.

### What the Buddha Saw

The **Buddha** lived during this period (around 6th-5th century BCE). He spent time in Vaishali among the Licchavis. He praised them for:
- Meeting regularly in their assemblies
- Discussing matters peacefully
- Respecting elders
- Honouring agreements
- Caring for the dharma

He saw their gana-sangha as a model worth following. When he organised his Buddhist sangha (community of monks), he gave it a similar council-based structure.

## What Kautilya Said About Cities

The political thinker **Kautilya** (Chanakya), writing about **300 BCE** in the **Arthashastra**, gave advice on how to build a kingdom. He wrote:

> "The kingdom shall be protected by fortifying the capital and the towns at the frontiers. The land should... be capable of sustaining the population. It should be beautiful, with cultivable land, mines, timber forests, elephant forests, and good pastures. It should not depend only on rain for water. It should have good roads and waterways..."

This shows that by the time of the Mauryan Empire, Indians had developed **sophisticated thinking** about urban planning, defence, infrastructure, and economy.

## Magadha — The Greatest Mahajanapada

Of all the mahajanapadas, **Magadha** in modern Bihar became the most powerful. Its capital was first **Rajagriha** (modern Rajgir), then later **Pataliputra** (modern Patna).

Magadha's strengths:
- **Fertile land** along the Ganga
- **Iron deposits** in nearby hills
- **Trade routes** — could reach northeast, eastern, and central India
- **Ambitious rulers** — kings like Bimbisara and Ajatashatru extended Magadha's reach

Magadha eventually conquered or absorbed many other mahajanapadas. By around 300 BCE, **Chandragupta Maurya** founded the **Mauryan Empire** from Magadha — one of the largest empires of the ancient world.

We will study the Mauryans in the next chapter.

## Before We Close

- After the **First Urbanisation** (Indus-Sarasvati, 2600-1900 BCE) ended, India had **no major cities for about a thousand years**
- The **Second Urbanisation** began in the **1st millennium BCE** in the Ganga plains
- People organised into **janapadas** — territories led by a raja with councils called **sabha** and **samiti**
- By 7th century BCE, **16 mahajanapadas** had emerged — Magadha, Kosala, Vatsa, Avanti, and others
- Cities had walls, moats, markets, workshops — sophisticated urban centres
- **Iron technology, agricultural surplus, trade, and coinage** drove this urban revival
- Some states like **Vajji** were **gana-sanghas** — early republics
- The **Buddha** praised the gana-sangha system
- **Magadha** became the most powerful, eventually leading to the Mauryan Empire
- Many of these ancient cities — **Patna, Mathura, Ujjain, Rajgir** — are still alive today
