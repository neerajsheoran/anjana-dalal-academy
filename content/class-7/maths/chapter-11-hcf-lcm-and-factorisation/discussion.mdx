## The Gift-Packing Puzzle

**Child:** Maa, I have a problem. My friend Meera has 36 chocolates and 48 toffees and she wants to make identical gift packs for her birthday party. How does she figure out how many packs to make?

**Mother:** Think about it this way, beta. Every pack must have the same number of chocolates and the same number of toffees. So the total number of packs must divide both 36 and 48 evenly. We need the common factors of these two numbers.

**Child:** Let me try. Factors of 36 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36. Factors of 48 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 48. The common ones are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 12.

**Mother:** And since Meera wants as many packs as possible, she picks the largest common factor, which is 12. Each pack gets 3 chocolates and 4 toffees. This largest shared factor is called the HCF -- the Highest Common Factor.

**Child:** So the HCF is like finding the biggest number that fits perfectly into both quantities?

**Mother:** Exactly. It shows up everywhere -- dividing supplies equally, cutting cloth into equal strips, planting rows of flowers with the same count per row.

## Breaking Numbers into Primes

**Child:** Maa, listing all factors takes so long for big numbers. Is there a shortcut?

**Mother:** Yes -- use prime factorisation. Every number can be written as a product of prime numbers. Take 540 for instance. Keep dividing by the smallest prime that works: 540 divided by 2 gives 270, divided by 2 gives 135, divided by 3 gives 45, divided by 3 gives 15, divided by 3 gives 5, divided by 5 gives 1. So 540 = 2 x 2 x 3 x 3 x 3 x 5.

**Child:** And to find the HCF, I compare the prime factorisations?

**Mother:** Right. For 54 = 2 x 3 x 3 x 3 and 90 = 2 x 3 x 3 x 5, the common primes are 2 (appearing once in both) and 3 (appearing twice in 90 but three times in 54 -- so we take the minimum, which is 2). HCF = 2 x 3 x 3 = 18.

**Child:** What if two numbers have no prime factors in common?

**Mother:** Then their HCF is 1, and we call them co-prime. Like 64 = 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 and 125 = 5 x 5 x 5. Not a single shared prime. Here is a nice pattern too -- any two consecutive numbers, like 20 and 21, are always co-prime.

**Child:** That is clever! They can never share a factor because they are right next to each other on the number line.

## When Bells Ring Together

**Child:** Maa, I learned about HCF, but what is LCM? My teacher mentioned it today.

**Mother:** LCM stands for Lowest Common Multiple. Imagine a temple where one bell rings every 9 minutes and another every 12 minutes. They both ring at 6 AM. When do they ring together next? You need a number that is a multiple of both 9 and 12.

**Child:** Multiples of 9: 9, 18, 27, 36... Multiples of 12: 12, 24, 36... The first one they share is 36!

**Mother:** So the LCM of 9 and 12 is 36. The bells ring together again after 36 minutes. For prime factorisation, the LCM method is the mirror image of HCF. Where HCF takes minimum counts of common primes, LCM takes maximum counts of all primes.

**Child:** So for 18 = 2 x 3 x 3 and 24 = 2 x 2 x 2 x 3, the LCM takes 2 (max 3 times) and 3 (max 2 times), giving 2 x 2 x 2 x 3 x 3 = 72?

**Mother:** Perfect!

---

## The Golden Link

**Child:** Maa, is there any connection between HCF and LCM?

**Mother:** A beautiful one. For any two numbers a and b, the product of their HCF and LCM equals the product of the numbers themselves. HCF times LCM equals a times b.

**Child:** Let me check with 15 and 20. HCF is 5, LCM is 60. 5 times 60 is 300. And 15 times 20 is also 300. It works!

**Mother:** This means if you know the HCF, you can find the LCM without doing prime factorisation again. Just use LCM = (a times b) divided by HCF.

**Child:** And what happens if I double both numbers?

**Mother:** Both the HCF and LCM also double. If HCF of 36 and 90 is 18, then HCF of 72 and 180 is 36. It follows directly from how prime factorisation works -- doubling adds one extra factor of 2 to each number.

**Child:** These shortcuts are going to save me so much time in exams!

## Real-Life Uses

**Child:** Maa, where do people actually use HCF and LCM in daily life?

**Mother:** A gardener with 60 rose plants and 84 marigold plants wants equal rows with one flower type per row. HCF of 60 and 84 is 12, so each row has 12 plants -- 5 rows of roses and 7 of marigolds. That is HCF in action.

**Child:** And LCM?

**Mother:** Your friend Priya visits the library every 8 days and Kavita visits every 6 days. If they meet today, they will next meet after LCM of 8 and 6 = 24 days. Bus schedules, train timetables, even the timing of traffic signals at a crossing -- all use LCM to find when things synchronise again.

**Child:** It is like HCF finds the biggest piece that fits into everything, and LCM finds the earliest moment when everything lines up together!

**Mother:** That is a wonderful way to remember it, beta. HCF looks backward at what divides evenly, LCM looks forward at when multiples meet. Two sides of the same mathematical coin.
