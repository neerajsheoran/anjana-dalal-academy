## Why Negative Times Negative Gives Positive

**Child:** Maa, I get that positive times negative gives negative — like 3 times −5 equals −15. But why does −3 times −5 equal positive 15? That feels strange.

**Mother:** Let me show you through a pattern, beta. Start with 3 times −4 equals −12. Now decrease the first number by 1 each time: 2 times −4 equals −8, then 1 times −4 equals −4, then 0 times −4 equals 0. Do you see what is happening to the products?

**Child:** They are going up by 4 each time!

**Mother:** Exactly. So continuing the pattern: −1 times −4 equals 4, and −2 times −4 equals 8. The pattern forces negative times negative to be positive. Mathematics demands consistency — if the pattern works for positive numbers, it must extend smoothly to negative numbers.

**Child:** So it is not just a rule to memorise — the pattern requires it?

**Mother:** That is exactly right. The sign rules are beautifully simple: matching signs give a positive result, clashing signs give a negative result. And the size of the answer is always the product of the sizes, no matter the signs. If you know 245 times 312 is 76,440, then −245 times −312 is also 76,440, while −245 times 312 is −76,440.

## How Division Follows Multiplication

**Child:** Maa, if multiplication has sign rules, does division follow the same ones?

**Mother:** Yes, because division is the reverse of multiplication. If −3 times 5 equals −15, then −15 divided by −3 equals 5, and −15 divided by 5 equals −3. Matching signs give positive quotients, clashing signs give negative quotients.

**Child:** So −24 divided by −6 is positive 4, and 21 divided by −7 is −3?

**Mother:** Correct! But there is one crucial difference from multiplication. Division is NOT commutative. 15 divided by 3 is 5, but 3 divided by 15 is not 5. And dividing by zero is always undefined.

**Child:** Why can we never divide by zero, Maa?

**Mother:** Suppose 7 divided by 0 equalled some number x. Then x times 0 would need to equal 7. But anything times 0 is 0, never 7. No such x exists, so the operation has no answer. That is why your calculator shows an error when you try it.

## Properties That Carry Over

**Child:** Maa, does integer multiplication behave the same way as whole number multiplication?

**Mother:** All the key properties carry over. Commutative: a times b equals b times a. For instance, 4 times −6 equals −24 and −6 times 4 equals −24. Associative: the grouping does not matter. And distributive: a times (b plus c) equals a times b plus a times c.

**Child:** What about multiplying by 0 and 1?

**Mother:** Any integer times 0 gives 0. Any integer times 1 stays unchanged. And here is a handy one — any integer times −1 gives its additive inverse. So −1 times 9 equals −9, and −1 times −9 equals 9. Multiplying by −1 simply flips the sign.

**Child:** Oh! That is why when we expand −(a + b), we get −a − b. It is the same as −1 times (a + b), which by the distributive property gives −1 times a plus −1 times b!

**Mother:** Brilliant connection, beta! The distributive property with −1 is exactly what explains why the sign flips inside brackets. Everything in mathematics is connected.

## Brahmagupta — The Pioneer

**Child:** Maa, who first figured out these rules for negative numbers?

**Mother:** The great Indian mathematician Brahmagupta, who lived from about 598 to 668 CE. In his work called Brahmasphutasiddhanta, written in 628 CE, he described systematic rules for arithmetic with negative numbers and zero — nearly 1400 years ago!

**Child:** He worked with negative numbers that long ago? Even today people find them confusing!

**Mother:** Brahmagupta called positive numbers "dhana" meaning wealth, and negative numbers "rina" meaning debt. He wrote that a debt taken away from nothing gives wealth — meaning 0 minus a negative number equals a positive number. His rules are exactly what we use in class today.

**Child:** India really gave the world so much in mathematics, Maa!

**Mother:** Absolutely, beta. The concept of zero, the decimal place value system, and the rules for working with negative numbers all trace back to Indian mathematicians. Every time you use these rules, you are using ideas perfected in India over a thousand years ago.

## Integers in the Real World

**Child:** Where do we actually see negative numbers in daily life, Maa?

**Mother:** Think about winter temperatures in Shimla or Manali. If the temperature drops by 4 degrees every hour, after 6 hours the total change is 6 times −4 equals −24 degrees.

**Child:** What about money?

**Mother:** If Anil borrows Rs 75 from each of his 3 friends, his total debt is 3 times −75 equals −Rs 225. The negative sign tells us it is money owed, not owned.

**Child:** And what about a deep-sea diver?

**Mother:** If a diver descends 6 metres every minute, after 7 minutes the position is 7 times −6 equals −42 metres. The negative naturally means "below the surface."

**Child:** So integers with their sign rules model anything that has two opposite directions — up and down, profit and loss, hot and cold?

**Mother:** Precisely! That is what makes integers so useful. They extend our number system to handle situations where direction matters. Positive and negative are not just abstract ideas — they represent real opposing quantities in the world around us.
