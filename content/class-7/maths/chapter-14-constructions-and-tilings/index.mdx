Armed with nothing more than a plain ruler and a compass, we can create geometrically perfect figures. In this chapter, we learn how to construct perpendicular bisectors, angle bisectors, and standard angles -- and then explore the captivating world of tilings and tessellations, where shapes fit together to cover surfaces with no gaps.

## The Perpendicular Bisector

### Folding a Rope in Half

Imagine a village carpenter who needs to find the exact centre of a plank without any measuring tape. He can use the same idea as the perpendicular bisector: mark two points at the ends, then use arcs to locate the midpoint precisely.

Given a line segment PQ, the **perpendicular bisector** is a line that:
- Passes through the **midpoint** of PQ (cutting it into two equal halves)
- Stands at **90 degrees** to PQ

> **Fundamental Property:** Every point that is **equally far** from P and Q lies on the perpendicular bisector of PQ.

### The Logic Behind It: A Proof Using Congruence

Let M be the point where the perpendicular bisector meets PQ. We can prove this construction works by showing that triangles formed on either side are congruent:

1. The two intersection points of arcs (call them A and B) are each equidistant from P and Q (same compass radius)
2. AP = AQ and BP = BQ (from the arc construction)
3. AB is common to both triangles ABP and ABQ
4. By SSS, triangle ABP is congruent to triangle ABQ

From this congruence:
- MP = MQ (M is the midpoint of PQ)
- Angle AMP = Angle AMQ = 90 degrees (they add up to 180 degrees on a straight line)

![Step-by-step construction of a perpendicular bisector of a line segment using compass arcs](perpendicular-bisector-construction.png)

### Construction Steps

Given a line segment PQ:

**Step 1:** Place the compass point at P. Open it to any radius that is more than half of PQ. Draw arcs above and below PQ.

**Step 2:** Without adjusting the compass width, place the point at Q and draw arcs that cross the first pair. Label the crossing points A (above) and B (below).

**Step 3:** Draw a straight line through A and B. This is the perpendicular bisector of PQ.

> **Historical Connection:** The **Shulba-Sutras**, ancient Indian texts on geometry composed over 2500 years ago during the Vedic period, describe methods for constructing perpendicular bisectors using a **rope** as a measuring tool. The Katyayana Shulba-Sutra explains how to find a midpoint by tying a rope between two pegs and pulling its centre taut -- the same geometric principle we apply today with a compass!

### Good to Know

- The two arc-pairs (above and below) need **not** use the same compass opening -- any two points equidistant from P and Q will work.
- You can even draw both arc-pairs on the **same side** of PQ (since any two distinct points determine a unique line).

## Building a 90-Degree Angle

To construct a right angle at a point O on a line:

**Step 1:** Using the compass, mark two points P and Q on the line at equal distances from O (making O the midpoint of PQ).

**Step 2:** Construct the perpendicular bisector of PQ. Since O is already the midpoint, you only need to find one additional point on the bisector. Draw arcs from P and Q (with equal radius) to locate a point A above the line.

**Step 3:** Draw OA. The angle between OA and the line is exactly **90 degrees**.

## Splitting an Angle in Half: The Angle Bisector

To **bisect** an angle is to divide it into two **equal** halves.

![Step-by-step construction of an angle bisector using compass arcs](angle-bisector-construction.png)

### Construction Steps

Given angle POQ:

**Step 1:** With the compass at the vertex O, draw an arc that cuts OP at point A and OQ at point B. Now OA = OB.

**Step 2:** With the compass at A, draw an arc. Without changing the radius, draw an arc from B. Let them meet at point C.

**Step 3:** Draw OC. This ray bisects angle POQ.

### Why This Works

By construction: OA = OB and AC = BC. OC is shared by both triangles OAC and OBC. By SSS, the triangles are congruent, which means angle AOC = angle BOC -- the angle has been split equally.

## Constructing Standard Angles

By combining perpendicular bisectors and angle bisectors, we can build many commonly used angles:

| Angle | How to Construct It |
|-------|--------|
| **90 degrees** | Construct a perpendicular at a point on a line |
| **45 degrees** | Bisect a 90-degree angle |
| **60 degrees** | Construct an equilateral triangle (all angles are 60 degrees) |
| **30 degrees** | Bisect a 60-degree angle |
| **120 degrees** | Place two 60-degree angles side by side |
| **150 degrees** | 180 - 30 degrees |

### Making a 60-Degree Angle

**Step 1:** Draw a ray OA.

**Step 2:** With the compass at O, draw an arc hitting OA at a point P.

**Step 3:** Without changing the compass width, place it at P and draw an arc that crosses the first arc at Q.

**Step 4:** Draw OQ. Angle POQ = 60 degrees.

This works because all three sides of triangle OPQ equal the compass radius, making it equilateral. Every angle of an equilateral triangle measures 60 degrees.

## Tilings and Tessellations

A **tiling** (or **tessellation**) is a pattern of shapes that covers a flat surface entirely, with no gaps and no overlaps.

![Tessellation patterns showing equilateral triangles, squares, and regular hexagons tiling a plane](regular-polygon-tessellations.png)

### Which Regular Polygons Tile by Themselves?

For a regular polygon to tile a flat surface on its own, its interior angle must go into 360 degrees a whole number of times (because the angles meeting at each corner must total exactly 360 degrees):

| Shape | Interior Angle | 360 / Angle | Can It Tile Alone? |
|-------|---------------|-------------|-----------|
| Equilateral triangle | 60 degrees | 6 | **Yes** (6 meet at each corner) |
| Square | 90 degrees | 4 | **Yes** (4 meet at each corner) |
| Regular hexagon | 120 degrees | 3 | **Yes** (3 meet at each corner) |
| Regular pentagon | 108 degrees | 3.33... | **No** |
| Regular octagon | 135 degrees | 2.67... | **No** (but can tile with squares) |

> Only **three** regular polygons can tile a flat surface all by themselves: equilateral triangles, squares, and regular hexagons.

### Mixing Shapes Together

Different regular polygons can combine to create tilings, as long as the angles at every meeting point sum to exactly **360 degrees**:
- Regular octagons + squares (135 + 135 + 90 = 360)
- Regular hexagons + equilateral triangles
- Squares + equilateral triangles

### Tilings in Indian Art and Architecture

Geometric tiling patterns have been part of Indian culture for millennia:
- **Kolam** and **rangoli** designs drawn at doorsteps across South India are intricate geometric tilings
- **Jali** screens in Mughal monuments like the Fatehpur Sikri palace feature precise tessellations carved in stone
- Temple floors across India, from Karnataka to Rajasthan, display square and hexagonal tile arrangements

## Constructing Regular Polygons Inside a Circle

To build a regular polygon inscribed within a circle:

1. Draw a circle
2. Divide the full 360 degrees at the centre into equal angles
3. Where the angle lines meet the circle, you get the vertices of the polygon

| Polygon | Central Angle | How to Build It |
|---------|--------------|-------------------|
| Equilateral triangle | 120 degrees | Step the compass around the circle with radius equal to the circle's radius, picking every second point |
| Square | 90 degrees | Draw two perpendicular diameters |
| Regular hexagon | 60 degrees | Step the compass (set to the radius) around the circle |

> **Surprising Fact:** To draw a regular hexagon inside a circle, just walk the compass around the circumference -- it lands exactly 6 times! This happens because the side of an inscribed regular hexagon equals the radius of the circle.

## Key Words to Remember

| Word | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Perpendicular bisector** | A line through the midpoint of a segment that meets it at right angles |
| **Bisect** | To divide into two equal parts |
| **Angle bisector** | A ray that splits an angle into two equal angles |
| **Compass** | A tool for drawing circles and arcs |
| **Tiling (Tessellation)** | A gap-free, overlap-free arrangement of shapes covering a flat surface |
| **Regular polygon** | A polygon with all sides equal and all angles equal |
| **Interior angle** | The angle formed inside a polygon at each vertex |
| **Shulba-Sutras** | Ancient Indian geometry texts that describe construction methods using ropes |
