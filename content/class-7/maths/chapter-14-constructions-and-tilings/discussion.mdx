## Finding the Middle Without Measuring

**Child:** Maa, how can I find the exact centre of a line segment using only a compass and a ruler, without actually measuring anything?

**Mother:** That is the magic of the perpendicular bisector, beta. Open your compass to any width greater than half the segment. Draw arcs from both endpoints -- above and below the line. Connect the two points where the arcs cross. That connecting line hits your segment at its precise midpoint, and it stands at a perfect right angle.

**Child:** But why does it work?

**Mother:** Because both crossing points are exactly the same distance from both endpoints -- that is how arcs work with equal compass openings. Any point equally far from both ends must lie on the perpendicular bisector. Two such points fix the line completely.

**Child:** So we are using logic instead of measurement!

**Mother:** Exactly. And this is not a new idea. The Shulba Sutras, our ancient Indian geometry texts written over 2500 years ago, describe the same principle using ropes. The Katyayana Shulba Sutra explains tying a rope between two stakes and pulling the midpoint taut to locate equidistant points. Mathematicians across cultures arrived at the same beautiful truth.

## Building Angles Without a Protractor

**Child:** Maa, how can I draw a 60-degree angle without a protractor?

**Mother:** Draw a ray from a point O. Place the compass at O and draw an arc across the ray. Now without changing the compass width, place it at the point where the arc meets the ray and draw another arc crossing the first one. Connect that crossing point to O. That angle is exactly 60 degrees.

**Child:** How can we be sure it is 60?

**Mother:** Because all three distances -- from O to the first point, from O to the second point, and between the two points -- equal the compass radius. You have made an equilateral triangle. Every angle of an equilateral triangle is exactly 60 degrees.

**Child:** Can I build other angles from 60?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Bisect 60 degrees to get 30. Construct a perpendicular for 90 degrees. Bisect 90 to get 45. Place two 60-degree angles next to each other for 120. And 180 minus 30 gives 150. Starting from just the 60-degree construction and the bisection technique, you can produce a whole family of standard angles.

## How Angle Bisection Works

**Child:** I understand the steps for bisecting an angle, Maa, but can you explain why the construction actually works?

**Mother:** Let us say you have angle POQ. You draw an arc from O that hits OP at A and OQ at B, so OA = OB. Then from A and B, you draw arcs with the same radius that meet at point C. Now look at triangles OAC and OBC.

**Child:** OA = OB from the first arc, AC = BC from the second pair of arcs, and OC is a shared side.

**Mother:** So by SSS, the two triangles are congruent. And congruent triangles have equal corresponding angles. That means angle AOC equals angle BOC -- the original angle has been split into two identical halves.

**Child:** So the construction is not just a trick -- there is a real proof behind it using triangle congruence!

**Mother:** Always, beta. Every geometric construction rests on solid reasoning. Understanding the proof means you can be confident the construction is exact, not approximate. And you are connecting ideas from different chapters -- congruence from geometry is the foundation for constructions here.

## Tiling the Floor

**Child:** Maa, I noticed that our kitchen floor uses square tiles, and the pavement near the gurudwara has hexagonal tiles. Can any shape tile a floor?

**Mother:** Only if the angles at each meeting point add up to exactly 360 degrees. For regular polygons: equilateral triangles have 60-degree angles, so 6 of them fit around a point. Squares have 90-degree angles, so 4 fit. Regular hexagons have 120-degree angles, so 3 fit. These are the only three regular shapes that can tile a surface alone.

**Child:** What about regular pentagons?

**Mother:** Their interior angle is 108 degrees. Divide 360 by 108 and you get 3.33 -- not a whole number. So pentagons always leave gaps. Regular octagons, with 135-degree angles, also cannot tile alone. But combine them with squares and it works perfectly: 135 + 135 + 90 = 360.

**Child:** So you can mix shapes as long as the angles add up?

**Mother:** Exactly. Hexagons and triangles, squares and triangles -- many combinations tile beautifully as long as every vertex has angles summing to 360 degrees.

## The Mathematics in Indian Art

**Child:** Are tilings only in maths textbooks, or do people actually use them?

**Mother:** They are everywhere in Indian life! The kolam patterns that Paati draws at the doorstep every morning are geometric tilings -- intricate, symmetrical, and mathematically precise. The jali screens in Mughal palaces like Fatehpur Sikri are tessellations carved in sandstone. Temple floors from Hampi to Jaisalmer use square and hexagonal tiles in gorgeous patterns.

**Child:** So artists and builders have been doing geometry for thousands of years?

**Mother:** For millennia! The Shulba Sutras describe geometric constructions for Vedic fire altars that required precise arrangements of bricks -- essentially tiling problems. When you admire a rangoli or a temple floor, you are looking at mathematics made beautiful. Even in nature, honeybees build hexagonal cells because hexagons tile perfectly while enclosing the most space with the least wax.

**Child:** Maa, I will never look at a tile floor the same way again!

**Mother:** And that, beta, is exactly the point. Mathematics is not locked inside textbooks. It is the hidden language of patterns -- in art, in architecture, in nature. Once you learn to read it, you see it everywhere.
