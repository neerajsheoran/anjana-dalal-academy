## Why Order Matters

**Child:** Maa, my classmate and I both solved 20 + 3 x 6 and got different answers. I got 38 and she got 138. Who is correct?

**Mother:** You are, beta! The confusion is about which operation to perform first. In 20 + 3 x 6, the multiplication 3 x 6 must happen before the addition. That gives 18. Then 20 + 18 = 38.

**Child:** But why does multiplication go before addition?

**Mother:** Think of it as a story. Priya had 20 stickers. Her friend gave her 3 packets with 6 stickers in each. The total is 20 plus 18, which is 38. If you add 20 and 3 first, you get 23 packets of 6, which is 138 — a completely different story that does not match the situation. The concept of terms keeps us on track.

**Child:** So the terms in 20 + 3 x 6 are 20 and 3 x 6?

**Mother:** Exactly! Terms are separated by the plus sign. You evaluate each term on its own first, then add them all together. This rule eliminates any confusion.

## Brackets to the Rescue

**Child:** Maa, Rohan bought a notebook for Rs 30 and a pen for Rs 18, then paid Rs 50. I wrote 50 - 30 + 18 and got Rs 38 as change. That seems too much!

**Mother:** You need brackets, beta. The correct expression is 50 - (30 + 18). Without brackets, the minus applies only to 30, and then 18 gets added separately. With brackets, you first find 30 + 18 = 48, then subtract from 50 to get Rs 2 change.

**Child:** So brackets group things that belong together?

**Mother:** Yes, just like punctuation in a sentence. "Come, let us eat, Dadi" is an invitation to eat together. "Come, let us eat Dadi" is... something very different! Brackets serve the same purpose in maths — they remove confusion. Always compute what is inside the brackets first.

**Child:** And when I remove brackets that have a minus sign in front, all signs inside flip?

**Mother:** Correct! 300 - (50 + 8) becomes 300 - 50 - 8. But 300 + (50 + 8) becomes 300 + 50 + 8 — no changes. This is a rule you must remember well for your exams.

## Swapping and Grouping

**Child:** Maa, does it matter in what order I add numbers?

**Mother:** Not at all! Whether you compute 9 + (-3) or (-3) + 9, the answer is 6 either way. This is called the commutative property. Think of wearing your bindi and your earrings — the order does not affect how you look!

**Child:** And what about grouping?

**Mother:** The associative property says you can group terms any way you prefer. For (-5) + 8 + (-9), you could add any two first and then the third. The answer is always -6. This is useful in real life — suppose I am adding up the grocery bill and I realize I forgot the dahi. I just add it to the total I already have. No need to start from scratch!

**Child:** Like when you were adding up the monthly kharcha and forgot the electricity bill?

**Mother:** Ha, exactly like that! I just added it at the end and the total was still correct. That is the beauty of the associative property.

## The Distributive Property

**Child:** Maa, I need to find 98 x 15 for my homework. Can I do this without long multiplication?

**Mother:** Of course! Break 98 into 100 - 2. Then 98 x 15 = (100 - 2) x 15 = 100 x 15 - 2 x 15 = 1500 - 30 = 1470. This is the distributive property at work.

**Child:** So I split one number to make the multiplication simpler?

**Mother:** Yes! And it works in reverse too. If you know 62 x 14 = 868, you can find 72 x 14 by writing it as (62 + 10) x 14 = 868 + 140 = 1008. You build on what you already know.

**Child:** This is how the aunty at our neighbourhood kirana store calculates! When I buy 4 packets of biscuits at Rs 48 each, she probably thinks 4 x 50 minus 4 x 2.

**Mother:** Exactly, beta. The distributive property is not just a textbook idea — it is the mental maths that shopkeepers, engineers, and homemakers use every single day. Once you master it, calculations become so much quicker.

## Expressions in Everyday Life

**Child:** Can we write real situations as expressions, Maa?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Suppose our family goes to the cinema. An adult ticket costs Rs 250 and a child ticket costs Rs 150. For 3 adults and 2 children, the total is 3 x 250 + 2 x 150 = 750 + 300 = Rs 1,050.

**Child:** And if Sunita aunty earns Rs 25,000 every month, spends Rs 8,000 on rent, Rs 6,000 on food, and Rs 3,000 on transport, her annual savings would be...

**Mother:** 12 x (25,000 - 8,000 - 6,000 - 3,000) = 12 x 8,000 = Rs 96,000. See how we used brackets to first compute the monthly savings, and then multiplied by 12 for the year?

**Child:** Maths is really just a way to describe what happens in the real world!

**Mother:** That is the heart of it, beta. An arithmetic expression is a compact way to capture a real-life situation. Once you learn to go back and forth between words and expressions, word problems will never scare you again.
