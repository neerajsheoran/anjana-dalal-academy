## The Magic of Parity

**Child:** Maa, my friend Aarav gave me a challenge — pick exactly 5 tiles from 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, 17 that add up to 28. I have been trying for so long!

**Mother:** Beta, you can stop trying because it is impossible! And the reason is beautiful. All nine tiles carry odd numbers. When you add 5 odd numbers, the answer is always odd. Think about it: odd plus odd makes even, so two pairs of odds give you two even numbers, and one odd number is left. Even plus even plus odd equals odd.

**Child:** But 28 is even! So I can never reach it with five odd numbers?

**Mother:** Exactly right. This idea is called parity — checking whether something is even or odd. It lets you prove impossibility without trying every combination.

**Child:** Can you give me another example, Maa?

**Mother:** Sure. Priya and Neha are sisters born one year apart. Can their ages ever add up to 50? Their ages are always consecutive — one even, one odd. Even plus odd is always odd. But 50 is even. So the answer is no, never!

## Building Magic Squares

**Child:** Maa, I saw this grid in class where every row, column, and diagonal gave the same total. How does that work?

**Mother:** That is a magic square! For a 3 by 3 grid using numbers 1 to 9, the total of all nine numbers is 45. Since three rows share this total equally, each row must add up to 45 divided by 3, which is 15. That number 15 is called the magic constant.

**Child:** How do we figure out where to place each number?

**Mother:** Start with the centre. It must be 5, because 5 is the only number that can participate in enough combinations summing to 15 across all four directions through the centre. Then 1 and 9 go on the middle edges, not the corners. Once those are fixed, the corners follow logically.

**Child:** Is this something new that mathematicians discovered recently?

**Mother:** Not at all, beta! India has a famous 4 by 4 magic square carved into the walls of the Parshvanath Jain Temple in Khajuraho. It dates back to the 10th century. Its magic constant is 34, so it is called the Chautis Yantra. Our country has a long tradition of mathematical puzzles!

## The 1089 Surprise

**Child:** Maa, let me show you a trick. Pick any 3-digit number where the first and last digits differ by at least 2.

**Mother:** Alright, I will pick 714.

**Child:** Write it backwards: 417. Subtract the smaller from the larger: 714 minus 417 equals 297. Now reverse 297 to get 792. Add them: 297 plus 792 equals 1089!

**Mother:** That is clever! Let me try with 831. Reversed: 138. Subtract: 831 minus 138 equals 693. Reverse 693 to get 396. Add: 693 plus 396 equals... 1089 again!

**Child:** It always gives 1089! Why does that happen, Maa?

**Mother:** It is the magic of our place value system. When you subtract a 3-digit number from its reverse, the middle digit of the answer is always 9, and the first and last digits always add up to 9. So when you add that number to its reverse, the structure forces the answer to be 1089 every time.

## Quick Divisibility Checks

**Child:** Maa, is there a fast way to tell whether 5274 divides evenly by 9? Long division takes so long!

**Mother:** Add its digits: 5 plus 2 plus 7 plus 4 equals 18. Is 18 divisible by 9? Yes! So 5274 is divisible by 9 too. That is the divisibility test for 9.

**Child:** Are there tricks like this for other numbers?

**Mother:** Many! For 2, just check if the last digit is even. For 5, the last digit should be 0 or 5. For 3, add the digits and check if the sum divides by 3. For 4, look at just the last two digits. And for 11, calculate the alternating sum of the digits.

**Child:** Let me try 11 with the number 95381. Alternating sum from the right: 1 minus 8 plus 3 minus 5 plus 9 equals 0. Zero is divisible by 11, so 95381 is divisible by 11!

**Mother:** Perfect! These shortcuts save enormous time in exams, especially when simplifying fractions or factorising large numbers.

## Palindromes and Hidden Patterns

**Child:** Maa, what exactly is a palindrome in maths?

**Mother:** A number that reads the same forwards and backwards — like 252, 4664, or 78987. And here is a fun game: take any number, reverse it, and add the two. Keep repeating. Many numbers eventually become palindromes!

**Child:** Let me try with 57. Reverse is 75. Add: 57 plus 75 equals 132. Not a palindrome. Reverse 132 to get 231. Add: 132 plus 231 equals 363. That is a palindrome!

**Mother:** Wonderful! And the digit sum is another lovely idea. You keep adding the digits of a number until just one digit remains. For 876: 8 plus 7 plus 6 equals 21, then 2 plus 1 equals 3. The digit sum is 3.

**Child:** Numbers really do have so many secrets, Maa!

**Mother:** They do, beta. Mathematics is not just about calculation — it is about spotting patterns and asking "why?" The more curious you are, the more surprises numbers reveal.
