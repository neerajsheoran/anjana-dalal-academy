## When is a Question Statistical?

**Child:** Maa, my teacher said some questions are "statistical" and some are not. What does that mean?

**Mother:** A statistical question is one where the answers are expected to vary. "How tall are Class 7 students in your school?" is statistical because each student has a different height. But "What is 12 times 5?" is not statistical because the answer is always 60.

**Child:** So "How many hours do children in my class sleep?" would be statistical?

**Mother:** Yes, because some sleep 7 hours, some 9, some 8 -- the answers vary. And when we summarise such data, we get a statistical statement, like "On average, an Indian household watches 4 hours of television daily." That comes from surveying many families and finding a representative number.

**Child:** So statistics is about understanding groups of numbers, not just one number?

**Mother:** Exactly, beta. It gives us tools to look at a pile of varied data and make sense of it -- to spot patterns and compare groups in a fair way.

## The Mean: Sharing Equally

**Child:** Maa, Virat scored 35, 12, 8, 67, and 28 runs in 5 innings. His average is 30. But he never scored 30 in any innings!

**Mother:** That is what makes the mean so interesting. It tells you the "equal share." If Virat could magically redistribute his 150 total runs evenly across 5 innings, each innings would have exactly 30 runs. It represents his typical performance, even though no single innings matched it.

**Child:** And Rohit scored 41, 58, 5, and 16 in only 4 innings. His total is just 120, but his average is also 30?

**Mother:** Right. Because the mean divides by the number of innings. It adjusts for how many chances a batsman had, making it a fair way to compare two players. The ancient Indian mathematician Mahaviracharya called this concept "samkarana" -- levelling or equalising.

**Child:** Like pouring water from full glasses into empty ones until every glass has the same level?

**Mother:** What a beautiful way to picture it! That is exactly what the mean does -- it levels out the highs and lows.

## When the Mean Misleads

**Child:** Maa, Geeta's family has heights 162, 168, 172, 174, and 109 cm. The mean is 157 cm. But four people are much taller than that!

**Mother:** You have caught the mean red-handed! The 109 cm member is probably a small child, and that very low value pulls the average down unfairly. We call such an extreme value an outlier.

**Child:** So what should we use instead?

**Mother:** The median -- the middle value when you line up the data in order. Sorted heights: 109, 162, 168, 172, 174. The one in the centre is 168 cm. That feels much more like the "typical" height of this family, does it not?

**Child:** How does the median work when there is an even number of values?

**Mother:** Take Arjun's family: 148, 156, 161, 163, 170, 176 cm. Six values. The two in the middle are 161 and 163. Average them: (161 + 163) / 2 = 162 cm. That is the median.

**Child:** And outliers cannot mess up the median?

**Mother:** Hardly. Whether that smallest height is 109 or 50, the median would still be 168. That is what makes the median trustworthy when the data has extreme values.

## Why We Draw Bar Graphs

**Child:** Maa, why bother drawing graphs when we already have the numbers?

**Mother:** Because your eyes process pictures far faster than rows of numbers. If I tell you that the Painting club has 20 students, Singing has 35, Coding has 15, and Cricket has 45, you have to compare four numbers mentally. In a bar graph, you instantly see that Cricket's bar is the tallest and Coding's is the shortest.

**Child:** What about double bar graphs?

**Mother:** Those compare two sets of data side by side. For example, the number of boys and girls in each club, or potato prices in two different towns. Placing bars next to each other makes differences jump out visually -- you can immediately spot which town pays more or which activity attracts more girls.

**Child:** My teacher warned us about misleading graphs. What should I look for?

**Mother:** Always check whether the vertical scale starts at zero. A graph that starts at 90 instead of 0 can make a tiny difference of 3 look enormous. Also make sure the scale markings are evenly spaced. Advertisers sometimes play with scales to exaggerate claims.

## Dot Plots and Understanding Spread

**Child:** What is a dot plot, and how is it different from a bar graph?

**Mother:** A dot plot shows each individual data value as a dot on a number line. It is wonderful for seeing how data is distributed -- whether it is bunched together or scattered widely. Imagine monthly potato prices placed as dots on a number line. You immediately see if prices are stable or jumping around wildly.

**Child:** So it shows the range and clustering at a glance?

**Mother:** Yes. If one town's prices range from 14 to 52 rupees and another's from 18 to 50, the dot plot shows which town has more volatile prices. The range -- maximum minus minimum -- puts a number on that spread.

**Child:** I love that statistics turns boring numbers into stories!

**Mother:** That is its magic, beta. A single number like the mean tells you the "centre," the range tells you the "spread," and a graph shows you the whole picture. Without statistics, data is just noise. With it, data becomes understanding.
