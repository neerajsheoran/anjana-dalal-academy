Numbers are everywhere around us -- "The temperature today is 38 degrees" or "Students scored an average of 72 marks." But how do we collect numbers, organise them, and draw meaningful conclusions? This chapter introduces the foundations of **statistics** -- the art and science of making sense of data.

## What Makes a Question Statistical?

A **statistical question** is one whose answer requires collecting data, and the data is expected to **vary**. For example:
- "How many hours do Class 7 students sleep each night?" (statistical -- sleep hours differ from student to student)
- "What is the weight of stray dogs in our neighbourhood?" (statistical -- each dog has a different weight)

A question with a single fixed answer is not statistical:
- "What is 12 x 5?" (not statistical -- the answer is always 60)

A **statistical statement** summarises data using numbers, percentages, or patterns. Examples:
- "I spend about 20 minutes walking to school each day."
- "The price of dal has increased by Rs 15 per kg this year."
- "On average, an Indian household watches 4 hours of television daily."

> **Statistics** is the discipline of collecting, arranging, analysing, interpreting, and displaying data.

## Finding a Single Number to Represent Data: The Mean

When we want to compare groups or summarise a collection of numbers, we often look for one **representative value**. The most widely used one is the **arithmetic mean** (or simply, the **average**).

### How to Calculate the Mean

> **Mean = Total of all values / Count of values**

**Example:** Virat scored 35, 12, 8, 67, and 28 runs across 5 innings.

Mean = (35 + 12 + 8 + 67 + 28) / 5 = 150 / 5 = **30 runs per innings**

Rohit scored 41, 58, 5, and 16 runs across 4 innings.

Mean = (41 + 58 + 5 + 16) / 4 = 120 / 4 = **30 runs per innings**

Both batsmen have the same average of 30, even though Virat scored more runs overall (150 vs 120). The mean levels the playing field when group sizes differ.

### The Mean as Equal Sharing

Another way to think about the mean: it is the amount each person would get if everything were **distributed equally**.

If 4 friends collect 2, 9, 7, and 6 mangoes respectively, the mean is (2 + 9 + 7 + 6) / 4 = 24 / 4 = **6 mangoes each** if shared fairly.

> **Indian Heritage:** The concept of averaging has deep roots in Indian mathematics. **Brahmagupta** (628 CE) used the term *samarajju* to describe the mean measure of a line segment. **Mahaviracharya** (850 CE) used *samkarana*, meaning "levelling" or "equalising" -- capturing the idea that the mean smooths out differences.

## Seeing Data Visually: Dot Plots

A **dot plot** displays each value in a data set as a dot on a number line. It instantly reveals:
- Where values **bunch together** (clusters)
- How **spread out** the data is
- The **highest** and **lowest** values

![Dot plot showing data values as dots on a number line for onion prices in two towns](dot-plot-onion-prices.png)

**Example:** Monthly potato prices (Rs/kg) in two towns:

Sundarpur: 18, 20, 19, 22, 25, 30, 34, 38, 42, 48, 50, 35
Chandanpur: 15, 14, 20, 27, 33, 30, 37, 34, 46, 52, 45, 36

On a dot plot, Chandanpur's prices scatter from 14 to 52 while Sundarpur's range from 18 to 50 -- both have wide spread but Chandanpur shows greater variability.

> **Note:** Dot plots do not show the order of data (which month had which price), but they are excellent for spotting patterns and spread at a glance.

## Different Ways to Compare Data

When studying two collections of numbers, we can examine:

| Measure | What It Reveals |
|---------|-----------------|
| **Total sum** | Overall quantity (but unfair when group sizes differ) |
| **Mean (average)** | Typical value per item |
| **Maximum** | Highest value in the set |
| **Minimum** | Lowest value in the set |
| **Range** (max - min) | How widely the data is spread |

## When the Mean Does Not Tell the Full Story

### Outliers and Their Effect

The mean is not always a fair summary. Look at this example:

Heights of five members of Geeta's family: 162 cm, 168 cm, 172 cm, 174 cm, 109 cm

Mean = (162 + 168 + 172 + 174 + 109) / 5 = 785 / 5 = **157 cm**

But 4 out of 5 family members are taller than 157 cm! The very short member at 109 cm (a young toddler) drags the average down. Such an extreme value is called an **outlier** -- a data point that sits far from the rest.

### The Median: A Resistant Alternative

The **median** is the middle value when data is sorted in order. It stays steady even when outliers try to pull things off course.

**How to find the median:**
1. Arrange the data from smallest to largest
2. If the count is **odd**, the median is the value sitting exactly in the middle
3. If the count is **even**, the median is the average of the two middle values

**Example (odd count):**
Geeta's family heights sorted: 109, 162, 168, 172, 174
Median = **168 cm** (the 3rd value out of 5)

This represents the family's typical height much better than the mean of 157 cm!

**Example (even count):**
Arjun's family heights sorted: 148, 156, 161, 163, 170, 176
Median = (161 + 163) / 2 = **162 cm**

### When to Use Mean vs Median

| Feature | Mean | Median |
|---------|------|--------|
| Sensitive to outliers? | **Yes** -- outliers drag it toward them | **No** -- barely moves |
| Best used when? | Data has no extreme values | Outliers are present |
| When are they similar? | When data is evenly spread | Same situation |

## Displaying Data with Bar Graphs

A **bar graph** uses rectangular bars to show data visually. The height of each bar matches the value it represents.

### Steps to Draw a Bar Graph

1. Draw two perpendicular lines (axes)
2. Write the categories along the horizontal axis
3. Choose a suitable **scale** for the vertical axis
4. Draw bars of the right height for each category
5. Keep all bars the same width with equal gaps between them

**Example:** Students enrolled in different hobby clubs:

| Hobby | Students |
|-------|---------|
| Painting | 20 |
| Singing | 35 |
| Coding | 15 |
| Cricket | 45 |

![Bar graph showing number of students in Painting, Singing, Coding, and Cricket clubs](bar-graph-student-activities.png)

The tallest bar (Cricket) instantly shows the most popular hobby, while the shortest bar (Coding) shows the least popular.

### Double Bar Graphs

![Double bar graph comparing two data sets side by side with a legend](double-bar-graph-comparison.png)

A **double bar graph** places two sets of bars side by side for direct comparison. For instance, comparing the number of boys and girls in each club, or potato prices across two years.

## Picking the Right Scale

When constructing bar graphs, choose the vertical scale wisely:
- For values up to 50, a scale of 1 unit = 5 works well
- For values up to 1000, try 1 unit = 100
- The scale should always start from **0** to keep comparisons honest

> **Caution:** A bar graph starting from a number other than 0 can exaggerate small differences, making them look much bigger than they are. Always examine the scale before drawing conclusions!

## Looking Deeper into Data

Careful observation of data raises interesting questions:
- Do seasons affect vegetable prices?
- What causes a particular month to have unusually high prices?
- How do price changes impact both farmers and buyers?

Statistics transforms raw numbers into **patterns**, **trends**, and **insights** that help us understand the world better.

## Key Words to Remember

| Word | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Statistics** | The study of collecting, organising, analysing, interpreting, and presenting data |
| **Statistical question** | A question that expects variable answers and requires data collection |
| **Mean (Arithmetic Mean)** | The total of all values divided by how many values there are; the "average" |
| **Median** | The middle value in a sorted data set |
| **Outlier** | A value that is far removed from the rest of the data |
| **Range** | The gap between the maximum and minimum values |
| **Dot plot** | A graph that shows each data value as a dot on a number line |
| **Bar graph** | A graph using rectangular bars to display and compare data |
| **Double bar graph** | A bar graph showing two related data sets placed side by side |
| **Scale** | The unit size used to mark values on a graph axis |
