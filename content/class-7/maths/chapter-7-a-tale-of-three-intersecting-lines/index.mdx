Three straight sticks joined end to end create the simplest closed shape in geometry — a triangle. Despite this simplicity, triangles hold rich properties. This chapter covers how to build triangles, when they can and cannot exist, the angle sum property, and the exterior angle relationship.

## The Anatomy of a Triangle

A **triangle** is made up of:
- **Three vertices** (corner points), typically labelled P, Q, R
- **Three sides** (line segments) connecting these vertices: PQ, QR, RP
- **Three interior angles** at each vertex: angle P, angle Q, angle R

We refer to a triangle by naming its vertices, such as **triangle PQR**. The vertices may be listed in any sequence.

## The Equilateral Triangle

An **equilateral triangle** has three sides of identical length — the most balanced and symmetric triangle possible.

![Step-by-step construction of an equilateral triangle using compass arcs from two endpoints](equilateral-triangle-construction.png)

### How to Construct One

To draw an equilateral triangle with each side measuring 5 cm:

**Step 1:** Use a ruler to draw the base PQ = 5 cm.

**Step 2:** Set the compass opening to 5 cm. Place the compass tip on P and draw an arc above the line.

**Step 3:** Without changing the compass opening, place the tip on Q and draw a second arc. The two arcs meet at a point R.

**Step 4:** Draw line segments PR and QR. Triangle PQR is your equilateral triangle.

> **Why this works:** Point R sits on both arcs, so it is exactly 5 cm from P and exactly 5 cm from Q. Along with PQ = 5 cm, every side measures the same.

## Constructing a Triangle from Three Known Sides

To build a triangle with sides 5 cm, 7 cm, and 9 cm:

**Step 1:** Draw the base PQ = 5 cm (pick any side to start).

**Step 2:** Open the compass to 7 cm and draw an arc from P.

**Step 3:** Open the compass to 9 cm and draw an arc from Q.

**Step 4:** The two arcs cross at a point R. Connect PR and QR to finish the triangle.

This intersection point works because:
- R lies on the arc of radius 7 cm from P, so PR = 7 cm
- R lies on the arc of radius 9 cm from Q, so QR = 9 cm

### Classifying Triangles by Their Sides

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Equilateral** | All three sides match |
| **Isosceles** | Exactly two sides match |
| **Scalene** | No two sides match |

## The Triangle Inequality

### Do All Triplets of Lengths Make a Triangle?

Not every combination of three lengths can form a triangle! Attempt to build a triangle with sides 2 cm, 3 cm, and 9 cm — you will fail. The two shorter sticks cannot bridge the gap across the longest one.

### The Shortcut Principle

Imagine walking between two bus stops. The direct footpath is always shorter than any detour through a third point. In a triangle, the straight side joining two vertices is always shorter than the two-side route through the third vertex. This gives us:

> **For any triangle with sides p, q, and r:**
> - p &lt; q + r
> - q &lt; p + r
> - r &lt; p + q
>
> Every side must be **shorter than** the combined length of the other two. This is the **triangle inequality**.

### Testing Whether a Triangle Can Exist

You only need one check: verify that the **longest side** is shorter than the sum of the remaining two.

**Example:** Do sides 8 cm, 12 cm, and 25 cm form a triangle?

Check: Is 25 &lt; 8 + 12?
Is 25 &lt; 20? **No!**

So this triangle **cannot exist**.

**Example:** Do sides 6 cm, 7 cm, and 11 cm form a triangle?

Check: Is 11 &lt; 6 + 7?
Is 11 &lt; 13? **Yes!**

So this triangle **can exist**.

> **Quick Test:** Identify the longest side. If it is shorter than the other two sides added together, a triangle is possible. Otherwise, it is not.

### Why Does the Inequality Guarantee a Triangle?

During construction:
1. The longest side serves as the base PQ
2. Arcs of the other two lengths are drawn from P and Q

Three scenarios arise:

| Scenario | Condition | Outcome |
|----------|-----------|---------|
| Arcs cross each other | Sum of shorter sides > longest side | **Triangle exists** |
| Arcs barely touch | Sum of shorter sides = longest side | No triangle (points lie on a single line) |
| Arcs fail to meet | Sum of shorter sides &lt; longest side | **No triangle** |

When the triangle inequality holds, the arcs always intersect, confirming the triangle can be formed.

| Set of Lengths | Longest &lt; Sum of Others? | Triangle Possible? |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| 3, 3, 7 | 7 &lt; 6? No | No |
| 5, 6, 9 | 9 &lt; 11? Yes | Yes |
| 4, 4, 7 | 7 &lt; 8? Yes | Yes |
| 8, 15, 30 | 30 &lt; 23? No | No |
| 2, 50, 50 | 50 &lt; 52? Yes | Yes |

## The Angle Sum Property

### Interior Angles Always Total 180 Degrees

One of the cornerstones of triangle geometry:

> **The three interior angles of any triangle always add up to 180 degrees.**

![Triangle with three angles torn off and placed together forming a straight line of 180 degrees](angle-sum-property-triangle.png)

You can verify this by:

1. **Paper tearing activity:** Cut a triangle from paper, tear off all three corners, and place them side by side — they form a straight line (180 degrees)!

2. **Parallel line proof:** Draw a line through one vertex running parallel to the opposite side. Using alternate angle relationships with the two other sides proves the three angles sit together on a straight line.

### Worked Examples

**Example:** In a triangle, angle P = 45 degrees and angle Q = 75 degrees. Find angle R.

Angle R = 180 − 45 − 75 = **60 degrees**

**Example:** In an equilateral triangle, what is each angle?

All three angles are identical. 3 × angle = 180, so each angle = **60 degrees**

**Example:** An isosceles triangle has an apex angle of 50 degrees. What are the two base angles?

Let each base angle be x.
50 + x + x = 180
2x = 130
x = **65 degrees**

![Three triangles showing acute, right, and obtuse types with angles marked](triangle-types-by-angles.png)

### Classifying Triangles by Their Angles

| Type | Description | Example Angles |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| **Acute triangle** | Every angle is below 90 degrees | 55, 65, 60 |
| **Right triangle** | One angle is exactly 90 degrees | 90, 50, 40 |
| **Obtuse triangle** | One angle exceeds 90 degrees | 110, 40, 30 |

> **Can a triangle have two right angles?** No! Two angles of 90 degrees already total 180 degrees, leaving nothing for the third angle.

> **Can a triangle have two obtuse angles?** No! Two angles exceeding 90 degrees each would exceed 180 degrees, breaking the angle sum rule.

## The Exterior Angle Property

An **exterior angle** forms when one side of a triangle is extended outward. It is the angle between the extension and the adjacent side.

> **Exterior Angle Property:** An exterior angle of a triangle equals the **sum of the two non-adjacent interior angles**.

If the exterior angle is at vertex R, and the interior angles at P and Q are 45 degrees and 75 degrees, then the exterior angle = 45 + 75 = **120 degrees**.

This follows from the angle sum property: the interior angle at R is 180 − 45 − 75 = 60 degrees, and the exterior angle = 180 − 60 = 120 degrees.

## Key Words to Remember

| Word | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Triangle** | A closed shape with three sides, three vertices, and three interior angles |
| **Equilateral triangle** | A triangle whose three sides (and three angles) are all equal |
| **Isosceles triangle** | A triangle with exactly two equal sides |
| **Scalene triangle** | A triangle with three sides of different lengths |
| **Triangle inequality** | The rule that every side of a triangle must be shorter than the sum of the other two |
| **Angle sum property** | The three interior angles of a triangle always total 180 degrees |
| **Exterior angle** | The angle between an extended side and the adjacent side of a triangle |
| **Acute triangle** | A triangle where every angle is less than 90 degrees |
| **Right triangle** | A triangle with one 90-degree angle |
| **Obtuse triangle** | A triangle with one angle greater than 90 degrees |
