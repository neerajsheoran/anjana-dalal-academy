## When Triangles Refuse to Exist

**Child:** Maa, I tried making a triangle with sides 2 cm, 3 cm, and 9 cm using my compass, but the arcs just would not cross. What went wrong?

**Mother:** Nothing went wrong with your drawing, beta. Those three lengths simply cannot form a triangle. The two shorter sides, 2 and 3, add up to only 5. But the longest side is 9. Since 5 is much less than 9, the arcs from the ends of the 9 cm base can never reach each other.

**Child:** So there is a rule for which sets of lengths actually work?

**Mother:** Yes, it is called the triangle inequality. The longest side must be strictly less than the sum of the two shorter sides. Think of it like walking between two places — the straight path is always shorter than any route through a third point.

**Child:** Let me check: 6, 7, and 11. The longest is 11. Is 11 less than 6 plus 7, which is 13? Yes! So this triangle can be made.

**Mother:** Correct! And what about 8, 15, and 30?

**Child:** Longest is 30. Sum of the other two is 8 plus 15 equals 23. Is 30 less than 23? No! So no triangle. This quick check is so useful, Maa!

## Drawing Triangles Step by Step

**Child:** Maa, how do I actually construct a triangle when I know all three sides?

**Mother:** Let me walk you through it. Say the sides are 5, 7, and 9 cm. First, draw the base PQ equal to 5 cm with your ruler. Then open your compass to 7 cm, place the tip on P, and draw an arc above the base. Now open the compass to 9 cm, place it on Q, and draw another arc. Where the two arcs cross is your third vertex R.

**Child:** Why does that crossing point give the correct triangle?

**Mother:** Because R lies on the first arc, so it is exactly 7 cm from P. It also lies on the second arc, so it is exactly 9 cm from Q. Together with PQ equal to 5 cm, all three sides are exactly what we wanted. The triangle inequality guarantees the arcs will actually cross.

**Child:** And for an equilateral triangle, all three arcs would use the same radius?

**Mother:** Exactly. For a 5 cm equilateral triangle, draw PQ equal to 5 cm, then arcs of 5 cm from both P and Q. The intersection gives the third vertex, and every side is identical. That is why equilateral triangles are the most perfectly balanced.

## Why Angles Always Add to 180 Degrees

**Child:** Maa, our teacher said the three angles of any triangle always total 180 degrees. Is that really true for every single triangle?

**Mother:** Every single one, beta. Here is a hands-on way to see it. Cut out a paper triangle, tear off all three corners, and arrange them side by side. They will form a perfectly straight line, and a straight angle is exactly 180 degrees.

**Child:** That is just one triangle though. How do we know it works for all of them?

**Mother:** There is an elegant proof using parallel lines. Draw a line through one vertex that runs parallel to the opposite side. The alternate angles formed with the other two sides give you the triangle's three angles sitting together along a straight line. Since a straight line is 180 degrees, the three angles must add to 180 degrees.

**Child:** So it connects back to what we learned about parallel lines and alternate angles!

**Mother:** That is the beauty of mathematics, beta. Different topics are deeply linked. Now, tell me — can a triangle ever have two right angles?

**Child:** If two angles are 90 degrees each, that is already 180 degrees. The third angle would be 0 degrees, which makes no sense! So no, it cannot happen.

**Mother:** And two obtuse angles? Each would be more than 90 degrees, so two of them would already exceed 180 degrees. Impossible!

## Sorting Triangles into Families

**Child:** How do we classify triangles, Maa?

**Mother:** Two ways. By sides: equilateral means all three sides match, isosceles means exactly two sides match, and scalene means every side is different. By angles: acute means all angles are below 90 degrees, right means one angle is exactly 90 degrees, and obtuse means one angle is more than 90 degrees.

**Child:** In an equilateral triangle, all angles are equal and they sum to 180 degrees, so each angle must be 60 degrees?

**Mother:** Spot on! And in an isosceles triangle with a 50-degree angle at the apex, the two base angles are equal. If each is x, then 50 plus 2x equals 180, so x equals 65 degrees.

**Child:** What about exterior angles? My teacher said they equal the sum of the two far-away interior angles.

**Mother:** That is the exterior angle property. If the angles at P and Q are 45 and 75 degrees, the interior angle at R is 60 degrees, and the exterior angle at R is 180 minus 60 equals 120 degrees. Notice that 45 plus 75 also equals 120. This property shows up in many exam problems.

## Testing Side Lengths Like a Pro

**Child:** Maa, how do I quickly decide if three given lengths form a triangle during an exam?

**Mother:** Find the longest length and check if it is less than the sum of the other two. That single check is all you need.

**Child:** Let me practice. How about 3, 3, and 7?

**Mother:** Longest is 7. Sum of others: 3 plus 3 equals 6. Is 7 less than 6? No. So no triangle.

**Child:** And 2, 50, and 50?

**Mother:** Longest is 50. Sum of others: 2 plus 50 equals 52. Is 50 less than 52? Yes. So a triangle exists. It would be a very tall, thin isosceles triangle, but it is valid!

**Child:** What if the sum exactly equals the longest side?

**Mother:** Then the three points lie on a straight line — they are collinear. No triangle forms because the shape collapses into a line. The longest side must be **strictly less** than the sum of the other two.
