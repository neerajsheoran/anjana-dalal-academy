Glance around any room and you will spot lines everywhere — the edges of a window, the ruled lines on a notebook, the metal rods of a gate. Some of these lines cross each other, while others run side by side forever. In this chapter, we will investigate what happens when lines meet (or refuse to meet) and uncover beautiful patterns in the angles they create.

## When Lines Cross

When two lines on a flat surface (a **plane**) meet at a point, they **intersect**. The meeting point is called the **point of intersection**.

![Two intersecting lines forming four angles labelled a, b, c, d at the point of intersection](intersecting-lines-four-angles.png)

Two intersecting lines always carve out **four angles** at the point where they cross.

### Linear Pairs

Two neighbouring angles at an intersection always add up to **180 degrees** because together they form a straight line. Such a pair is called a **linear pair**.

If angle **a** = 110 degrees:
- a + b = 180, so b = 70 degrees
- b + c = 180, so c = 110 degrees
- c + d = 180, so d = 70 degrees

### Vertically Opposite Angles

Angles that sit directly across from each other at the intersection point are called **vertically opposite angles**. In the example above:
- a and c are vertically opposite (both 110 degrees)
- b and d are vertically opposite (both 70 degrees)

> **Key Property:** Vertically opposite angles are **always equal**. We can prove this using the fact that linear pairs sum to 180.

**Proof:** Since a + b = 180 and b + c = 180, we get a = c. Similarly, since a + b = 180 and a + d = 180, we get b = d.

## Perpendicular Lines

When two lines intersect so that **all four angles are equal**, each angle must be **90 degrees** (because 4 x 90 = 360).

Lines meeting at 90 degrees are called **perpendicular lines**. We mark them with a small square at the intersection.

## Lines That Never Meet

Some lines, no matter how far you stretch them in either direction, will **never intersect**. Such lines are called **parallel lines**.

![Parallel lines with arrow marks and perpendicular lines with a square symbol at the intersection](parallel-perpendicular-lines.png)

**Parallel lines** are lines on the same plane that do not cross, no matter how far they are extended.

**Everyday examples of parallel lines:**
- The two rails of a railway track
- Opposite edges of a ruler or a book
- The horizontal lines on ruled notebook paper
- The top and bottom edges of a blackboard

> **Careful:** Two lines must be on the **same plane** to be called parallel. A line drawn on the floor and a line drawn on the wall may never meet, but they are not parallel because they live on different planes.

### How We Mark Them

- Parallel lines carry **arrow marks** (>) on them
- A second pair of parallel lines uses double arrows (>>)
- Perpendicular lines are marked with a **small square** at the crossing point

## Creating Lines by Folding Paper

Paper folding offers a hands-on way to explore parallel and perpendicular lines:

1. A square sheet's **opposite edges** are parallel to each other
2. Its **adjacent edges** are perpendicular to each other
3. Folding horizontally in half produces a crease **parallel** to the top and bottom edges
4. Each additional horizontal fold doubles the number of parallel creases
5. A vertical fold creates a crease **perpendicular** to the horizontal ones

## Transversals

A **transversal** is a line that cuts across two or more lines at separate points.

![Transversal crossing two parallel lines with all 8 angles labelled showing corresponding and alternate angle pairs](transversal-parallel-lines-angles.png)

When a transversal crosses two lines, it produces **8 angles** in total — 4 at each crossing point.

```
     1  2          line p
     4  3
                   transversal t
     5  6          line q
     8  7
```

Since vertically opposite angles give us 4 equal pairs, there are at most **4 distinct angle values** among the 8 angles.

## Corresponding Angles

When a transversal crosses two lines, angles sitting in the **same position** at each crossing are called **corresponding angles**.

The corresponding pairs are:
- Angle 1 and Angle 5
- Angle 2 and Angle 6
- Angle 3 and Angle 7
- Angle 4 and Angle 8

### The Corresponding Angles Property

> **When a transversal crosses parallel lines**, corresponding angles are **always equal**.

The reverse is also true: if corresponding angles formed by a transversal are equal, the two lines **must be** parallel.

If the lines are **not** parallel, their corresponding angles will **never** be equal.

## Alternate Angles

**Alternate angles** are pairs that sit on **opposite sides** of the transversal and **between** the two lines.

In the standard labelling:
- Angle d and Angle f are alternate angles
- Angle c and Angle e are alternate angles

### Proving Alternate Angles Are Equal (for Parallel Lines)

Using facts we already know:
1. Angle f = Angle b (corresponding angles, lines are parallel)
2. Angle b = Angle d (vertically opposite angles)
3. Therefore, Angle f = Angle d

> **Alternate angles formed by a transversal crossing parallel lines are always equal.**

## Determining All Eight Angles from Just One

When a transversal cuts two parallel lines and you know **a single angle**, every other angle can be found.

**Example:** If angle 6 = 140 degrees:

| Angle | Measure | Reason |
|-------|---------|--------|
| Angle 6 | 140° | Given |
| Angle 2 | 140° | Corresponding to 6 |
| Angle 8 | 140° | Vertically opposite to 6 |
| Angle 4 | 140° | Corresponding to 8 |
| Angle 5 | 40° | Linear pair with 6 (180 - 140) |
| Angle 1 | 40° | Corresponding to 5 |
| Angle 7 | 40° | Vertically opposite to 5 |
| Angle 3 | 40° | Corresponding to 7 |

> **Elegant fact:** With parallel lines and a transversal, there are only **two distinct angle measures**, and they always add up to **180 degrees**.

## Testing Whether Lines Are Parallel

To check if two lines are parallel:
1. Draw a transversal cutting both lines
2. Measure the corresponding angles (or alternate angles)
3. If the corresponding angles are **equal**, the lines are parallel
4. If they are **not equal**, the lines are not parallel

**Example:** If one angle at the first crossing is 115° and the corresponding angle at the second crossing is 65°, the lines are **not parallel** (since 115 does not equal 65).

## Constructing Parallel Lines

![Step-by-step diagram of drawing parallel lines using a set square and ruler](drawing-parallel-lines-set-square.png)

### Using a Set Square and Ruler

1. Draw a line **p** using a ruler
2. Place a set square with one edge along line p
3. Hold a ruler against another edge of the set square
4. Slide the set square along the ruler to a new position
5. Draw a second line along the same edge of the set square

The two lines are parallel because the corresponding angles (both 90°) are equal.

### Through Paper Folding

To create a line parallel to line **p** through point **P**:
1. Fold a crease **t** perpendicular to **p** that passes through P
2. Fold another crease **q** perpendicular to **t** through P
3. Lines **p** and **q** are parallel (both are perpendicular to t)

## Co-Interior Angles (Same-Side Interior Angles)

When a transversal intersects two parallel lines, the **co-interior angles** are the angles between the two lines and on the same side of the transversal.

Co-interior angles always sum to **180 degrees**.

## Measurement vs Proof

When you measure angles with a protractor, your readings may not be perfectly precise — a linear pair might not add to exactly 180°, or vertically opposite angles might differ slightly. This happens because:
- Protractors have limited precision
- Drawn lines have thickness (true geometric lines are infinitely thin)

In geometry, we establish properties through **reasoning and proof**, not through measurement alone. A proof guarantees a result is always true; a measurement can only approximate.

## Key Words to Remember

| Word | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Intersecting lines** | Lines that meet at a point on a flat surface |
| **Linear pair** | Two neighbouring angles at an intersection that sum to 180° |
| **Vertically opposite angles** | Angles across from each other at an intersection; always equal |
| **Perpendicular lines** | Lines that cross at exactly 90° |
| **Parallel lines** | Lines on the same plane that never meet, however far extended |
| **Transversal** | A line crossing two or more lines at distinct points |
| **Corresponding angles** | Angles in matching positions at each crossing of a transversal; equal when lines are parallel |
| **Alternate angles** | Angles on opposite sides of the transversal, between the two lines; equal when lines are parallel |
| **Proof** | A logical chain of reasoning showing a mathematical statement is always true |
