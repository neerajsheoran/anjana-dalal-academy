When a tailor measures cloth, a whole number of centimetres often is not precise enough — the fabric might be a little more than 3 metres but not quite 4. To capture such in-between measurements, we need numbers that venture "beyond the point." Welcome to the world of decimals!

## Why Whole Numbers Are Not Always Enough

Imagine you are helping your mother measure a piece of ribbon for a gift. The ribbon is clearly longer than 7 cm but shorter than 8 cm. To pin down the exact length, we divide the gap between 7 and 8 into **10 equal parts**. Each tiny part is **one-tenth** of a centimetre.

![Ruler zoomed in between 2 cm and 3 cm divided into 10 equal parts showing tenths](ruler-tenths-scale.png)

If the ribbon reaches the 4th mark past 7, its length is **7 and 4 tenths cm**, written as 7 4/10 cm.

> **Why always 10 parts?** Because our entire number system is built on powers of ten. Splitting into 10 parts fits perfectly with place value and keeps arithmetic smooth.

## Understanding Tenths

When one whole unit is split into **10 equal pieces**, each piece is called **one-tenth**, written as 1/10.

**Essential relationships:**
- 10 one-tenths = 1 whole unit
- 1/10 + 1/10 + 1/10 + ... (10 times) = 10 x 1/10 = 1

A stick that measures 5 and 3/10 units can also be described as **53 one-tenths**:
- 53 x 1/10 = 53/10 = 50/10 + 3/10 = 5 + 3/10 = 5 3/10

### How to Read Numbers with Tenths

| Number | How to Read |
|--------|------------|
| 6 1/10 | "six and one-tenth" |
| 3/10 | "three-tenths" |
| 52/10 | "fifty-two tenths" |
| 29 1/10 | "twenty-nine and one-tenth" |

### Adding Tenths

Anu's dupatta has two sections — one is 3 5/10 units long and the other is 2 8/10 units long. What is the total length?

**Approach 1:** Add units and tenths separately
- Units: 3 + 2 = 5
- Tenths: 5/10 + 8/10 = 13/10 = 1 unit and 3/10
- Total: 5 + 1 + 3/10 = **6 3/10 units**

**Approach 2:** Convert entirely to tenths
- 35/10 + 28/10 = 63/10 = 6 3/10 units

### Subtracting Tenths

To compute 14 2/10 - 8 5/10:

We cannot take 5 tenths from 2 tenths directly, so we borrow 1 unit (= 10 tenths) from 14:
- 14 2/10 becomes 13 12/10
- 13 12/10 - 8 5/10 = **5 7/10**

This borrowing works identically to borrowing in whole-number subtraction!

## Introducing Hundredths

Sometimes tenths are still not fine enough. If a ribbon measuring 6 3/10 units is folded in half, the folded length falls between 3 1/10 and 3 2/10. To be precise, we divide each tenth into **10 even tinier parts**.

Each of these tiny parts is **one-hundredth**, written as 1/100.

**Essential relationships:**
- 10 one-hundredths = 1 one-tenth
- 100 one-hundredths = 1 whole unit

So a folded ribbon length of **3 units, 1 tenth, and 5 hundredths** can also be expressed as **3 and 15 hundredths**.

### Writing Numbers with Hundredths

A single measurement can be written in three equivalent ways:

| Way | Example |
|-----|---------|
| Units, tenths, and hundredths | 2 units, 3 tenths, 6 hundredths |
| Units and hundredths | 2 and 36 hundredths |
| All hundredths | 236 hundredths |

### Adding Hundredths

To add 12 4/10 7/100 and 3 8/10 5/100:

**Approach 1:** Add units, tenths, and hundredths separately
- Units: 12 + 3 = 15
- Tenths: 4/10 + 8/10 = 12/10 = 1 unit and 2/10
- Hundredths: 7/100 + 5/100 = 12/100 = 1/10 + 2/100
- Carry the extra tenth: 2/10 + 1/10 = 3/10
- Total: 15 + 1 + 3/10 + 2/100 = **16 3/10 2/100**

**Approach 2:** Convert to hundredths
- 1247/100 + 385/100 = 1632/100 = **16 3/10 2/100**

> **The Pattern:** Adding tenths and hundredths follows the exact same carry-over logic as adding whole numbers. When hundredths reach 10, they carry into tenths. When tenths reach 10, they carry into units!

### Subtracting Hundredths

To find 18 6/10 - 7 2/10 9/100:

Rewrite 6/10 as 5/10 and 10/100 (borrow from tenths):
- 18 5/10 10/100 - 7 2/10 9/100 = **11 3/10 1/100**

## The Decimal Place Value System

### Extending Place Value to the Right

In our place value system, each position is **10 times** the one to its right:
- 10 ones = 1 ten
- 10 tens = 1 hundred
- 10 hundreds = 1 thousand

Continuing this pattern **to the right** of the ones place:
- 1 one / 10 = 1 tenth
- 1 tenth / 10 = 1 hundredth
- 1 hundredth / 10 = 1 thousandth

```
... 1000  100  10  1  .  1/10  1/100  1/1000 ...
      |    |   |  |     |      |       |
     x10  x10 x10  .   /10   /10     /10
```

> **Did You Know?** The word "decimal" comes from the Latin "decem" meaning ten. This connects beautifully with the Sanskrit word "dasha," and similar words exist in Hindi (das), Marathi (daha), Gujarati (das), and many other Indian languages.

![Place value table extending from thousands to hundredths with the decimal point highlighted](decimal-place-value-table.png)

### The Decimal Point

To separate the whole-number part from the fractional part, we use a small dot called the **decimal point**.

| Quantity | Decimal Form |
|----------|-------------|
| 4 hundreds and 9 ones | 409 |
| 4 tens and 9 tenths | 40.9 |
| 4 units and 9 hundredths | 4.09 |

The decimal point sits between the **units place** and the **tenths place**.

### Place Value Table

| Hundreds | Tens | Units | . | Tenths | Hundredths |
|----------|------|-------|---|--------|------------|
| 4 | 0 | 9 | | | |
| | 4 | 0 | . | 9 | |
| | | 4 | . | 0 | 9 |

Although 409, 40.9, and 4.09 use the same digits, they are completely different numbers!

### Reading Decimal Numbers

- **5.3** is read as "five point three" or "five and three-tenths"
- **12.68** is read as "twelve point six eight" or "twelve and sixty-eight hundredths"
- **0.07** is read as "zero point zero seven" or "seven hundredths"

### Equivalent Decimals

Adding zeros after the last decimal digit does not alter the value:
- 4.2 = 4.20 = 4.200
- 0.9 = 0.90
- 8.5 = 8.50

But zeros between the decimal point and a digit **do** change the value:
- 4.2 is NOT the same as 4.02

## Computing with Decimals

### Adding Decimals

Line up the decimal points and add column by column, just as you would with whole numbers.

**Example:** 17.23 + 4.59

```
  17.23
+  4.59
-------
  21.82
```

- Hundredths: 3 + 9 = 12 (write 2, carry 1)
- Tenths: 2 + 5 + 1 = 8
- Units: 7 + 4 = 11 (write 1, carry 1)
- Tens: 1 + 1 = 2

### Subtracting Decimals

Align the decimal points and subtract, borrowing when the top digit is smaller.

**Example:** 23.41 - 5.76

```
  23.41
-  5.76
-------
  17.65
```

- Hundredths: 1 - 6 (borrow: 11 - 6 = 5)
- Tenths: 3 - 7 (borrow: 13 - 7 = 6)
- Units: 2 - 5 (borrow: 12 - 5 = 7)
- Tens: 1

## Comparing Decimals

To decide which decimal is larger:
1. Compare the whole-number parts first
2. If equal, compare the tenths digits
3. If still equal, compare the hundredths digits
4. Keep going until you find a difference

**Examples:**
- 4.8 > 4.08 (because 8 tenths > 0 tenths)
- 6.35 < 6.9 (because 3 tenths < 9 tenths)
- 9.70 = 9.7 (trailing zeros do not change value)

## Decimals All Around Us

Decimal numbers pop up everywhere in daily life:
- **Temperature:** A pleasant winter morning in Delhi might be 12.5 degrees Celsius
- **Currency:** A bag of atta at the kirana store costs Rs 325.50
- **Sports:** Neeraj Chopra's Olympic javelin throw was 87.58 metres
- **Cooking:** A recipe might call for 1.5 cups of rice

## Key Words to Remember

| Word | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Decimal** | A number using a decimal point to express values less than one (from Latin "decem" meaning ten) |
| **Decimal point** | The dot (.) separating the whole-number part from the fractional part |
| **Tenth** | One of 10 equal parts of a unit (1/10 = 0.1) |
| **Hundredth** | One of 100 equal parts of a unit (1/100 = 0.01) |
| **Thousandth** | One of 1000 equal parts of a unit (1/1000 = 0.001) |
| **Place value** | The worth of a digit based on where it sits in the number |
| **Equivalent decimals** | Decimals representing the same value, such as 0.6 and 0.60 |
