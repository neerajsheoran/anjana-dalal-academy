## Why Statistics Matters

**Child:** Maa, statistics feels so different from algebra and geometry. It is all about data and charts.

**Mother:** That is exactly what makes it special, beta. Statistics is how mathematics talks to the real world. When the government decides how many new schools to build, when a doctor tests whether a medicine works, when your favourite IPL team picks players -- all of it relies on data analysis. The word "statistics" comes from the Latin *status*, meaning "state," because kings and emperors were the first to count their people and resources.

**Child:** India does that too, right? The census?

**Mother:** Yes. The Census of India happens every ten years -- it is one of the biggest data collection exercises on Earth. The 2011 census recorded information on more than 121 crore people: their ages, languages, occupations, education levels, and much more. Organising and making sense of that mountain of numbers is pure statistics -- the very skills you are building in this chapter.

## Sorting Data into Tables and Graphs

**Child:** Maa, what is the difference between primary and secondary data?

**Mother:** Primary data is what you collect yourself for a particular question -- like asking every student in your class how many siblings they have. Secondary data is information someone else already gathered -- government reports, published research, website databases. For your board exam, you mostly work with given data, but knowing the difference matters for projects and competitive exams.

**Child:** When do I use grouped versus ungrouped frequency tables?

**Mother:** If the values repeat and there are not too many distinct numbers, list each value with its count -- that is an ungrouped table. But when values are spread over a wide range with many different numbers, bundle them into class intervals. For instance, test scores from 0 to 100 could be grouped as 0--10, 10--20, and so on. The class mark (midpoint) represents all values in that interval.

**Child:** How is a histogram different from a bar graph?

**Mother:** A bar graph has **gaps** between the bars -- it is for discrete categories like "number of students per house." A histogram has **no gaps** -- the bars touch because the data is continuous and each interval leads directly into the next. Also, if class widths are not all equal, you must use **frequency density** (frequency divided by class width) for the bar height. This ensures the area of each bar correctly represents the frequency.

**Child:** And frequency polygons?

**Mother:** Plot each class mark against its frequency, join the dots with straight lines, and close the shape by extending to imaginary classes with zero frequency at both ends. The big advantage of frequency polygons is that you can overlay two of them on the same graph to compare distributions -- something that is messy with histograms.

## Mean, Median, and Mode

**Child:** When should I use mean, and when median or mode?

**Mother:** Each has its strength, beta. The **mean** accounts for every data point, so it gives a comprehensive average -- but it gets distorted by extreme values. If five friends have pocket money of Rs 50, Rs 60, Rs 55, Rs 65, and Rs 500, the mean is Rs 146, which does not represent anyone's typical pocket money. The **median** (Rs 60 in this case) ignores the extremes and shows the "middle" experience. The **mode** tells you the most common value -- it is the only measure that works for non-numeric data like "favourite festival" or "preferred language."

**Child:** Can you give another Indian example?

**Mother:** Think about house prices in a neighbourhood. A few luxury bungalows can push the mean price sky-high, making the area seem more expensive than it really is for most families. The median price gives a truer picture of what a typical buyer would pay. Economists in India regularly report median household income alongside mean income for exactly this reason.

**Child:** What formulas must I know for the exam?

**Mother:** For ungrouped data -- Mean = sum of all values divided by the count. For a frequency distribution -- Mean = sum of (frequency x value) divided by total frequency. Median for odd n: the ((n+1)/2)th value after sorting. Median for even n: the average of the (n/2)th and (n/2 + 1)th values. Mode: whichever value appears the most. These are straightforward, but careless arithmetic is the biggest mark-killer -- always double-check your sums.

## Graph Drawing and Exam Tips

**Child:** Any tips for drawing graphs in the exam, Maa?

**Mother:** Five rules. First, use a ruler and a sharp pencil -- neat graphs earn marks. Second, label both axes clearly: the x-axis shows the variable (class intervals or categories), the y-axis shows frequency. Third, write the scale you are using. Fourth, give the graph a title. Fifth, for histograms, shade or cross-hatch the bars, and for frequency polygons, mark the plotted points clearly before joining them.

**Child:** What types of questions come most often?

**Mother:** Drawing or interpreting histograms (especially with unequal class widths), constructing frequency polygons, calculating mean from a frequency table, and finding median and mode from listed data. Increasingly, boards are asking application-based questions where you must interpret data from a real-life scenario -- rainfall patterns, class test scores, traffic data.

**Child:** How does this chapter connect to what we learn later?

**Mother:** In Class 10, you will compute mean, median, and mode for grouped data with more advanced formulas and draw cumulative frequency curves called **ogives**. In Classes 11 and 12, statistics grows into probability, standard deviation, and correlation. Data science careers -- which are among the highest-paying globally -- all grow from these foundations. The great Indian statistician **P.C. Mahalanobis** founded the Indian Statistical Institute and developed the Mahalanobis distance, a concept used worldwide in machine learning today.
