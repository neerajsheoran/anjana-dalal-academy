## Introduction

Look around any room and you will see lines meeting at corners, edges running parallel to one another, and angles of every size. The geometry of lines and angles governs how walls meet floors, how roads intersect at junctions, and how railway tracks remain evenly spaced. In this chapter, we examine the relationships between angles formed when lines cross each other, and the special properties that emerge when a transversal cuts through parallel lines.

---

## Basic Terms and Definitions

A **line segment** is the portion of a line bounded by two endpoints. A **ray** begins at one endpoint and stretches endlessly in one direction. A **line** has no endpoints and extends without bound in both directions.

When three or more points lie on a single line, they are called **collinear points**. Points that do not share a common line are **non-collinear points**.

### Classifying Angles

| Category           | Range of Measure               |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| **Acute angle**    | Greater than 0 and less than 90 degrees    |
| **Right angle**    | Exactly 90 degrees             |
| **Obtuse angle**   | Greater than 90 and less than 180 degrees  |
| **Straight angle** | Exactly 180 degrees            |
| **Reflex angle**   | Greater than 180 and less than 360 degrees |

### Common Angle Pairs

* **Complementary angles:** A pair of angles that together measure **90 degrees**.
* **Supplementary angles:** A pair of angles that together measure **180 degrees**.
* **Adjacent angles:** Two angles sharing a common vertex and a common arm, with their other arms on opposite sides of the shared arm.
* **Linear pair:** Adjacent angles whose non-shared arms together form a straight line (their measures add to 180 degrees).
* **Vertically opposite angles:** The pairs of non-adjacent angles created when two straight lines cross.

## Intersecting and Non-intersecting Lines

When two lines in a plane share a common point, they **intersect**. When they never share a point regardless of how far they are extended, they are **parallel**. Parallel lines maintain a constant perpendicular separation everywhere; this separation is called the **distance between two parallel lines**.

---

## The Linear Pair Axiom

> **Axiom 6.1:** If a ray stands on a line, the two adjacent angles created on either side add up to **180 degrees**.

Such a pair of angles is called a **linear pair**.

> **Axiom 6.2 (Converse):** If two adjacent angles sum to 180 degrees, the arms that are not shared form a straight line.

These two statements together constitute the **Linear Pair Axiom**.

**Illustration:** Suppose ray OC rises from line AB. Then angle AOC + angle COB = 180 degrees.

> **Key idea:** When several rays emerge from a single point, the angles around that point always total **360 degrees**.

---

## Vertically Opposite Angles

**Theorem 6.1:** When two lines intersect, each pair of **vertically opposite angles is equal**.

**Proof:** Let lines PQ and RS cross at point O.

Ray OP stands on line RS, so angle POR + angle POS = 180 degrees ... (1)

Ray OS stands on line PQ, so angle POS + angle QOS = 180 degrees ... (2)

From (1) and (2): angle POR + angle POS = angle POS + angle QOS

Subtracting angle POS from both sides gives **angle POR = angle QOS**.

By similar reasoning, **angle POS = angle QOR**.

**Illustration:** Lines JK and LM cross at O with angle JOL : angle LOK = 4 : 5. Find every angle.

angle JOL + angle LOK = 180 degrees (linear pair)

angle JOL = (4/9) x 180 = 80 degrees

angle LOK = (5/9) x 180 = 100 degrees

angle JOM = angle LOK = 100 degrees (vertically opposite)

angle MOK = angle JOL = 80 degrees (vertically opposite)

## Parallel Lines and a Transversal

A **transversal** is a line that cuts across two or more lines at separate points. When a transversal crosses two parallel lines, **eight angles** are formed. These angles fall into recognisable families:

### Families of angle pairs

1. **Corresponding angles:** One angle sits at each intersection in the same relative position (for example, both in the upper-right corner). They resemble the letter F.
2. **Alternate interior angles:** Both lie between the parallel lines but on opposite sides of the transversal. They trace a Z or S shape.
3. **Alternate exterior angles:** Both lie outside the parallel lines on opposite sides of the transversal.
4. **Co-interior angles (same-side interior angles):** Both lie between the parallel lines on the same side of the transversal. They form a C or U shape.

![Transversal cutting two parallel lines showing corresponding angles, alternate interior angles, and co-interior angles labeled](parallel-lines-transversal-angles.png)

### Key Axioms and Theorems

> **Axiom 6.3 (Corresponding Angles Axiom):** When a transversal intersects two parallel lines, every pair of **corresponding angles is equal**.

> **Axiom 6.4 (Converse):** If a transversal crosses two lines and produces a pair of equal corresponding angles, those two lines are **parallel**.

**Theorem 6.2:** When a transversal crosses two parallel lines, every pair of **alternate interior angles is equal**.

**Theorem 6.3:** When a transversal crosses two parallel lines, every pair of **co-interior angles is supplementary** (sums to 180 degrees).

**Theorem 6.4 (Converse of 6.2):** If alternate interior angles are equal, the two lines are parallel.

**Theorem 6.5 (Converse of 6.3):** If co-interior angles are supplementary, the two lines are parallel.

---

## Lines Parallel to the Same Line

**Theorem 6.6:** If two lines are each parallel to a third line, they are **parallel to each other**.

Suppose line p is parallel to line l, and line q is also parallel to line l. Draw any transversal t across all three. Because p || l, corresponding angles with the transversal are equal: angle 1 = angle 2. Because q || l, corresponding angles are equal: angle 1 = angle 3. Hence angle 2 = angle 3, and by the converse of the Corresponding Angles Axiom, p || q.

## Angle Sum Property of a Triangle

**Theorem 6.7:** The three interior angles of any triangle add up to **180 degrees**.

**Proof sketch:** Through one vertex of the triangle, draw a line parallel to the opposite side. The alternate interior angles created by this construction correspond exactly to the other two angles of the triangle. Together with the angle at the chosen vertex, they form a straight angle of 180 degrees.

![Proof diagram for the angle sum property of a triangle showing a line through vertex A parallel to BC with alternate angles marked](triangle-angle-sum-proof.png)

**Theorem 6.8:** When one side of a triangle is extended, the **exterior angle** formed equals the **sum of the two non-adjacent interior angles**.

**Illustration:** In triangle DEF, angle D = 40 degrees and angle E = 55 degrees. Then angle F = 180 - 40 - 55 = 85 degrees. If side EF is extended to G, the exterior angle DFG = angle D + angle E = 40 + 55 = 95 degrees.

![Triangle ABC with side BC extended to D, showing the exterior angle ACD equal to the sum of the two non-adjacent interior angles](exterior-angle-theorem.png)

> **Key idea:** Every exterior angle of a triangle exceeds each of the two non-adjacent interior angles individually.

---

## Summary

1. When a ray stands on a line, the two adjacent angles total **180 degrees** (Linear Pair Axiom).
2. **Vertically opposite angles** at an intersection are always equal.
3. For parallel lines cut by a transversal: corresponding angles are equal, alternate interior angles are equal, and co-interior angles sum to 180 degrees.
4. Two lines each parallel to a third line are parallel to each other.
5. The interior angles of a triangle sum to **180 degrees**.
6. An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles.
