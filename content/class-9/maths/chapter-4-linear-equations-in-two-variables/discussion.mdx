## From One Variable to Two

**Child:** Maa, in Class 8 we solved equations like 3x plus 5 equals 11, which gave one answer. Now we have 3x plus 4y equals 24, and the textbook says there are infinitely many solutions. How can one equation have unlimited answers?

**Mother:** Think of it this way, beta. With one variable, you are searching for a single point on a number line -- there is usually just one spot. But with two variables, you are searching for points on an entire flat surface -- the Cartesian plane. The solutions form a complete straight line, and a line contains infinitely many points. Every single point on that line makes the equation true.

**Child:** So each solution is an ordered pair (x, y)?

**Mother:** Exactly. Take 3x plus 4y equals 24. The pair (0, 6) works because 0 plus 24 equals 24. The pair (8, 0) works because 24 plus 0 equals 24. The pair (4, 3) works because 12 plus 12 equals 24. You can generate as many solutions as you want by picking any value for x and computing y, or the other way around.

**Child:** This connects beautifully with coordinate geometry from the last chapter!

**Mother:** That is the real magic. Algebra gives you the equation; geometry gives you the picture. They are two ways of expressing the same mathematical truth. This connection between equations and graphs, first made systematic by Descartes, is the foundation of all higher mathematics.

## Drawing Graphs of Linear Equations

**Child:** To draw the graph, we need at least two points. Why not just one, Maa?

**Mother:** Because through a single point, infinitely many different lines can pass -- you would not know which one to draw. But two points uniquely determine one straight line. In practice, I recommend finding three points: two to draw the line and a third to double-check. If the third point does not fall on your line, you know there is an arithmetic mistake somewhere.

**Child:** What do the special cases x equals a and y equals b look like on the graph?

**Mother:** The equation x equals 5 gives a vertical line passing through (5, 0), running parallel to the y-axis. Every point on this line has x-coordinate 5, no matter what y is. Similarly, y equals minus 3 is a horizontal line through (0, -3), parallel to the x-axis. A common exam mistake is drawing x equals 5 as a single dot instead of a full vertical line -- remember, it represents infinitely many points!

![Graph showing x = 3 as a vertical line and y = -2 as a horizontal line on the coordinate plane](vertical-horizontal-lines-graph.png)

**Child:** And y equals mx always goes through the origin?

**Mother:** Yes. The equation y equals 3x passes through (0, 0), (1, 3), (2, 6). This form represents direct proportionality -- y increases in exact proportion to x. In physics, if you hold the current constant, the relationship between voltage and resistance follows exactly this pattern -- that is Ohm's law, V equals IR.

## Real-World Applications

**Child:** Where do we see linear equations in two variables in real life, Maa?

**Mother:** They are everywhere. When a sabzi wala says "3 kg of tomatoes and 2 kg of onions cost Rs 150," that is the equation 3x plus 2y equals 150, where x is the price per kg of tomatoes and y is the price per kg of onions. When a civil engineer designs a wheelchair ramp, its slope follows a linear equation. Even the fare meter of an auto-rickshaw in Bangalore or Mumbai -- a fixed starting charge plus a per-km rate -- follows the form y equals mx plus c.

**Child:** But with just one equation and two unknowns, we cannot pin down the exact price of tomatoes and onions, can we?

**Mother:** Correct -- one equation gives you a line of possibilities, not a unique answer. To find exact values, you need two independent equations. In Class 10, you will study systems of two linear equations -- two lines on the plane that intersect at a single point, giving you the one solution common to both equations.

## Lines Parallel to the Axes

**Child:** Maa, why does x equals 4 represent a whole line and not just the number 4?

**Mother:** On a number line -- which is one-dimensional -- x equals 4 is indeed a single point. But on the Cartesian plane -- which is two-dimensional -- x equals 4 means "x is 4 and y can be anything at all." You can rewrite it as 1 times x plus 0 times y minus 4 equals 0. Since y's coefficient is 0, y is free. So you get the points (4, 0), (4, 1), (4, -5), (4, 100) -- all forming a vertical line.

**Child:** So y equals 0 is the x-axis itself?

**Mother:** Yes! And x equals 0 is the y-axis. The axes are themselves graphs of linear equations. For board exams, remember that an equation like 4x plus 8 equals 0 can be rewritten in two-variable form as 4x plus 0y plus 8 equals 0, which is a vertical line at x equals minus 2.

**Child:** Any final exam tips for this chapter, Maa?

**Mother:** Three things. First, always present your solutions in a neat table format -- it helps you stay organised and impresses examiners. Second, verify each solution by substituting back into the original equation. Third, when drawing graphs, label the axes, mark the scale clearly, write the equation beside the line, and extend the line with arrows to show it continues infinitely. These presentation habits prevent silly mark losses. The ancient Indian mathematician Aryabhata used systematic substitution methods for solving equations over 1500 years ago -- careful, methodical work has always been the key to mathematical success.

## Preparing for What Comes Next

**Child:** What will we study next that builds on this chapter?

**Mother:** In Class 10, you will learn to solve pairs of linear equations simultaneously -- using graphical methods, substitution, and elimination. The graphical intuition you are building now will make those methods much easier to understand. You will see how two lines on a plane can intersect at a point (one unique solution), be parallel (no solution), or overlap completely (infinitely many solutions). It is a beautiful topic.

**Child:** So this chapter is the foundation for all of that?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Right now you are learning to work with one equation and its line. Soon you will work with two equations and two lines. And later, in higher classes, you will deal with systems of many equations in many variables -- the mathematics that powers everything from weather prediction to machine learning. It all starts here, with a single straight line on the Cartesian plane.
