## Understanding Polynomials

**Child:** Maa, my friend wrote x plus 1/x in a test and our teacher marked it wrong, saying it is not a polynomial. Why not?

**Mother:** A polynomial is like a disciplined recipe -- every term must have the variable raised to a whole number power: 0, 1, 2, 3, and so on. The expression 1/x is the same as x to the power minus 1, and minus 1 is not a whole number. Similarly, sqrt(x) means x to the power 1/2, which is also not allowed. Think of polynomials as the "obedient children" of the algebra family -- they follow strict rules about exponents.

**Child:** So the degree is simply the biggest power?

**Mother:** Exactly. Degree 1 means linear, degree 2 means quadratic, degree 3 means cubic. Here is a subtle point that examiners love: a non-zero constant like 11 has degree zero, because 11 is the same as 11 times x to the power 0. But the zero polynomial -- just the number 0 -- has no defined degree. That is a classic trap in board exams.

**Child:** And what about naming them by the number of terms?

**Mother:** One term is a monomial, two terms make a binomial, three terms give a trinomial. Beyond three, we simply say "polynomial." In the real world, polynomials describe the curved path of a cricket ball through the air, the shape of a suspension bridge cable, or even the trajectory of an ISRO rocket. The degree tells you how many times the curve can change direction.

## Zeroes of a Polynomial

**Child:** Maa, finding zeroes of a linear polynomial is easy -- just solve ax plus b equals zero. But what about quadratics and cubics?

**Mother:** For a linear polynomial ax plus b, there is exactly one zero: minus b divided by a. A quadratic can have up to 2 zeroes, a cubic up to 3. The beautiful insight is that a polynomial of degree n has at most n zeroes. Each zero corresponds to a point where the polynomial's graph touches or crosses the horizontal axis.

**Child:** So when a question asks "verify whether 3 is a zero of p(x) equals x-squared minus 9," I just substitute?

**Mother:** Yes, beta. p(3) equals 9 minus 9 equals 0. Since the result is zero, yes, 3 is a zero. Always write out the substitution step clearly in exams -- students lose marks by jumping straight to the answer without showing the work.

## Remainder Theorem and Factor Theorem

**Child:** The Remainder Theorem says that dividing p(x) by (x minus a) gives a remainder equal to p(a). Can you explain why, Maa?

**Mother:** When you divide p(x) by (x minus a), the result is p(x) equals (x minus a) times some quotient q(x) plus a remainder r. Now put x equal to a in this equation: p(a) equals (a minus a) times q(a) plus r. The first part vanishes because a minus a is zero. So p(a) equals r. The remainder is just the polynomial evaluated at x equals a. It is so elegant!

**Child:** And the Factor Theorem is the special case when the remainder is zero?

**Mother:** Spot on. If p(a) equals 0, the remainder is zero, meaning (x minus a) divides p(x) perfectly -- so (x minus a) is a factor. This is your most powerful tool for breaking down cubic polynomials. Just test small integer values like 1, minus 1, 2, minus 2 until you find one that gives zero.

**Child:** Should I always start with x equals 1 or minus 1 in exams?

**Mother:** Yes, because the arithmetic is simplest. If neither works, try 2, minus 2, then 3, minus 3. A good shortcut is to try factors of the constant term. For instance, if the polynomial ends with the number 6, try plus or minus 1, 2, 3, and 6. This approach almost always finds a zero quickly for board exam problems.

## Factorisation Techniques

**Child:** Splitting the middle term makes sense in theory, but sometimes I struggle to find the right pair of numbers, Maa.

**Mother:** Let me give you a systematic method for ax-squared plus bx plus c. You need two numbers whose sum is b and whose product is a times c. List the factor pairs of a times c and check which pair adds up to b. For example, to factorise 10x-squared plus 11x plus 3: a times c equals 30. Factor pairs of 30 include (5, 6), and 5 plus 6 equals 11. So split 11x into 5x plus 6x, then group and factor. With practice, you will spot the right pair almost instantly.

**Child:** For cubics, I first find one factor using the Factor Theorem, then deal with the remaining quadratic?

**Mother:** Exactly. Once you identify (x minus a) as a factor, divide p(x) by (x minus a) to get a quadratic. Then factorise that quadratic by splitting the middle term. This two-step strategy handles every cubic polynomial you will encounter in your board examination.

## Algebraic Identities

**Child:** Maa, there are so many identities now -- the old ones like (a plus b)-squared, and new ones like (a plus b)-cubed and a-cubed plus b-cubed plus c-cubed minus 3abc. How do I keep them all straight?

**Mother:** Organise them in your mind like shelves. The "square shelf" has three identities involving (x plus y)-squared, (x minus y)-squared, and the difference of squares. The "cube shelf" has (x plus y)-cubed and (x minus y)-cubed. Then the "power identity" shelf has the three-variable expression: x-cubed plus y-cubed plus z-cubed minus 3xyz equals (x plus y plus z) times (x-squared plus y-squared plus z-squared minus xy minus yz minus zx).

**Child:** And the magical shortcut when x plus y plus z equals zero?

**Mother:** Yes! When x plus y plus z equals zero, the entire right side has (x plus y plus z) as a factor, which is zero. So x-cubed plus y-cubed plus z-cubed equals 3xyz. This is a favourite in competitive exams. For example, calculate (-15)-cubed plus 8-cubed plus 7-cubed. Since minus 15 plus 8 plus 7 equals 0, the answer is 3 times (-15) times 8 times 7 equals minus 2520. No need to compute each cube separately!

**Child:** These identities also help with quick mental calculations, right? Like computing 98-cubed?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Write 98 as (100 minus 2) and use the cube identity. In JEE preparation and olympiad training, this habit of rewriting numbers in convenient forms saves tremendous time. The legendary mathematician Srinivasa Ramanujan filled entire notebooks with such identities -- mathematicians around the world are still exploring his work today.
