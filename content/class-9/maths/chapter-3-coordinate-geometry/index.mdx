## Introduction

Imagine you are at a large wedding hall in Delhi and someone tells you, "Aunty is sitting in the hall." That is not enough to find her -- you need to know which row and which seat. In the same way, describing the location of a point on a flat surface requires **two** pieces of information. This simple yet powerful idea forms the basis of **Coordinate Geometry**, a branch of mathematics pioneered by the French philosopher-mathematician **Rene Descartes** (1596-1650), who showed how algebra and geometry could speak the same language.

> **Key idea:** To pinpoint the position of any point on a flat surface, we need two perpendicular reference lines and the distances of the point from each of them.

---

## The Cartesian System

The **Cartesian coordinate system** (named in honour of Descartes) is built by drawing two perpendicular number lines on a flat surface.

### The axes

* The **horizontal** number line is the **x-axis** (also written as XX').
* The **vertical** number line is the **y-axis** (also written as YY').
* Their meeting point is the **origin**, labelled **O**.

On the x-axis, positive values extend to the right, and on the y-axis, positive values extend upward.

![Cartesian plane showing x-axis, y-axis, origin O, and the four quadrants labeled I, II, III, IV with sign conventions](cartesian-plane-quadrants.png)

### The four quadrants

The two axes carve the plane into **four quadrants**, numbered I through IV in the anticlockwise direction starting from the upper-right region:

| Quadrant | x-coordinate | y-coordinate | Sign Pattern |
| -------- | ------------ | ------------ | ------------ |
| **I**    | Positive     | Positive     | (+, +)       |
| **II**   | Negative     | Positive     | (-, +)       |
| **III**  | Negative     | Negative     | (-, -)       |
| **IV**   | Positive     | Negative     | (+, -)       |

> **Key idea:** The entire flat surface containing the axes and the four quadrants is called the **Cartesian plane**, the **coordinate plane**, or the **xy-plane**.

---

## Coordinates of a Point

Every point on the Cartesian plane is identified by an **ordered pair (x, y)** where:

* The **x-coordinate** (also called the **abscissa**) measures how far the point is from the y-axis, along the horizontal direction. It is positive to the right and negative to the left.
* The **y-coordinate** (also called the **ordinate**) measures how far the point is from the x-axis, along the vertical direction. It is positive upward and negative downward.

**Convention:** The x-coordinate always comes **first**. So (2, 7) is a different point from (7, 2), unless both coordinates are equal.

### Special points

* **Origin:** Its coordinates are **(0, 0)**.
* **Points on the x-axis:** These have coordinates of the form **(x, 0)** -- the y-coordinate is always zero.
* **Points on the y-axis:** These have coordinates of the form **(0, y)** -- the x-coordinate is always zero.

**Example:** A point R is 5 units to the right of the y-axis and 2 units above the x-axis. Then R = **(5, 2)**.

**Example:** A point S is 3 units to the left of the y-axis and 7 units below the x-axis. Then S = **(-3, -7)**, which lies in Quadrant III.

## Plotting Points on the Cartesian Plane

To plot a point (a, b) on the Cartesian plane:

1. Begin at the origin O.
2. Walk **a units** along the x-axis (rightward if a > 0, leftward if a < 0).
3. From there, walk **b units** parallel to the y-axis (upward if b > 0, downward if b < 0).
4. Mark the point.

**Example:** Plot the point (-4, 5).

![Step-by-step plotting of point (-3, 4) showing horizontal movement left then vertical movement up from the origin](plotting-point-step-by-step.png)

* Start at O.
* Move 4 units to the left along the x-axis.
* Move 5 units upward parallel to the y-axis.
* This point sits in **Quadrant II**.

**Example:** Plot the point (7, 0).

* Start at O.
* Move 7 units to the right along the x-axis.
* Since the y-coordinate is 0, the point lies on the x-axis itself.

## Reading Coordinates from a Graph

To determine the coordinates of a point shown on a graph:

1. Drop a perpendicular from the point to the x-axis. Where it meets the x-axis gives the **x-coordinate**.
2. Drop a perpendicular from the point to the y-axis. Where it meets the y-axis gives the **y-coordinate**.
3. Write the result as (x, y).

**Example:** If a point M is 6 units to the right of the y-axis and 5 units above the x-axis, then M = (6, 5), lying in Quadrant I.

**Example:** If a point N is 2 units to the left of the y-axis and 8 units below the x-axis, then N = (-2, -8), lying in Quadrant III.

---

## Summary

1. Locating a point in a plane requires two perpendicular reference lines.
2. The horizontal reference is the **x-axis** and the vertical is the **y-axis**; together they form the **coordinate axes**.
3. The axes create four **quadrants**: I (+, +), II (-, +), III (-, -), IV (+, -).
4. The axes meet at the **origin**, whose coordinates are **(0, 0)**.
5. The **x-coordinate** (abscissa) tells the horizontal distance from the y-axis; the **y-coordinate** (ordinate) tells the vertical distance from the x-axis.
6. A point's location is written as **(x, y)**, with x always first.
7. Points on the x-axis have the form (x, 0); points on the y-axis have the form (0, y).
8. When x is not equal to y, the points (x, y) and (y, x) are different.
