## Introduction

Have you ever wondered why buildings stand straight, why railway tracks never converge, or why a rangoli pattern looks perfectly balanced? Behind all these everyday observations lies the discipline of **geometry** -- the science of shapes, sizes, and spatial relationships.

Thousands of years ago, civilisations across the globe developed geometric knowledge out of necessity. In ancient India, scholars composed the **Sulbasutras** (roughly 800-500 BCE), which contained detailed rules for constructing sacred fire altars with exact measurements. Baudhayana's Sulbasutra, for instance, describes a relationship between the sides of a right triangle that predates the Greek formulation by centuries. Egyptian surveyors, meanwhile, devised methods to re-establish farm boundaries after the annual Nile floods washed away old markers. Chinese and Babylonian mathematicians independently catalogued their own geometric results.

Despite this rich global heritage, the approach was largely recipe-based -- individual problems solved with individual methods. It was **Euclid** (approximately 325-265 BCE), a mathematician working in Alexandria, who changed everything. He gathered the scattered geometric knowledge of his era and organised it into a single, logically connected framework called the **Elements** -- a set of 13 volumes that became one of the most influential works in the history of science.

> **Key idea:** What made Euclid revolutionary was not the discovery of new shapes, but the insistence that every geometric truth must be traced back to a handful of starting assumptions through pure logical reasoning.

## Euclid's Definitions

Euclid opened his first volume with **23 definitions** meant to clarify the basic objects of geometry. Among the most notable:

1. **A point** is that which has no part.
2. **A line** is breadthless length.
3. The ends of a line are points.
4. **A straight line** is a line which lies evenly with the points on itself.
5. **A surface** is that which has length and breadth only.
6. The edges of a surface are lines.
7. **A plane surface** is a surface which lies evenly with the straight lines on itself.

Consider how dimensions decrease as we move from one object to another:

![Diagram showing the progression from a 3D solid to a 2D surface to a 1D line to a 0D point](solid-surface-line-point-dimensions.png)

* A **solid** (like a brick) occupies 3 dimensions -- length, breadth, and height
* A **surface** (like a sheet of paper) has 2 dimensions -- length and breadth
* A **line** (like a taut thread) has only 1 dimension -- length
* A **point** (like a needle tip) has 0 dimensions

> **Key idea:** Modern mathematics treats "point", "line", and "plane" as **undefined terms**. Attempting to define them precisely leads to a never-ending chain where each new definition requires another. Instead, we accept them based on shared intuitive understanding.

## Euclid's Axioms

**Axioms** (also called common notions) are self-evident truths so fundamental that they apply across all branches of mathematics, not just geometry. Euclid listed seven:

1. **Things which are equal to the same thing are equal to one another.** (If Priya's height equals Rahul's height, and Rahul's height equals Meera's height, then Priya and Meera are equally tall.)
2. **If equals are added to equals, the wholes are equal.** (If two shopkeepers each have 500 rupees and both receive 200 rupees more, they each now have 700 rupees.)
3. **If equals are subtracted from equals, the remainders are equal.** (If two jars each contain 3 litres and you pour out 1 litre from each, both jars hold 2 litres.)
4. **Things which coincide with one another are equal to one another.** (If one shape fits perfectly on top of another, they are equal -- the principle of superposition.)
5. **The whole is greater than the part.**
6. **Things which are double of the same things are equal to one another.**
7. **Things which are halves of the same things are equal to one another.**

## Euclid's Postulates

While axioms are universal, **postulates** are assumptions specific to geometry. Euclid stated five:

**Postulate 1:** A straight line may be drawn from any one point to any other point.

This tells us that connecting any two locations with a straight path is always possible. In modern language:

> **Key idea (Axiom 5.1):** Given two distinct points, there exists a **unique** straight line passing through both.

**Postulate 2:** A terminated line can be produced indefinitely.

A "terminated line" is what we now call a **line segment**. This postulate guarantees that any segment can be stretched in either direction without limit to form a complete line.

**Postulate 3:** A circle can be drawn with any centre and any radius.

**Postulate 4:** All right angles are equal to one another.

**Postulate 5:** If a straight line falling on two straight lines makes the interior angles on the same side of it taken together less than two right angles (less than 180 degrees), then the two straight lines, if produced indefinitely, meet on that side on which the sum of angles is less than 180 degrees.

![Diagram illustrating Euclid's fifth postulate showing a transversal cutting two lines with interior angles summing to less than 180 degrees](euclids-fifth-postulate-diagram.png)

> **Key idea:** The fifth postulate is the most intricate. Its essence concerns parallel lines -- when the interior angles on one side add up to exactly 180 degrees, the two lines will never meet, making them parallel.

## Theorems Derived from Euclid's Framework

Starting from his definitions, axioms, and postulates, Euclid methodically proved 465 propositions (theorems) using nothing but logical deduction.

### Illustration: Showing that AB + BC = AC

Suppose three points A, B, and C sit on a line with B lying between A and C. The segment AC perfectly overlaps with the combined segments AB and BC. By Axiom 4 (things that coincide are equal), we conclude **AB + BC = AC**.

### Illustration: Building an equilateral triangle on a given segment

Given a line segment PQ:

1. Draw a circle centred at P with radius PQ (Postulate 3).
2. Draw another circle centred at Q with radius QP (Postulate 3).
3. Let the two circles intersect at a point R.
4. Now PR = PQ (both are radii of the first circle) and QR = PQ (both are radii of the second circle). By Axiom 1 (things equal to the same thing are equal to one another), PR = QR = PQ. Hence triangle PQR is equilateral.

![Construction of an equilateral triangle using two intersecting circles centered at the endpoints of a line segment](equilateral-triangle-construction.png)

### Theorem 5.1: Two distinct lines cannot share more than one common point.

**Proof:** Assume, for the sake of argument, that two distinct lines share two common points M and N. That would mean two different lines pass through the same pair of points M and N, violating Axiom 5.1 (a unique line passes through two distinct points). This contradiction proves that two distinct lines can have **at most one** point in common.

## Important Results

* **If a point M lies between P and Q such that PM = MQ**, then PM = PQ/2. The point M is called the **midpoint** of PQ.
* **Every line segment possesses exactly one midpoint.** (This can be demonstrated using Euclid's axioms.)
* **If on a line segment WXYZ we have WY = XZ**, then WX = YZ. (This follows from Axiom 3: subtracting equal quantities from equal quantities yields equal remainders.)

## Summary

1. **Undefined terms:** In modern geometry, point, line, and plane are accepted without formal definition.
2. **Axioms and postulates** are foundational assumptions accepted without proof; axioms apply broadly, while postulates are geometry-specific.
3. **Theorems** are statements established through logical deduction from definitions, axioms, and postulates.
4. Euclid's axioms address properties like transitivity of equality, the effect of adding or subtracting equals, and the relationship of parts to wholes.
5. Euclid's five postulates govern the drawing of lines and circles, the equality of right angles, and the behaviour of lines cut by a transversal (the parallel postulate).
6. Two distinct lines can share at most one point in common.
