## The Number Family

**Child:** Maa, why are there so many types of numbers? Natural numbers, whole numbers, integers, rational numbers... it is confusing! Can we not just say "numbers"?

**Mother:** Think of it like our family, beta. You have your immediate family, then your extended family, then your entire community. Each circle is bigger than the last. Numbers work the same way. Natural numbers are the smallest circle -- just 1, 2, 3, and so on. Add zero, and you get whole numbers. Include negatives, and you reach integers. Allow fractions, and you arrive at rational numbers.

**Child:** But why did we need to keep expanding?

**Mother:** Because each set had a limitation. Imagine you have 5 rotis and eat all 5 -- natural numbers cannot express what is left. That is why zero was invented, and India's contribution of zero changed mathematics forever. Then think about temperature in Shimla dropping below zero -- you need negative numbers. Or dividing Rs 100 equally among 3 friends -- you need fractions.

**Child:** So rational numbers cover everything?

**Mother:** That is what ancient mathematicians believed until someone discovered that the diagonal of a perfect square cannot be written as any fraction. That discovery opened the door to irrational numbers!

## Irrational Numbers and the Number Line

**Child:** Maa, our teacher said sqrt(2) is irrational. How do we know for certain that nobody will ever find a fraction equal to it?

**Mother:** There is a beautiful proof by contradiction. Suppose sqrt(2) equals some fraction p/q in its simplest form -- meaning p and q share no common factor. Square both sides: 2 equals p-squared divided by q-squared. So p-squared equals 2 times q-squared. This tells us p-squared is even, which means p itself must be even. Write p as 2k. Then 4k-squared equals 2q-squared, so q-squared equals 2k-squared, making q even too. But wait -- we said p and q have no common factor! Both being even is a contradiction. So our assumption was wrong, and sqrt(2) cannot be rational.

**Child:** That is so clever! Can we actually show sqrt(2) on the number line?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Draw a square with sides of 1 unit, with one corner at zero on the number line. The diagonal of this square has length sqrt(2) -- that comes directly from the Pythagoras theorem. Now take a compass, put the point at zero, open it to the length of the diagonal, and draw an arc onto the number line. Where the arc touches is exactly sqrt(2). The ancient Indian scholars who wrote the Sulbasutras knew this construction over 2800 years ago -- they used it when building sacred fire altars with precise measurements.

![Unit square placed on the number line with a compass arc transferring the diagonal length to mark sqrt(2)](sqrt2-compass-construction.png)

**Child:** What mistakes should I watch out for in exams, Maa?

**Mother:** Three common traps. First, students write "pi equals 22/7" and call pi rational. Remember, 22/7 is only an approximation -- the true value of pi is irrational. Second, do not mix up non-terminating recurring decimals (which are rational) with non-terminating non-recurring decimals (which are irrational). Third, always remember that rationals and irrationals together fill every single point on the number line -- that is the real number system.

## Decimal Expansions

**Child:** Maa, how can I quickly figure out whether a fraction will give a terminating decimal or a repeating one?

**Mother:** Look at the denominator after reducing the fraction to its simplest form. If the denominator's prime factors are only 2s and 5s, the decimal terminates. Any other prime factor, and the decimal repeats. For instance, 3/8 -- the denominator 8 equals 2-cubed, so it terminates as 0.375. But 5/11 -- the denominator 11 is neither 2 nor 5, so it repeats as 0.454545...

**Child:** And how do I convert a repeating decimal back into a fraction?

**Mother:** Use a multiplication trick. Say you have x = 0.444... Multiply by 10: 10x = 4.444... Now subtract the original: 10x minus x = 4. That gives 9x = 4, so x = 4/9. For two-digit repeating blocks, multiply by 100 instead. This method appears in board exams regularly, so practise it with different types of repeating blocks.

**Child:** One thing really puzzles me -- the textbook says 0.9999... equals 1 exactly. Is that really true?

**Mother:** Let x = 0.9999... Then 10x = 9.9999... Subtract: 10x minus x = 9, so 9x = 9, and x = 1. It is not "nearly 1" or "approximately 1" -- it is precisely 1. The same number has two different decimal representations. This surprises many people, but the mathematics is watertight.

## Operations on Real Numbers

**Child:** Maa, when I add a rational and an irrational number, the answer is always irrational. But adding two irrational numbers can sometimes give a rational answer -- like sqrt(7) plus negative sqrt(7) equals zero. Is there a simple way to remember this?

**Mother:** Think of it as a mixing rule. Rational mixed with irrational always stays irrational -- whether you add, subtract, multiply, or divide. But irrational mixed with irrational has no guarantee. You must check each case individually. For your exams, keep these standard identities handy: sqrt(a) times sqrt(b) equals sqrt(ab), and the conjugate identity (sqrt(a) + sqrt(b)) times (sqrt(a) - sqrt(b)) equals a minus b.

**Child:** Why do we bother rationalising denominators?

**Mother:** When you have 1 divided by (3 + sqrt(5)), the denominator has a square root, which makes further calculations messy. Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate (3 - sqrt(5)), and the denominator becomes 9 minus 5 equals 4 -- clean and rational. Engineers and scientists always prefer rational denominators because they make comparisons and computations much simpler. In competitive exams like JEE, rationalisation is a basic building block for harder simplification problems.

## Laws of Exponents for Real Numbers

**Child:** We already know exponent rules for whole number powers. What changes when the exponent is a fraction like 3/2?

**Mother:** The core rules stay exactly the same -- the notation just extends. a to the power (m/n) means take the n-th root of a, then raise it to the power m. So 4 to the power (3/2) means the square root of 4 is 2, and 2 cubed is 8. The four laws carry over directly: same base multiplication means add exponents, division means subtract, power of a power means multiply exponents.

**Child:** Any quick strategy for solving these in exams?

**Mother:** Always start by expressing the base as a power of a prime number. For example, to simplify 243 to the power (2/5), recognise that 243 equals 3 to the fifth. Then 3 to the fifth raised to the power 2/5 gives 3 to the power 2, which is 9. The great Indian mathematician Srinivasa Ramanujan had an extraordinary ability to spot such patterns instantly. You can develop the same skill through regular practice.

**Child:** So the key is knowing the powers of small prime numbers?

**Mother:** Exactly. Memorise the squares, cubes, fourth powers, and fifth powers of 2, 3, 5, and 7. It takes just a few minutes of revision but saves enormous time during examinations and prevents careless arithmetic errors.
