## Chords and the Centre

**Mother:** Beta, today let us talk about circles. You see the steel plate we eat in -- a thali?

**Child:** Yes, Maa. It is round.

**Mother:** Exactly. Now imagine stretching a thread from one edge of the thali to another edge, across the plate. That thread is like a **chord**. If the thread passes right through the centre dot, it becomes the **diameter** -- the longest chord possible.

**Child:** So every diameter is a chord, but not every chord is a diameter?

**Mother:** Correct! Now here is a beautiful property. If I drop a line straight down from the centre of the thali to any chord -- making a 90° angle with it -- that line will hit the chord exactly at its middle point. The perpendicular from the centre always **bisects** the chord.

**Child:** So if I know the radius and the distance from the centre to the chord, I can find the chord's length using the Pythagoras theorem?

**Mother:** Exactly right. Suppose our thali has radius 13 cm and a chord sits 5 cm away from the centre. The half-chord length is sqrt(13² - 5²) = sqrt(169 - 25) = sqrt(144) = 12 cm. So the full chord is 24 cm.

**Child:** Maa, what about equal chords?

**Mother:** Two chords of the same length always sit at equal distances from the centre. And they always create equal angles at the centre. Think of it like this -- if you place two identical threads inside the same thali, both threads will be equally far from the middle dot, and both will "open up" the same angle when you join their ends to the centre.

## The Inscribed Angle Magic

**Child:** Maa, the textbook says the angle at the centre is double the angle at the circumference. Why?

**Mother:** Let me explain with a rangoli. Imagine you draw a big circle on the floor. Pick two points A and B on the circle to form an arc. Now join A and B to the centre O -- you get the central angle AOB. Then join A and B to any other point P sitting on the circle -- you get the inscribed angle APB. No matter where P sits on the major arc, angle APB will always be exactly half of angle AOB.

**Child:** That is amazing! So if angle AOB is 120°, then angle APB is 60°?

**Mother:** Yes! And since every point on the major arc gives the same inscribed angle, all angles in the same segment are **equal**. This is why the theorem is so powerful -- it connects every point on an arc to the same angle.

**Child:** What about the semicircle case?

**Mother:** The diameter creates a 180° angle at the centre. Half of 180° is 90°. So any angle inscribed in a semicircle is a perfect right angle! Masons in old Indian forts used this trick -- they would draw a semicircle on the ground, pick any point on it, and the angle formed was guaranteed to be 90°. No need for a set square.

**Child:** So if I ever need to check a right angle, I can just draw a semicircle?

**Mother:** Precisely, beta. That is Thales' theorem in action -- discovered around 600 BCE, but the geometry works the same today as it did then.

## Cyclic Quadrilaterals

**Child:** Maa, what is a cyclic quadrilateral?

**Mother:** When all four corners of a quadrilateral touch a single circle, we call it a cyclic quadrilateral. The circle passing through all four vertices is the **circumcircle**.

**Child:** And opposite angles add up to 180°?

**Mother:** Always. Think of it this way: in cyclic quadrilateral ABCD, arc BCD creates angle A, and arc BAD creates angle C. Together, these two arcs complete the full circle -- 360° at the centre. Since each inscribed angle is half the central angle of its arc, angle A + angle C = half of 360° = 180°.

**Child:** So if angle A is 75°, angle C must be 105°?

**Mother:** Exactly. And similarly angle B + angle D = 180°. A very common exam mistake is applying this rule to adjacent angles instead of opposite angles. Always double-check which angles are across from each other.

**Child:** Is the converse also true?

**Mother:** Yes. If you find a quadrilateral where opposite angles sum to 180°, you can be sure that a circle passes through all four corners. This is helpful when a problem asks you to prove that four points lie on a common circle -- just show that the opposite angles of the quadrilateral they form are supplementary.

## Solving Circle Problems

**Child:** Maa, with so many theorems, how do I know which one to use?

**Mother:** Think of it as a toolkit. If the problem mentions **chords**, reach for the perpendicular-bisector theorem or the equal-chords-equal-distance result. If it mentions **angles**, decide whether you need the central-angle-is-double rule, the same-segment theorem, or the semicircle property. If a **quadrilateral inscribed in a circle** appears, go straight to the supplementary-opposite-angles theorem.

**Child:** Should I always draw a diagram?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Mark the centre, draw all relevant radii, label every chord and angle. Half the battle in circle geometry is seeing the right triangles and equal lengths hiding in the figure. A neat, well-labelled diagram will guide you to the correct theorem almost automatically.

**Child:** Thanks, Maa. I feel much more confident about circles now!

**Mother:** You should, beta. These theorems connect beautifully to each other. Once you see the patterns, circles become one of the most elegant topics in geometry.
