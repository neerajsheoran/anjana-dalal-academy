## Introduction

Picture a potter's wheel spinning in a Jaipur workshop. Every speck of clay on the rim traces out a perfect **circle** -- each point sitting at the same fixed distance from the hub at the centre. That fixed distance is the **radius**, and the hub itself is the **centre**. In this chapter, we explore the rich geometry hidden inside circles: how chords behave, how arcs create angles, and what happens when a quadrilateral sits snugly inside a circle.

---

## Fundamental Vocabulary

* **Centre (O):** The anchor point; every point on the circle is equally far from it.
* **Radius (r):** The constant gap between the centre and any point on the circumference.
* **Chord:** Any straight line segment whose both endpoints rest on the circle. Think of the string stretched across a tabla drum head.
* **Diameter:** The special chord that passes right through the centre. It is the **longest possible chord** and measures exactly 2r.
* **Arc:** A curved portion of the circumference. When a chord cuts a circle, the larger curve is the **major arc** and the smaller curve is the **minor arc**.
* **Semicircle:** The arc formed when a diameter splits the circle into two equal curves.
* **Segment:** The region trapped between a chord and its arc. The bigger region is the **major segment**; the smaller is the **minor segment**.
* **Sector:** The "pizza-slice" region enclosed by two radii and the arc between them.

## Angles Formed by Chords at the Centre

When you join the endpoints of a chord to any other point, the lines form an angle. The **angle subtended by chord PQ at point R** is simply angle PRQ.

* When R is the centre O, the resulting angle is called the **central angle**.
* Longer chords open up wider central angles; shorter chords produce narrower ones.

> **Theorem 9.1:** If two chords of the same circle are equal in length, they carve out **equal central angles**.

**Why this is true:** Suppose chords AB and CD are equal and O is the centre. In triangles OAB and OCD, we have OA = OC and OB = OD (all radii), and AB = CD (given). By the SSS congruence rule, the two triangles match perfectly, which forces angle AOB = angle COD.

> **Theorem 9.2 (Converse):** If two chords produce equal central angles, the chords themselves must be **equal in length**.

## The Perpendicular-Bisector Property

> **Theorem 9.3:** Drop a perpendicular from the centre of a circle onto any chord. It lands exactly at the chord's **midpoint**, splitting the chord into two equal halves.

Imagine a bamboo stick placed horizontally inside a circular hoop. If you drop a plumb line from the topmost point of the hoop straight down to the stick, it will hit the stick right in the middle.

![Circle with perpendicular OM drawn from centre O to chord AB, showing that M is the midpoint of AB](perpendicular-from-centre-bisects-chord.png)

> **Theorem 9.4 (Converse):** If a line from the centre reaches the midpoint of a chord, that line is automatically **perpendicular** to the chord.

---

## How Far Are Equal Chords from the Centre?

The **distance of a chord from the centre** is measured along the perpendicular from O to the chord.

> **Theorem 9.5:** Two chords of equal length sit at **equal distances** from the centre.

> **Theorem 9.6 (Converse):** Two chords that are equally far from the centre must be **equal in length**.

> **Useful observation:** The diameter is the longest chord and passes through the centre, so its distance from the centre is zero. As chords grow shorter, they drift further from the centre -- like parallel cuts across a round roti: the cut through the middle is the longest, and cuts near the edge are shorter.

## The Central-Angle-to-Inscribed-Angle Relationship

This is arguably the most powerful result in the chapter.

> **Theorem 9.7:** The angle that an arc makes at the centre is exactly **twice** the angle it makes at any point on the remaining circumference.

So if arc PQ creates angle POQ at the centre and angle PRQ at a point R on the circle, then angle POQ = 2 x angle PRQ.

![Circle showing arc PQ subtending angle POQ at the centre and angle PRQ at the circumference, with the central angle being double the inscribed angle](central-angle-double-inscribed-angle.png)

> **Theorem 9.8:** All angles standing in the **same segment** of a circle are **equal** to one another.

Since each inscribed angle is half the same central angle, they must all be identical.

> **Theorem 9.9 (Converse):** If a line segment joining two points creates equal angles at two other points on the same side, all four points are **concyclic** (they share a common circle).

> **Special case -- the semicircle rule:** A diameter subtends 180° at the centre. Half of that is 90°. Therefore the angle sitting in a semicircle is always a **right angle**. Indian temple architects historically used this property to verify right angles during construction by inscribing triangles in semicircular arches.

## Cyclic Quadrilaterals

A quadrilateral is called **cyclic** when all four of its vertices lie on a single circle. The circumscribed circle is sometimes called the **circumcircle**.

> **Theorem 9.10:** In any cyclic quadrilateral, each pair of **opposite angles** adds up to **180°** (they are supplementary).

For cyclic quadrilateral ABCD: angle A + angle C = 180°, and angle B + angle D = 180°.

![Cyclic quadrilateral ABCD inscribed in a circle with opposite angles marked as supplementary](cyclic-quadrilateral-supplementary-angles.png)

> **Theorem 9.11 (Converse):** If a quadrilateral's opposite angles sum to 180°, it must be **cyclic** -- a circle can be drawn through all four vertices.

**Practical check:** Suppose you measure the four corner angles of a decorative tile as 70°, 110°, 85°, and 95°. Because 70° + 85° = 155° (not 180°), this tile is not cyclic. But if the angles were 70°, 110°, 110°, and 70°, then 70° + 110° = 180° confirms a cyclic shape.

---

## Summary

1. Equal chords produce equal central angles, and the reverse holds too.
2. A perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord; a line from the centre to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular.
3. Equal chords are equidistant from the centre, and equidistant chords are equal.
4. The central angle is **double** the inscribed angle for the same arc.
5. Angles in the same segment are equal.
6. The angle in a semicircle is **90°**.
7. Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add up to **180°**.
