## Almost Everything Around You Is a Mixture

Look at your dinner plate. The dal is a mixture (water, lentil, salt, spices). The rice is mostly one substance, but the salt and ghee mixed into it make it a mixture. The buttermilk in your glass is a mixture. The air you are breathing right now is a mixture (78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, plus traces of other gases). Even the steel of your spoon is a mixture (mostly iron, with small amounts of carbon and other elements).

Pure substances -- ones with only one kind of particle -- are surprisingly rare in everyday life. Almost everything is a mixture.

> **Key idea:** A **mixture** is a combination of two or more substances physically combined, without forming a new compound. The substances can usually be separated again.

In this chapter, we will sort mixtures into useful categories and learn the techniques scientists and chemists use to pull them apart.

---

## Two Main Kinds of Mixtures

Pour some sugar into a glass of water and stir. The sugar disappears. You cannot see the individual sugar particles anywhere. The whole liquid looks uniform.

Now stir some sand into a glass of water. The sand does not dissolve. You see clearly that there are two things in the glass -- water and sand.

These two examples show the two big categories of mixtures.

### Homogeneous mixtures (solutions)

A **homogeneous mixture** has a **uniform composition** throughout. Wherever you sample it, the composition is the same.

- Sugar solution
- Salt water
- Air
- Vinegar
- Brass (a solid solution of copper and zinc)

Homogeneous mixtures are also called **solutions**. The substance that dissolves (sugar) is the **solute**, and the substance it dissolves in (water) is the **solvent**.

### Heterogeneous mixtures

A **heterogeneous mixture** has **visibly different** components -- you can see distinct regions of the different substances.

- Sand in water
- Oil and water
- Salt mixed with chalk powder
- A bowl of mixed fruit
- Concrete

> **Key idea:** A solution looks uniform; a heterogeneous mixture does not. The difference comes down to how completely one component is dispersed in the other.

## Properties of a Solution

If you dissolved 10 grams of sugar in 100 grams of water, you have a solution. Here are its key properties:

- It is **homogeneous** -- looks uniform throughout.
- Particle size is extremely small -- under 1 nanometre (one-billionth of a metre).
- The particles **do not settle** even after very long times.
- It does **not scatter light** -- a beam of light passes through invisibly.
- It cannot be separated by simple filtration.

### Concentration of a solution

Concentration tells us how much solute is dissolved per unit of solvent (or solution). One common way:

**Mass percentage = (mass of solute ÷ mass of solution) × 100%**

If you dissolve 5 g of salt in 95 g of water, the solution has 5/100 = 5% mass concentration of salt.

### Saturated and unsaturated solutions

If you keep adding sugar to a glass of water, eventually no more dissolves and the extra sugar just settles. The water is **saturated** -- it has dissolved as much as it can hold at that temperature.

Add less than the saturation amount and you have an **unsaturated** solution.

Heat the water and you can dissolve more sugar -- so saturation depends on temperature. This is why hot chai dissolves sugar much faster than cold water.

## Suspensions and Colloids

Not all mixtures with a liquid are solutions. There are two intermediate categories worth knowing.

### Suspensions

A **suspension** has visible particles that stay suspended in a liquid for a while but slowly settle to the bottom.

- Muddy river water
- Chalk dust stirred into water
- Whitewash paint before stirring

Properties:
- Particles are visible to the eye (larger than 1000 nm).
- Particles settle if left undisturbed.
- A beam of light is scattered visibly.
- The mixture is **heterogeneous**.

### Colloids

A **colloid** sits between a true solution and a suspension. Its particles are small enough not to settle, but big enough to scatter light.

- Milk (fat droplets in water)
- Fog (tiny water droplets in air)
- Smoke (solid particles in air)
- Whipped cream
- Jelly

Properties:
- Particles between 1 and 1000 nm.
- Particles **do not settle**.
- The mixture **scatters light** -- you can see the path of a beam through it.
- Appears homogeneous to the eye but is actually heterogeneous at the microscopic level.

### Tyndall effect

When a strong beam of light shines through a colloid, you see the path of the beam clearly. This is called the **Tyndall effect** -- caused by colloidal particles scattering the light.

You see the Tyndall effect when:
- Sunlight streams through the gaps between leaves on a misty morning
- A film projector's beam is visible in a dusty cinema hall
- Car headlights cut through fog
- Sunlight enters a dimly lit room through a window

A true solution does not show the Tyndall effect. Only colloids and suspensions do.

> **Key idea:** Solutions (true) — no Tyndall effect. Colloids and suspensions — Tyndall effect visible.

## How to Separate Mixtures

Different mixtures need different separation techniques. Here are the main methods every science student should know.

### 1. Filtration

For separating an **insoluble solid from a liquid**.

Pass the mixture through filter paper. The solid stays behind; the liquid passes through.

Used for: separating sand from water, tea leaves from chai, mud from drinking water.

### 2. Evaporation

For recovering a **dissolved solid** from a solution.

Heat the solution gently. The liquid evaporates and leaves the solid behind.

Used for: getting salt from sea water (the basis of all Indian sea-salt production along the Gujarat and Tamil Nadu coasts), making sugar crystals from cane juice.

### 3. Distillation

For separating two **miscible liquids** with different boiling points.

Heat the mixture. The liquid with the lower boiling point evaporates first. The vapour is then condensed back to liquid in a separate vessel.

Used for: purifying water (laboratory-grade distilled water), separating water from ink, refining petrol/diesel from crude oil.

### 4. Fractional distillation

For separating a mixture of **several miscible liquids** with close boiling points.

A taller, more complex distillation column lets each liquid condense at a different height based on its boiling point.

Used for: separating the gases in air (industrial liquid nitrogen, oxygen, argon), separating petroleum into petrol, diesel, kerosene, naphtha.

### 5. Sublimation

For separating a **sublimable solid** (turns directly from solid to gas) from a non-sublimable solid.

Heat the mixture. The sublimable solid changes to vapour and is collected by cooling.

Used for: separating ammonium chloride or naphthalene from common salt.

### 6. Separating funnel

For separating **two immiscible liquids** (liquids that don't mix).

Pour the mixture into the funnel. The heavier liquid settles at the bottom. Open the tap to drain it out; close the tap when the second liquid reaches the top.

Used for: separating oil from water, kerosene from water.

### 7. Chromatography

For separating different **coloured components** of a mixture.

Place a drop of the mixture on a strip of paper. As a solvent (like water) climbs the paper, different components move different distances based on how strongly they stick to the paper or dissolve in the solvent.

Used for: separating the colours in ink, identifying pigments in plant extracts, food adulteration testing in laboratories.

### 8. Centrifugation

For separating denser particles from a liquid quickly.

Spin the mixture rapidly. Denser particles fly to the outside of the rotor; lighter ones stay in the middle.

Used for: separating cream from milk (used in every dairy across India), separating plasma from blood cells in hospital labs, washing machines (extracting water from clothes).

### 9. Crystallisation

For obtaining **pure crystals** of a solid from its impure solution.

Dissolve the impure solid in a hot solvent. Let it cool slowly. As the solution cools, pure crystals form, leaving impurities behind in the liquid.

Used for: purifying salt, making sugar crystals, growing copper sulphate crystals in school labs.

> **Key idea:** Match the technique to the mixture. Insoluble solid + liquid → filtration. Dissolved solid → evaporation or crystallisation. Two liquids with different boiling points → distillation. Two immiscible liquids → separating funnel.

## Summary

- A **mixture** contains two or more substances physically combined; they can usually be separated again.
- **Homogeneous mixtures (solutions)** have uniform composition. Examples: sugar water, air, brass.
- **Heterogeneous mixtures** have visibly different components. Examples: sand in water, oil and water.
- In a solution, the substance that dissolves is the **solute**; the substance it dissolves in is the **solvent**.
- A solution that has dissolved its maximum is **saturated**; one that can dissolve more is **unsaturated**.
- **Suspensions** have visible particles that settle over time.
- **Colloids** have particles small enough not to settle but large enough to scatter light (Tyndall effect).
- Only solutions DO NOT show the Tyndall effect; colloids and suspensions DO.
- Separation techniques:
  - **Filtration** — insoluble solid from liquid
  - **Evaporation** / **Crystallisation** — dissolved solid
  - **Distillation** / **Fractional distillation** — miscible liquids
  - **Sublimation** — sublimable solid from mixture
  - **Separating funnel** — immiscible liquids
  - **Chromatography** — coloured components
  - **Centrifugation** — denser components from liquid

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Sea salt from the Gulf of Khambhat**. India is one of the world's top three salt producers. In Gujarat, vast saltpans evaporate sea water under the sun. The water evaporates, leaving behind pure salt — the simplest large-scale industrial application of evaporation you can imagine.

**Petroleum refining at Jamnagar**. India's largest oil refinery, run by Reliance, uses massive fractional distillation columns to separate crude oil into petrol, diesel, kerosene, naphtha, lubricants, and asphalt. Same principle as a school lab experiment, just at colossal scale.

**Food adulteration testing**. The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India uses chromatography to test for adulteration in milk, ghee, spices, and edible oils. Different chemicals move different distances on the test paper, revealing what was actually in the sample.

### Try it at home — Watch the Tyndall effect

**What you'll need:** A glass of water, a teaspoon of cornflour (or milk), a torch (your phone torch works), a dark room.

**Steps:**
1. Take two glasses of clean water.
2. In one, dissolve a teaspoon of sugar (true solution).
3. In the other, mix a tiny pinch of cornflour or a few drops of milk (colloid).
4. Darken the room.
5. Shine the torch through each glass from the side.

**What to look for:**
- In the **sugar solution**, the torch beam passes through invisibly — you cannot see the path of light inside the water.
- In the **cornflour/milk colloid**, the beam of light is clearly visible inside the liquid — you can trace its path.

You have just demonstrated the Tyndall effect with two glasses and a torch. The same principle is at work when you see sunlight beams in a dusty cinema hall or fog cut by car headlights.
