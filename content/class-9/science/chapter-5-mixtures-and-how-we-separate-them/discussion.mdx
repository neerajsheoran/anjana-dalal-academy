## What Makes Something a Mixture?

**Student:** Sir, I have a doubt. Almost everything around me is described as a mixture. Then what is NOT a mixture?

**Mentor:** Good question. A pure substance — like distilled water, or pure gold, or table salt — has only ONE kind of particle. Pure water is made of nothing but H₂O molecules. Pure salt is made of nothing but sodium chloride.

A mixture combines two or more such substances physically. The particles of each ingredient retain their own identity. You can usually separate them again.

The clean test: can I separate the components without a chemical reaction? If yes, it is a mixture. If you would need to break chemical bonds, you are looking at a compound or an element, not a mixture.

**Student:** So buttermilk is a mixture but milk itself is not?

**Mentor:** Milk itself is also a mixture — water, fats, proteins, sugars all combined. Buttermilk is a churned version of the same mixture. In everyday life, finding a truly pure substance is hard. Even tap water has dissolved minerals; even kitchen salt has traces of iodine or magnesium.

## Solutions, Suspensions, and Colloids

**Student:** I get confused between solutions, suspensions, and colloids. They all look like one thing mixed with another.

**Mentor:** Think of them as three different scales of mixing.

**Solution** — the smallest particles, less than 1 nanometre. Completely uniform. You cannot see the particles; they do not settle; a beam of light passes through without scattering. Sugar in water is the classic example.

**Suspension** — the largest particles, bigger than 1000 nanometres. The particles are visible to the eye. They settle if you leave the mixture alone. Stir mud into water and wait — the mud sinks to the bottom.

**Colloid** — in between. The particles are too small to see but too big to ignore. They do not settle. But they scatter light — that is the Tyndall effect. Milk is a beautiful example.

**Student:** So whether something is a colloid or a suspension depends on particle size?

**Mentor:** Yes — and on whether the particles settle. Stir mud into water: it is a suspension because mud particles are large enough to settle. Mix corn flour into water and stir well: it can act as a colloid because the cornflour particles are small enough to stay dispersed.

This is also why our chai is technically a colloid — tea leaves are filtered out, but tiny tannin and pigment particles remain suspended too finely to settle. A beam of sunlight through chai would scatter visibly — the Tyndall effect.

## The Tyndall Effect Everywhere

**Student:** When did I last see the Tyndall effect without knowing it?

**Mentor:** Many times. Walk into a small dusty room in your house with a sunny window. The streaks of sunlight you see are sunbeams scattering off dust particles — a Tyndall effect.

Stand outside in the early morning fog. Car headlights cut through the fog visibly — sunlight or headlight beams scattered by tiny water droplets in the air — another Tyndall effect.

Sit in a dim cinema hall. The film projector's beam is clearly visible across the hall — light scattered by smoke and dust in the air.

A monsoon mist hanging in the air. The same Tyndall effect.

Once you know what to look for, you start seeing it everywhere. It is one of those small physics phenomena that lights up the world.

## Choosing the Right Separation Technique

**Student:** Sir, there are so many separation techniques. How do I know which one to use for a given mixture?

**Mentor:** Look at the components and ask three questions.

First, are the components in different physical states? If one is solid and the other liquid, that already narrows your options. If both are liquids, that narrows differently.

Second, what is unique about the components? Different boiling points? Different solubilities? Different densities? Different sizes?

Third, what tool exploits that uniqueness?

Let me walk you through some examples:

- **Tea leaves in chai** → Different physical states (solid in liquid), and the solid is insoluble. Filtration through a tea strainer.
- **Salt in sea water** → Different physical states, but the solid is dissolved. Cannot filter. Use evaporation.
- **Water and ink** → Both are liquids, but they are miscible. They have different boiling points. Distillation.
- **Oil and water** → Both liquids, but immiscible (do not mix). Separating funnel.
- **Sand and salt** → Both solids. Add water — salt dissolves, sand does not. Filter to remove sand. Evaporate the filtrate to get salt back.

The pattern: identify the property that differs, choose the tool that uses that property.

## Crystallisation vs Evaporation

**Student:** Why do we use crystallisation when simple evaporation also gives back the dissolved solid?

**Mentor:** Because evaporation gives back EVERYTHING that was dissolved — including impurities. If your salt water has dirt particles or other dissolved salts, evaporation leaves all of it behind in a messy crust.

Crystallisation is more selective. You dissolve the impure solid in a hot solvent. As the solution cools, the desired substance crystallises out in pure form, but most impurities stay behind in the remaining liquid (which you then discard).

This is why sugar manufacturing uses crystallisation, not direct evaporation. The juice from sugarcane has many dissolved substances. Crystallisation isolates pure sucrose from the rest.

**Student:** So crystallisation is purer than evaporation?

**Mentor:** Almost always, yes. If purity matters, crystallise. If you just want to recover whatever was dissolved, evaporation is faster.

## Indian Industries and Mixture Separation

**Student:** Are these techniques used in real Indian industries?

**Mentor:** Constantly. Reliance's Jamnagar refinery uses fractional distillation to separate crude oil into petrol, diesel, kerosene, naphtha, and many other products. The columns are taller than apartment buildings, but the principle is the same as the lab.

The Indian salt industry — second largest in the world — runs on evaporation. Gujarat's saltpans evaporate sea water under the sun and harvest the salt by the truckload.

Indian dairies separate cream from milk using centrifugation. The Amul plant in Anand processes millions of litres a day using exactly this technique.

The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India uses chromatography to test ghee, edible oils, and spices for adulteration. Different chemicals move different distances on the test paper, revealing what is actually in the sample.

Indian chemistry is not just textbook physics. It is the engine of huge sectors of our economy.

**Student:** That helps me see why this chapter matters.

**Mentor:** Excellent. Every glass of milk, every grain of salt, every litre of petrol you encounter today involves the same separation chemistry you are now studying.
