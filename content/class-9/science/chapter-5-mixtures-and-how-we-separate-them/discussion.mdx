## What Makes Something a Mixture?

**Meera:** Arjun, I have a doubt. The textbook says almost everything around us is a mixture. So what is actually NOT a mixture?

**Arjun:** Pure substances. Things made of only one kind of particle. Distilled water is just H₂O molecules, nothing else. Pure gold is just gold atoms. Pure table salt is just sodium chloride.

**Meera:** And a mixture is when two pure things are jumbled together?

**Arjun:** Physically jumbled. The key word is physical — each ingredient keeps its own identity. You can usually pull them apart again.

**Meera:** Is there a clean test to spot one?

**Arjun:** Yes. Ask — can I separate the components WITHOUT a chemical reaction? If yes, mixture. If you would need to break chemical bonds to split it, it is a compound or element.

**Meera:** OK so buttermilk is obviously a mixture. But what about plain milk? It LOOKS uniform.

**Arjun:** Milk is also a mixture, yaar. Water, fats, proteins, sugars — all combined. The uniform look is just because the particles are tiny. Buttermilk is the same mixture, just churned.

**Meera:** So finding a TRULY pure substance is actually rare?

**Arjun:** Very rare in daily life. Even your tap water has dissolved minerals. Even the iodised salt mummy uses has magnesium and iodine added. Pure is more of a chemistry-lab concept than a kitchen concept.

## Solutions, Suspensions, and Colloids

**Meera:** I keep mixing up solutions, suspensions, and colloids. To me they all just look like one thing dissolved in another.

**Arjun:** Think of them as three different scales of mixing, based on particle size.

**Meera:** Tell me one at a time.

**Arjun:** **Solution** — smallest particles, less than 1 nanometre. Completely uniform. You cannot see the particles, they never settle, and a torch beam passes through without scattering. Sugar in water is the classic.

**Meera:** OK. And suspension?

**Arjun:** **Suspension** — biggest particles, more than 1000 nanometres. You can see them with your eyes. They settle if you leave the mixture alone. Stir mud in water and walk away — the mud sinks.

**Meera:** And colloids are the middle ones?

**Arjun:** Yes. **Colloid** — too small to see, too big to ignore. Particles never settle, but they scatter light. That scattering is the **Tyndall effect**. Milk is the textbook example.

**Meera:** So whether something is a colloid or suspension depends on particle size?

**Arjun:** Size AND whether it settles. Mud in water settles, so it is a suspension. But corn flour stirred properly stays dispersed — that is acting like a colloid.

**Meera:** Wait, so our chai is a colloid?

**Arjun:** Technically yes. We filter out the leaves, but tiny tannin and pigment particles stay suspended too finely to settle. If you shine a torch sideways through a glass of chai in a dim room, you will see the beam — Tyndall effect right at the breakfast table.

## The Tyndall Effect Everywhere

**Meera:** When have I seen the Tyndall effect without knowing what it was called?

**Arjun:** All the time, you just never named it. Walk into Daadi's storeroom on a sunny afternoon. The shafts of light coming through the window — that visible streak is sunlight scattering off floating dust. Tyndall effect.

**Meera:** Oh, like in those old film shots?

**Arjun:** Exactly. Or stand outside in the early-morning Delhi winter fog. Car headlights cut through the mist in those bright cones — that is light scattered by tiny water droplets. Another Tyndall.

**Meera:** Cinema halls also, no? The projector beam is super visible across the hall.

**Arjun:** Yes, that is dust and tiny smoke particles scattering the light. And during monsoon when there is mist on the hills — same thing again.

**Meera:** It is suddenly everywhere.

**Arjun:** That is the fun of physics. Once you know what to look for, the world feels lit up by little phenomena you used to scroll past.

## Choosing the Right Separation Technique

**Meera:** Arjun, the chapter has SO many separation techniques. How do I know which one to use for a given mixture?

**Arjun:** Look at the components and ask yourself three questions. First — are the components in different physical states? Solid in liquid? Two liquids? That narrows the options immediately.

**Meera:** OK, what is the second?

**Arjun:** What property is UNIQUE to one of the components? Different boiling points? Different solubilities? Different densities? Different particle sizes?

**Meera:** And the third?

**Arjun:** What tool exploits that unique property? Let me walk you through examples.

**Meera:** Please, with stuff I actually know.

**Arjun:** Tea leaves in chai — different states, solid is insoluble. Filter through a strainer. Salt in seawater — also different states, but the salt is DISSOLVED, so you cannot filter. Use evaporation instead.

**Meera:** OK.

**Arjun:** Water and ink — two liquids, miscible, but different boiling points. Distillation. Oil and water — two liquids but they refuse to mix. Separating funnel. Sand and salt — both solids. Add water, salt dissolves, sand does not. Filter to remove sand, evaporate the filtrate to get salt back.

**Meera:** So the pattern is — find the property that differs, pick the tool that uses that property.

**Arjun:** That is literally the whole chapter in one sentence. Board exam loves this pattern.

## Crystallisation vs Evaporation

**Meera:** I do not get this — why use crystallisation when simple evaporation also gives back the dissolved solid?

**Arjun:** Because evaporation gives back EVERYTHING that was dissolved. Including impurities. If your salt water has dust or other dissolved salts, evaporation leaves all of it behind as a messy crust.

**Meera:** Ah, so it is not selective.

**Arjun:** Right. Crystallisation IS selective. You dissolve the impure solid in a hot solvent. As the solution slowly cools, only the substance you want crystallises out in clean form. The impurities mostly stay behind in the leftover liquid, which you discard.

**Meera:** Is that how sugar is purified industrially?

**Arjun:** Exactly. Sugarcane juice has a mess of dissolved stuff in it. Crystallisation isolates clean white sucrose crystals from all the rest. Direct evaporation would just give you brown sticky goop.

**Meera:** So crystallisation is purer than evaporation?

**Arjun:** Almost always. If purity matters, crystallise. If you just want to dump-recover whatever was dissolved and do not mind impurities, evaporation is quicker.

## Indian Industries and Mixture Separation

**Meera:** Are these techniques really used in big Indian industries? Or is this just textbook stuff?

**Arjun:** Constantly used. The Reliance refinery at Jamnagar uses fractional distillation to split crude oil into petrol, diesel, kerosene, naphtha, lubricants — all in one tall column. The columns are taller than apartment buildings, but the principle is exactly what your textbook describes.

**Meera:** Wait, really? The same idea?

**Arjun:** Same idea. Different boiling points, different layers come off at different heights of the column. Scaled up massively, but the physics is identical.

**Meera:** And salt?

**Arjun:** India is the second-largest salt producer in the world. The Gujarat saltpans evaporate seawater under the sun, all day, for months. Whole villages run on that one process.

**Meera:** What about dairy? Amul and all?

**Arjun:** Centrifugation. Amul's plant in Anand spins millions of litres of milk a day to separate cream from skimmed milk. The cream is less dense, so it floats to the inside of the spinning bowl.

**Meera:** Any other example?

**Arjun:** FSSAI uses chromatography to test ghee, edible oils, and spices for adulteration. Different chemicals travel different distances on the test paper, so you can see exactly what is in the sample. That is how they catch milk powder mixed into khoya during Diwali.

**Meera:** That actually makes me feel like this chapter matters in real life.

**Arjun:** It is the engine of huge sectors of our economy, yaar. Every glass of milk, every grain of salt, every litre of petrol you touch involves the same separation chemistry you are studying right now.
