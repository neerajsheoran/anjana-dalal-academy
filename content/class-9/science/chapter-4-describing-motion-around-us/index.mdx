## Everything Is Moving

Look out of any window and motion is everywhere. A street dog trots across the road. A pigeon flies past. Tree leaves wave in the breeze. Clouds drift overhead. Even when you stand absolutely still, the Earth is rotating beneath your feet at over 1,500 km/h at the equator -- and orbiting the Sun at over 100,000 km/h.

Motion is one of the easiest things to notice and one of the most powerful ideas in all of physics. In this chapter we will learn to describe motion not just in everyday words, but in precise quantities, equations, and graphs.

> **Key idea:** Motion happens when an object's **position changes with time** relative to a chosen reference point. Choose the reference point, watch the position change, and you can describe any motion.

---

## Position and Reference Point

To describe motion, you first need a **reference point** -- a fixed spot from which all positions are measured. Without a reference point, "moving" has no meaning.

If a Vande Bharat train is racing along the tracks at 160 km/h, you would say it is moving relative to the **ground**. But for a passenger sitting inside, the train is stationary relative to **the seat in front**. Both descriptions are true -- they just use different reference points.

When we put a number on position, we also need a **direction**. Saying "the school is 2 km from the temple" is fine for a chat, but a physicist would specify "2 km north of the temple". Distance + direction = a complete position description.

---

## Distance and Displacement — Two Different Numbers

This is where everyday English starts to mislead. We use "distance" loosely, but in physics, **distance** and **displacement** are two different quantities.

- **Distance** is the **total length** of the path actually travelled. It does not care about direction. If you walked 3 km north, then 4 km south, your distance is **7 km**.
- **Displacement** is the **straight-line gap** between start and end positions, with a direction. For that same walk, your displacement is **1 km south** of where you started.

> **Key idea:** Distance is path length (no direction). Displacement is the net change in position (with direction). They can be very different.

A car going round a 5 km circular track once travels a **distance** of 5 km. Its **displacement** is **zero** because it ended exactly where it started.

---

## Speed and Velocity

Speed and velocity sound the same in daily English. In physics they are different.

- **Speed** = distance ÷ time. It has only magnitude, no direction. It is a **scalar**.
- **Velocity** = displacement ÷ time. It has magnitude AND direction. It is a **vector**.

If a cricket ball travels 40 metres in 4 seconds, its speed is **10 m/s**. If it ended up 40 metres east of where it started, its velocity is **10 m/s east**.

### Uniform vs non-uniform motion

- **Uniform motion** — the object covers equal distances in equal time intervals. The speed is constant.
- **Non-uniform motion** — the speed changes over time. The object covers different distances in equal time intervals.

A car driving steadily on a long stretch of highway approaches uniform motion. A car in Mumbai traffic, with frequent stops, is firmly non-uniform.

### Average speed and average velocity

For non-uniform motion, single-instant speed varies. We often calculate the **average**:

**Average speed = total distance ÷ total time**

**Average velocity = total displacement ÷ total time**

Example: A school bus covers 30 km in 1 hour due to morning traffic. Its average speed is 30 km/h, even though it sometimes touched 60 km/h and sometimes stopped at red lights.

---

## Acceleration — When Velocity Changes

When you press the accelerator in an auto-rickshaw, the velocity increases. When you press the brake, the velocity decreases. Either way, the velocity is changing. Whenever velocity changes, we say the object is **accelerating**.

**Acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time taken**

**SI unit:** m/s² (metres per second squared).

If a car speeds up from 0 m/s to 20 m/s in 5 seconds, its acceleration is:

(20 − 0) / 5 = **4 m/s²**

This means its velocity increases by 4 m/s every second.

### Positive and negative acceleration

- If velocity is **increasing**, acceleration is **positive**.
- If velocity is **decreasing** (the object is slowing down), acceleration is **negative**. We sometimes call this **deceleration** or **retardation**.

> **Key idea:** **Acceleration** is the rate at which velocity changes. Positive acceleration means speeding up; negative acceleration (retardation) means slowing down. A turning object also accelerates because its direction of velocity changes.

## Describing Motion with Graphs

A picture is worth a thousand words. In physics, a **motion graph** can describe an entire journey at a glance.

### Position-time (or distance-time) graphs

On the **x-axis**: time. On the **y-axis**: position (or distance from start).

- A **horizontal line** means the object is at rest (position not changing).
- A **straight slanting line** means uniform motion (constant speed).
  - Steeper slope = faster motion.
- A **curve** means non-uniform motion (changing speed).

The slope of a position-time graph gives the speed.

### Velocity-time graphs

On the **x-axis**: time. On the **y-axis**: velocity.

- A **horizontal line** means uniform velocity (constant speed in one direction).
- A **straight slanting line** means uniform acceleration.
  - Steeper slope upward = faster acceleration.
  - Slope downward = deceleration.
- The **area under** a velocity-time graph gives the **distance travelled**.

> **Key idea:** Slope of a position-time graph = velocity. Slope of a velocity-time graph = acceleration. Area under a velocity-time graph = distance.

---

## Equations of Motion (Straight Line, Uniform Acceleration)

For motion in a straight line with constant acceleration, there are three useful equations. Let:

- **u** = initial velocity
- **v** = final velocity
- **a** = acceleration
- **t** = time
- **s** = displacement (distance covered)

The three equations:

**1.** v = u + at  
**2.** s = ut + ½at²  
**3.** v² = u² + 2as

Once you know any three of these quantities, you can find the other two.

### Worked example

A bus starts from rest and accelerates at 2 m/s² for 10 seconds. How far has it travelled, and what is its final velocity?

- u = 0 (starts from rest)
- a = 2 m/s²
- t = 10 s

Using **v = u + at**: v = 0 + (2)(10) = **20 m/s**

Using **s = ut + ½at²**: s = (0)(10) + ½(2)(10²) = **100 m**

---

## Uniform Circular Motion

Not all motion is in a straight line. Think of the ceiling fan, the hands of a clock, the moon going round the Earth, an athlete running on a circular track.

When an object moves along a circular path at constant speed, we say it has **uniform circular motion**.

Here is a subtle but important point -- even though the **speed** is constant, the **velocity** is constantly changing. Why? Because velocity has a direction, and on a circle, the direction of motion changes at every instant. Changing velocity means the object is **accelerating** even at constant speed.

This is one of the deeper insights in physics: an object moving in a circle is constantly accelerating, even if it never speeds up or slows down.

> **Key idea:** A ceiling fan blade is constantly accelerating even though it spins at constant rate. Constant speed plus changing direction = changing velocity = acceleration.

---

## Summary

- **Motion** = a change in position relative to a chosen reference point.
- **Distance** = total path length (no direction). **Displacement** = straight-line gap from start to end (with direction).
- **Speed** = distance ÷ time (scalar). **Velocity** = displacement ÷ time (vector).
- **Uniform motion** = equal distance in equal time. **Non-uniform motion** = changing speed.
- **Acceleration** = change in velocity ÷ time. SI unit: m/s². Negative acceleration = deceleration.
- **Position-time graph**: slope = velocity. **Velocity-time graph**: slope = acceleration, area = distance.
- Three equations of motion (uniform acceleration in a straight line):
  - v = u + at
  - s = ut + ½at²
  - v² = u² + 2as
- **Uniform circular motion** at constant speed still involves acceleration because direction changes.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Highway safety in India** is built around these equations. The Ministry of Road Transport sets stopping-distance rules so that a truck travelling at 80 km/h has time to brake before colliding with vehicles ahead. The "safe following distance" varies with speed because v² = u² + 2as -- doubling your speed means quadrupling your stopping distance.

**Cricket bowling speed gun**. When the speedometer behind the stumps flashes "146 km/h", it has used the same equations -- measuring the change in the ball's position over very small time intervals to calculate velocity.

**ISRO Mars Orbiter Mission**. To put a spacecraft in orbit around Mars, ISRO engineers had to know exactly when to accelerate, when to decelerate, and by how much. Every burn of the engine was calculated using the equations of motion -- now extended to three dimensions and to enormous distances.

### Try it at home — Measure your walking speed

**What you'll need:** A measuring tape (or a known distance like the length of a balcony), a phone with a stopwatch.

**Steps:**
1. Mark off 10 metres along a straight path (a corridor or a verandah works).
2. Walk normally from start to end while your phone times you.
3. Repeat at jogging pace, then running pace.
4. Record three times for each pace.

**Calculate:**
- **Walking speed** = 10 m ÷ time (in seconds) = ___ m/s
- Convert to km/h: multiply m/s by 3.6.
- A typical walking speed is about 1.2 m/s ≈ 4 km/h. Jogging is roughly 2.5 m/s ≈ 9 km/h. Sprinting (in short bursts) can reach 10 m/s ≈ 36 km/h.

You have just used the basic definition of speed in your own kitchen corridor.
