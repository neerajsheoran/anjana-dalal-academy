## What Is Matter?

**Child:** Maa, our teacher said everything around us is matter. But what about the wind? I cannot see it or hold it. Is wind also matter?

**Mother:** That is a wonderful question, beta. Yes, wind is matter. Even though you cannot see it, air has mass and takes up space. Think about it -- when you pump air into a bicycle tyre, the tyre puffs up and becomes heavier. That proves air has both mass and volume. The rule is simple: if something has mass and volume, it is matter.

**Child:** Then what about things like love or fear? They feel very real to me!

**Mother:** They are real experiences, but they are not matter. You cannot put love on a weighing scale or pour fear into a jar. Emotions, thoughts, dreams -- none of these have mass or volume, so science does not classify them as matter. Light, sound, and heat are also not matter -- they are forms of energy.

**Child:** Maa, the chapter talks about Panch Tatva. What is that?

**Mother:** Thousands of years ago, Indian philosophers said the entire universe is made from five elements -- prithvi, jal, agni, vayu, and akash. They called this the Panch Tatva. Greek thinkers had a similar idea. It was not a scientific theory in the modern sense, but it showed that our ancestors were deeply curious about the nature of the world. Today, we classify matter differently -- by its physical state and by its chemical nature.

## Particles and Their Behaviour

**Child:** The textbook says matter is made of tiny particles. But how do we know that if we cannot see them?

**Mother:** Think about what happens when I make chai. I add a spoonful of sugar to hot water, stir it, and the sugar disappears. But the chai tastes sweet, right? That sweetness tells us the sugar is still there -- it has broken into particles so tiny that they slip into the spaces between water particles. We cannot see them, but we can taste them!

**Child:** And the experiment with haldi?

**Mother:** Exactly. A pinch of haldi turns a whole bucket of water yellow. Even if you dilute that water again and again, the colour persists. One grain of haldi contains millions of particles. That is how incredibly small they are.

**Child:** Maa, why does the smell of your biryani reach my room before you even call me for dinner?

**Mother:** Because particles are always moving! The aromatic particles escape from the biryani, mix with air particles, and drift through the house. This mixing is called diffusion. And here is a fun fact -- hot food sends its smell farther and faster because heat gives particles more kinetic energy, making them zip around more quickly. On a cold morning, the smell of parathas does not travel as fast.

**Child:** And particles also attract each other?

**Mother:** Yes. There is a force of attraction between particles, but its strength varies. In an iron kadhai, the particles grip each other so tightly that you cannot break it with your hands. In a biscuit, the grip is weaker -- you can snap it easily. In water, the particles hold on just enough to stay together but still flow freely. These differences in attractive forces explain why substances behave so differently.

## The Three States of Matter

**Child:** So the three states are solid, liquid, and gas. But why do they behave differently? Why is a stone hard while water flows?

**Mother:** It all comes down to three things at the particle level: spacing, attractive forces, and kinetic energy. In solids, particles are packed tightly in neat rows with strong attraction -- they can only vibrate in place. That is why your wooden study table holds its shape. In liquids, particles are still close but can slide over each other -- that is why water pours from a jug. In gases, particles are far apart with almost no attraction, zooming around wildly -- that is why the air from a punctured balloon escapes in every direction.

**Child:** But Maa, what about a sponge? I can squeeze it -- is it not a solid?

**Mother:** The sponge itself is definitely a solid. Those tiny holes in it are filled with air. When you squeeze a sponge, you push the air out -- the solid part does not actually compress. This is a question examiners love because it tests whether you truly understand solids.

**Child:** And why does diffusion happen fastest in gases?

**Mother:** Because gas particles have the most space between them and the highest speed. Spray perfume in one corner of a room and you smell it across the room within seconds. But drop a spoonful of ink into a glass of water and it takes minutes to spread. In solids, diffusion is incredibly slow -- it can take years for atoms to creep through a solid block.

## Changing States: Melting, Boiling, and Beyond

**Child:** When I leave an ice cream cone out in the sun, it melts. And when Maa boils water for chai, it produces steam. What is happening to the particles?

**Mother:** When ice cream warms up, its particles absorb heat and vibrate faster. At the melting point, their energy overcomes the forces holding them in a rigid pattern, and they start flowing -- solid becomes liquid. When I heat water further, the particles move even faster until they have enough energy to break free completely and fly apart -- liquid becomes gas.

**Child:** But why does the temperature not rise while ice is melting? The flame is still on!

**Mother:** That is the clever concept of latent heat. During melting, all the incoming heat goes toward breaking the bonds between particles, not toward making them move faster. Since temperature measures kinetic energy, and kinetic energy is not increasing during melting, the temperature stays flat at 0 degrees Celsius until every bit of ice has turned to water. Only then does the temperature resume climbing.

**Child:** Is that why steam burns are worse than hot water burns?

**Mother:** Precisely! Steam at 100 degrees Celsius carries extra hidden energy -- the latent heat of vaporisation. When steam touches your skin, it first releases that latent heat as it condenses back to water, and then the hot water also gives off heat as it cools. So steam dumps much more energy onto your skin than boiling water at the same temperature. This concept is so important that thermal power plants use steam to spin turbines and generate electricity.

**Child:** What about naphthalene balls? They just vanish from the cupboard without leaving any liquid!

**Mother:** That is sublimation -- a direct jump from solid to gas, skipping the liquid stage entirely. Camphor does the same thing. If you place a camphor tablet near a Ganesh idol during puja, it slowly disappears over a few days. The solid particles gain enough energy to escape directly into the air. The reverse process -- gas turning directly to solid -- is called deposition.

## Evaporation and Cooling

**Child:** Maa, wet clothes dry even on a cloudy day when there is no boiling happening. How is that possible?

**Mother:** At any temperature, some particles at the surface of a liquid are lucky enough to have extra kinetic energy. They break free and escape into the air as vapour. This is evaporation, and it happens at every temperature, not just at the boiling point. Boiling converts the whole liquid at a specific temperature; evaporation quietly steals particles from the surface at any temperature.

**Child:** So that is why I feel chilly when I step out of a swimming pool?

**Mother:** Exactly, beta. The water on your skin evaporates, and the escaping particles take heat energy from your body. Your skin cools down. The same principle explains why the matka in Nani's house keeps water so refreshingly cool in May -- water seeps through the clay pores and evaporates on the outer surface, pulling heat from the water inside.

**Child:** What controls how fast evaporation happens?

**Mother:** Four key factors. First, surface area -- spread clothes flat on the line and they dry faster than if you bunch them up. Second, temperature -- clothes dry quicker on a scorching afternoon than on a cold morning. Third, humidity -- during the monsoon, the air is already loaded with moisture, so evaporation slows down and clothes stay damp for hours. Fourth, wind -- a strong breeze sweeps away the vapour hanging near the surface, letting more liquid particles escape. This is why hanging clothes near an open window on a breezy day works wonders.

## Temperature Scales and Practical Tips

**Child:** Why do scientists use Kelvin instead of Celsius?

**Mother:** The Kelvin scale begins at absolute zero -- the theoretical temperature at which particles have the least possible energy. There are no negative numbers on the Kelvin scale, which makes scientific equations cleaner. Converting is easy: just add 273 to a Celsius reading. So 0 degrees Celsius becomes 273 K, and 100 degrees Celsius becomes 373 K.

**Child:** Any final advice for preparing this chapter for exams, Maa?

**Mother:** Remember three important distinctions. First, boiling versus evaporation -- boiling is a bulk phenomenon at a fixed temperature, while evaporation is a surface phenomenon at any temperature. Second, know what happens at the particle level during every state change: melting, boiling, sublimation, and their reverses. Third, practise Celsius-to-Kelvin conversions until they feel automatic. And whenever you explain a concept, use a real-life Indian example -- matka cooling, drying clothes in the monsoon, camphor at the puja. Examiners love that connection between science and daily life.
