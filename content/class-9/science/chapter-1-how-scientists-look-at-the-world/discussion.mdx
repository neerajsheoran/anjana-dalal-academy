## Why "How We Know" Matters

**Student:** Sir, I have been reading the chapter and I am a little confused. What is the difference between "what we know" and "how we know"? I thought science was about facts.

**Mentor:** Good catch -- that is exactly the right question to start with. Facts are the *result*, but science is more interested in the *process* that produces those facts. Anyone can say "the earth goes around the sun". The harder, more useful question is -- how do we know? What observations were made, what models were built, what alternatives were ruled out? When you understand the process, you understand why the fact is reliable.

**Student:** So when you read a science textbook, the equations and definitions are just the end product?

**Mentor:** Exactly. Behind every equation in this book is a long story of observations, mistakes, arguments, and revisions. As you go through Class 9, you will learn many results -- Newton's laws, the structure of atoms, the cell theory. But always try to ask, "Why do we believe this?" That habit alone will turn you from a passive reader into an active scientist.

**Student:** Is that why the chapter mentions Meghnad Saha simplifying stars?

**Mentor:** Yes. Saha did not have a spaceship to fly into a star. He worked with photographs of starlight from a telescope. He built a model. He made predictions. He compared the predictions to what astronomers saw. That entire chain is the "how" -- and the result, an equation that connects star colour to temperature, is just the cherry on top.

## Models and What to Ignore

**Student:** I find one thing strange. The chapter says scientists deliberately ignore details. But ignoring details sounds careless. How can good science be careless?

**Mentor:** Excellent question -- and this is where many students get stuck. Ignoring details is not careless when it is done on purpose, for a specific reason, and the person doing it is aware of what was left out.

Think of a Google Map of Mumbai. The map ignores the colour of each building, the people walking on each footpath, the items inside every shop. If a map showed every single detail of Mumbai, it would be as large as Mumbai itself -- and completely useless! The map is useful precisely because it ignores almost everything and keeps only what helps you navigate.

**Student:** So a scientific model is like a map?

**Mentor:** Beautifully put. And just like there are different maps for different purposes -- road maps, metro maps, weather maps -- there are different scientific models for different questions. A physicist studying a falling stone ignores air resistance and uses a simple model. The same physicist studying a parachute jump cannot ignore air resistance -- it becomes the most important thing. Same scientist, same world, different model for different question.

**Student:** How do scientists know which details to ignore?

**Mentor:** Two ways. First, experience -- they know from previous problems which effects are usually small. Second, by checking afterwards -- if the simple model gives a good answer, the ignored details were probably small. If the model fails, you go back and put in the details you ignored. It is an iterative process.

## Units, Symbols, and Why They Matter

**Student:** Sir, I have a doubt about units. Why do we use kilograms? Why not just say "a lot of mass" or "a little mass"?

**Mentor:** Imagine you asked the dukandaar for "a lot of dal". He might give you 5 kg, you might have expected 1 kg, and now you have a problem. Words like "a lot" and "a little" mean different things to different people. A kilogram means exactly the same thing in every shop, every laboratory, every weighing scale on Earth. That is the power of a standard unit.

**Student:** Why kilograms specifically? Why not pounds, like the Americans use?

**Mentor:** That is the very question that brought down a passenger plane. In 1983, ground crew loaded fuel in pounds when the calculations called for kilograms. The pilots ran out of fuel mid-flight over Canada. They had to glide the plane to an emergency landing. No one was seriously hurt, but it was a frightening lesson.

The SI units -- kg, m, s, K -- are the global standard. Most countries, including India, use them everywhere. When pilots, doctors, engineers, and scientists all agree on units, mistakes like the Air Canada incident are far less likely.

**Student:** What about mathematics in science? I get a little nervous when equations appear.

**Mentor:** That is completely normal -- but try to think of equations as **compact sentences in a language**, not as walls to climb. The equation "Distance = Speed × Time" is just a short way of saying, "If you know how fast something is moving and for how long, you can figure out how far it has gone." If you read it as a sentence, the fear shrinks.

A good habit: whenever you see an equation in this book, first try to say it in plain English. If you can express it as a sentence, you understand it. The mathematics is just the precision tool, not the meaning itself.

## Laws, Theories, Predictions

**Student:** Sir, this might be a silly doubt, but what is the difference between a law and a theory? My friend told me "evolution is just a theory, so it might not be true."

**Mentor:** That is not a silly doubt at all -- it is a confusion that millions of people share. In everyday English, "theory" often means a guess. "I have a theory about who took my pen." That is the *opposite* of what theory means in science.

In science, a theory is a well-tested explanation that has survived a lot of checking. The atomic theory, the cell theory, the theory of evolution -- these are not guesses. They are the strongest, most carefully built explanations we have, supported by enormous amounts of evidence.

A law describes a pattern (what happens). A theory explains why the pattern occurs.

**Student:** Like Newton's laws describe how objects move, and atomic theory explains why elements combine?

**Mentor:** Exactly. Newton's laws say "if you push something, here is how its motion will change." That is the *what*. Atomic theory goes deeper -- it says "elements combine in fixed ratios because they are made of atoms that bond in specific ways." That is the *why*.

**Student:** And the chapter talked about predictions. How is a scientific prediction different from a guess?

**Mentor:** A guess is a hopeful claim with no backing. "I think it will rain tomorrow" without any reason is a guess. A prediction is a reasoned claim that follows from a model, a law, or a theory. "The IMD predicts rain over Mumbai tomorrow afternoon because the satellite shows a low pressure area moving in from the Arabian Sea, the humidity is over 85 per cent, and the wind speed and direction match the conditions before recent monsoon rains." That whole chain of reasoning is a prediction.

The other thing about a prediction is -- it can fail. And if it does, scientists go back and ask, "What did we get wrong?" A guess just disappears when it is wrong. A prediction leaves a useful trail behind it.

## Viral Claims, Eclipse Myths, and Critical Thinking

**Student:** Sir, my cousin sent me a WhatsApp forward saying eclipses make food poisonous. The chapter says it is wrong, but how can I argue with her? She believes it.

**Mentor:** This is exactly where Class 9 science becomes useful in real life. You do not need to argue -- you need to ask gentle questions. Try these:

"What physical change happens to food during an eclipse?" -- The answer is, basically none. An eclipse is just the moon casting a shadow on part of the Earth. Temperature does not drop sharply, no chemicals enter the food, and the time is short.

"If shadows made food harmful, would food kept indoors all evening be dangerous too?" -- Of course not. Indoor food is in shadow for hours every day.

"Have any scientists, including Indian scientists, ever measured this and shown that food becomes harmful?" -- No.

When the claim cannot survive even basic questions, you do not need to argue. You have shown that the claim does not stand on evidence.

**Student:** That is a much better way than arguing.

**Mentor:** And here is the harder lesson -- sometimes a claim *will* survive your questions. In that case, scientific thinking demands that you take it seriously, even if you do not like it. Critical thinking is not about disagreeing with everything -- it is about following evidence, whether or not the evidence agrees with your preferences.

## Estimation, and Why "Roughly Right" Beats "Exactly Wrong"

**Student:** The chapter mentioned estimation -- like guessing how much air we breathe. But why is rough estimation useful? Should we not always aim for exact answers?

**Mentor:** Exact answers are nice when you can get them. But many questions in real life do not have an exact answer easily within reach -- and a rough estimate is the most useful first step.

Imagine your father is told by a salesperson that a new water purifier will save him "lakhs of rupees" over its lifetime. Should he buy it?

A rough estimate -- how many years will it last, how much extra water purification typically costs per year, what is the price of the purifier -- tells him in two minutes whether the claim is plausible. If the salesperson's claim is in the right ballpark, your father can ask for the detailed numbers. If it is wildly off, your father can politely thank the salesperson and walk away. That is a real, daily use of estimation.

**Student:** It is like a sanity check.

**Mentor:** Exactly that. Engineers, doctors, judges, journalists -- all of them estimate constantly. They reach for precision when they need it. They estimate when speed matters more. A scientist who can only do precise calculations is half a scientist. A scientist who can also estimate is the whole package.

**Student:** So I should practise both?

**Mentor:** Both, yes. And start with estimation -- it is more fun, and it builds the intuition that makes precision possible later. Try estimating how many people are in a Mumbai local train compartment, how many drops of water fall on your hand if you cup it in a shower for five seconds, how many books are on your school library shelves. Each one trains a muscle that you will use for the rest of your life.

## Science Is a Human Story

**Student:** Sir, the chapter ends with the line, "Science is a human activity." But I have always imagined scientists as serious people in white coats, working alone in labs. Are they not very different from us?

**Mentor:** A few are. Most are not. Scientists are people who got curious about something and stayed honest with the evidence. That description fits a lot of people you know -- your grandmother who can tell from the smell whether a vegetable has gone bad, your uncle who notices which crops grow better in his field, your neighbour who diagnoses the family TV when it stops working.

Real scientists also argue, change their minds, ask each other for help, and sometimes get things spectacularly wrong before getting them right. C.V. Raman tried many ideas before he discovered the effect that won him the Nobel Prize. Tessy Thomas, the woman who led the Agni missile programme, had to push through years of doubt about whether a woman could lead a defence project.

**Student:** So science is something I am already doing in small ways?

**Mentor:** Whenever you ask "why?", check whether something is true, or compare two ideas honestly -- yes, you are doing science. This chapter just gives names and structure to those habits. By the end of Class 9, you will recognise yourself as a scientific thinker. By the end of Class 10, you will have the toolkit to take it further if you choose. Either way, the habits are yours for life.

**Student:** Thank you, sir. The chapter makes much more sense now.

**Mentor:** Good. Keep asking, keep checking, keep being honest with what you find. That is the whole of science in nine words.
