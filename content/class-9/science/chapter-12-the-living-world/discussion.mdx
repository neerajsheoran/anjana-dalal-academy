## Why So Many Species?

**Meera:** Arjun, the chapter says India alone has over 100,000 known species. How does so much fit in one country?

**Arjun:** Because India is geographically wild — Himalayas, Thar desert, Sundarbans mangroves, Western Ghats, Andaman corals. Each habitat evolved its own species. The Ghats alone have hundreds of frog species found nowhere else.

**Meera:** And worldwide?

**Arjun:** Tens of millions, probably. Most still unnamed, especially in tropical rainforests and the deep ocean. Earth has been habitable for about 4 billion years. Every generation produces slight variations, and over that vast time life has explored almost every body plan that works.

**Meera:** So different environments favour different forms?

**Arjun:** Yes. A desert favours forms that conserve water — camels, cacti, certain reptiles. A rainforest favours forms that compete for light — tall trees, climbers, vivid flowers. The deep ocean favours pressure-tolerant forms that can live without sunlight.

**Meera:** And the chapter says evolution did not just branch once. It has branched, re-branched, even converged.

**Arjun:** Right. Bats and birds both fly, but they evolved flight independently. Dolphins look like fish but are mammals. Each experiment that survived added to the species count.

**Meera:** Discovery is still happening. I read that scientists found new frog species in the Western Ghats just a few years ago.

**Arjun:** Every year, new species are discovered in our own country. The Zoological Survey of India and various universities are constantly adding to the list. The living world is much richer than any textbook can capture.

## Why Classify?

**Meera:** Arjun, a tiger is a tiger. Why do we need to call it *Panthera tigris*? Is it just to make biology harder for board prep?

**Arjun:** Ha. Actually, classification solves real problems. First, it lets scientists communicate across languages. A researcher in Brazil and a researcher in India can both say *Panthera tigris* and know exactly which species. No translation needed.

**Meera:** That makes sense for international research papers.

**Arjun:** Second, classification reflects evolutionary relationships. Look at the genus *Panthera*. Tigers, lions, leopards, jaguars — they all share the genus name. That tells you they share a common ancestor and many traits. They are biological cousins.

**Meera:** So the name itself carries information about the family tree.

**Arjun:** Yes. Third, classification helps you predict properties. The moment a scientist tells you a new species is a bird, you immediately know it has feathers, lays eggs, is warm-blooded, has a beak. You do not have to study each new bird from scratch.

**Meera:** And fourth?

**Arjun:** It is the foundation of all the rest of biology. Ecology, evolution, genetics, conservation — every one of them needs you to first agree on what species you are talking about.

**Meera:** Okay, so classification is not just a list to memorise. It is biology's framework.

**Arjun:** Exactly. It is how biologists organise the chaos of life into something they can actually study.

## The Five Kingdoms

**Meera:** Arjun, why five kingdoms? Why not three or ten?

**Arjun:** Because the system keeps getting refined as scientific knowledge grows. Linnaeus originally had only two — plants and animals. That was 18th century thinking.

**Meera:** And then?

**Arjun:** Microscopes improved, and scientists realised bacteria were so different from plants and animals that they needed their own kingdom. Later, it became clear that fungi were not plants either — their cell walls are made of chitin, not cellulose, and they cannot photosynthesise.

**Meera:** So the current five are bacteria, protista, fungi, plants, animals?

**Arjun:** Yes. Whittaker proposed this system in the 1960s. Monera for bacteria, no nucleus. Protista for single-celled eukaryotes like amoeba and paramecium. Fungi for multicellular decomposers. Plantae for multicellular autotrophs with cellulose walls. Animalia for multicellular heterotrophs that can move.

**Meera:** And modern molecular biology has gone further, na?

**Arjun:** Yes. There is now a three-domain system — Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya — based on DNA differences. But for our school level, the five-kingdom system gives a clear first picture.

**Meera:** So it might change again as biologists learn more?

**Arjun:** Almost definitely. Science is always refining its categories. Five kingdoms is the best summary for now, not the final answer.

## Why Are Insects So Successful?

**Meera:** Arjun, the chapter says insects make up about 80% of all animal species. Why so many?

**Arjun:** Several reasons working together. Their exoskeleton — the hard outer shell — protects them and stops them losing water. That is huge. It let insects colonise dry land in ways most other animals could not.

**Meera:** So being small and tough is an advantage?

**Arjun:** Yes, and being small is itself an advantage. A single neem tree can support thousands of insect species without them interfering with each other. Each finds its own little niche on the bark, leaves, flowers, fruits.

**Meera:** And their reproduction is insane.

**Arjun:** Most insects lay many eggs and reproduce fast. A pair of houseflies could theoretically produce a million offspring in a few months if all survived. Speed and numbers.

**Meera:** Plus flight, right?

**Arjun:** Yes. Wings let them escape predators, find food, colonise new habitats, spread across continents. Most insect groups have winged forms at some point in their life cycle.

**Meera:** I learnt the word metamorphosis in primary school but never thought of it as an advantage.

**Arjun:** It is a huge one. A butterfly's caterpillar eats leaves. The adult drinks nectar. Same species, two completely different food sources, two different habitats. One species effectively exploits two niches without competing with itself between life stages.

**Meera:** So all of this together — armour, small size, fast reproduction, flight, metamorphosis — that is why insects are about 80% of animal species.

**Arjun:** Yes. And in India alone, the diversity is enormous and still largely uncatalogued. The Western Ghats and Northeast India are insect biodiversity hotspots. Entomologists at the Zoological Survey of India find new species regularly.

## India's Biodiversity — A Treasure

**Meera:** Arjun, our geography teacher last year called India a megadiverse country. Why is our biodiversity so high?

**Arjun:** Because we have nearly every climate and landscape packed into one country. Snowy Himalayan peaks. Cold deserts in Ladakh. The hot Thar desert. The Gangetic plains. Both ghats — Western and Eastern. The Deccan plateau. Coastal mangroves. Tropical rainforests in the Northeast and Kerala. Each landscape supports its own specially adapted species.

**Meera:** And the chapter mentions India's deep evolutionary history.

**Arjun:** Yes — India was once a separate continent that drifted into Asia about 50 million years ago. During that long drift, many species evolved in isolation. That is why several Indian species are endemic — found nowhere else on Earth.

**Meera:** Like the lion-tailed macaque in the Western Ghats?

**Arjun:** Exactly. And the Malabar giant squirrel, hundreds of frog species, many flowering plants. All endemic to the Ghats. If we lose that habitat, those species disappear from Earth forever.

**Meera:** That is sobering. And the Ghats are under serious pressure from highways, mining, hill stations.

**Arjun:** Yes. Habitat loss, climate change, and poaching are all eroding what we have. Project Tiger and Project Elephant, the network of national parks and sanctuaries — they are real efforts to slow it down, but they are not enough on their own.

**Meera:** I read the Chipko movement in social studies. Villagers in Uttarakhand hugging trees to stop them being cut. That was decades before "environment" became a global topic.

**Arjun:** Yes. India has a long history of local communities protecting their environment. Now we need that spirit at national and international scale.

**Meera:** What can we actually do as Class 9 students?

**Arjun:** Pay attention. Every time we visit a park or a hill station or a beach, ask which species live there, are they thriving or declining, what is the local conservation story. Biology becomes meaningful when we connect it to the real world.

**Meera:** And the choices being made in the next few years — about forests, urban planning, pollution — will decide what is left when we are adults.

**Arjun:** Which is why the chapter is not just exam content. The biodiversity around us is something we are about to inherit.
