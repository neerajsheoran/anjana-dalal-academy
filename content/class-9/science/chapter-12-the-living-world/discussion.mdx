## Why So Many Species?

**Student:** Sir, the chapter says there might be tens of millions of species on Earth. Why so many?

**Mentor:** Because life is incredibly creative. Earth has been habitable for about 4 billion years. In that vast time, with reproduction producing slight variations every generation, life has explored almost every possible body plan that works.

Different environments favour different forms. A desert favours forms that conserve water. A rainforest favours forms that compete for light. A deep ocean favours forms adapted to high pressure and no sunlight. Over billions of years, life has filled every niche.

Add to this the fact that life is not just one branching tree — it has branched, re-branched, and sometimes converged in surprising ways. Bats and birds both fly, but evolved flight independently. Dolphins look fish-like but are mammals. Each evolutionary experiment that survived added to the count.

India alone has over 100,000 known species. The total worldwide is hard to pin down because most species — particularly in tropical rainforests and the deep ocean — have not even been named yet.

**Student:** That is hard to imagine.

**Mentor:** Yes. The living world is far richer than any single classroom can convey. The Western Ghats alone contain hundreds of frog species, many found nowhere else. We are still discovering new species in our own country every year.

## Why Classify?

**Student:** Sir, why bother classifying things? If a tiger is a tiger, why call it *Panthera tigris*?

**Mentor:** For several reasons. First, classification lets us communicate clearly across language barriers. A scientist in Brazil and a scientist in India can both say "Panthera tigris" and know exactly what species is being discussed — without needing a translator.

Second, classification reflects RELATIONSHIPS. The fact that tigers (*Panthera tigris*), lions (*Panthera leo*), leopards (*Panthera pardus*), and jaguars (*Panthera onca*) all share the genus name *Panthera* tells you they are closely related — they share a common ancestor and many traits.

Third, classification helps us PREDICT properties. When a scientist discovers a new species of bird, they immediately know it has feathers, wings, lays eggs, has a beak, is warm-blooded — because it is a bird. Classification gives a quick framework for understanding any organism by its group.

Fourth, classification is the foundation of biology. Every advanced topic — ecology, evolution, genetics, conservation — depends on knowing what species we are talking about and how they relate.

**Student:** So classification is a tool, not just a list?

**Mentor:** Exactly. It is biology's framework for organising and understanding life.

## The Five Kingdoms

**Student:** Sir, why exactly five kingdoms? Why not three or ten?

**Mentor:** Classification has evolved over time as scientific knowledge has grown. Linnaeus originally proposed just two kingdoms — plants and animals. As microscopes improved, scientists realised that bacteria were so different from both that they needed their own kingdom. Then it became clear that fungi were not plants either — they have completely different cell walls (chitin instead of cellulose) and do not photosynthesise.

Today's five-kingdom system was proposed by R.H. Whittaker in the 1960s. The five are:

1. MONERA — bacteria; simplest, no nucleus.
2. PROTISTA — single-celled eukaryotes (amoeba, paramecium).
3. FUNGI — multicellular decomposers with chitin walls.
4. PLANTAE — multicellular autotrophs with cellulose walls.
5. ANIMALIA — multicellular heterotrophs with no walls, capable of locomotion.

Modern molecular biology has further refined this into a three-domain system (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya), but for school biology, the five-kingdom system gives a good first picture.

**Student:** And it might change again as we learn more?

**Mentor:** Almost certainly. Science is always being refined. The current five kingdoms are the best summary for now, but new discoveries about DNA, cell structure, and evolutionary relationships continue to shape biology.

## Why Are Insects So Successful?

**Student:** Sir, the chapter says insects are about 80% of all animal species. Why so many?

**Mentor:** Insects are extraordinarily successful for several reasons.

Their EXOSKELETON — the hard outer covering — provides protection and prevents water loss. This let insects colonise dry land in a way most other animals could not.

Their SIZE — being small means they can find food and shelter in places larger animals cannot. A single tree can support thousands of insect species without their interfering with each other.

Their REPRODUCTION — most insects lay many eggs and reproduce rapidly. A pair of houseflies could theoretically produce over a million offspring in just a few months, if all survived.

Their FLIGHT — wings let many insects escape predators, find new habitats, and disperse over wide areas.

Their METAMORPHOSIS — many insects have different larval and adult stages that eat different foods and occupy different habitats. A butterfly's caterpillar eats leaves; the adult drinks nectar. So one species can exploit two food sources without competition between life stages.

All this adds up. Insects have been around for over 400 million years and have radiated into every habitat on land and many in fresh water. There are over 1 million named insect species and probably 5-10 million yet to be named.

**Student:** And in India?

**Mentor:** Indian insect biodiversity is enormous and largely uncatalogued. Entomologists at the Zoological Survey of India and various universities discover new species regularly. The Western Ghats and Northeast India are especially rich.

## India's Biodiversity — A Treasure

**Student:** Why is India's biodiversity so high?

**Mentor:** India is geographically remarkable. We have nearly every climate and landscape packed into one country: snowy Himalayan peaks; high cold deserts in Ladakh; the hot Thar desert; the Gangetic plains; the Western and Eastern Ghats; the Deccan plateau; the dry Andhra-Karnataka plateau; the coastal mangroves; the tropical rainforests of the northeast and Kerala. Each environment supports its own specially adapted species.

India also has a long evolutionary history. India was once a separate continent that drifted into Asia about 50 million years ago. During its drift, many species evolved in isolation — that is why some Indian species are found nowhere else (called endemic species).

The Western Ghats alone are home to thousands of species found only there. The lion-tailed macaque, the Malabar giant squirrel, hundreds of frog species, many flowering plants — all endemic to the Ghats. Lose the Ghats, and these species disappear from Earth forever.

**Student:** That is sobering.

**Mentor:** Yes. And it is why protection matters so much. Habitat loss, climate change, and poaching are eroding India's biological treasure. Project Tiger, Project Elephant, the network of national parks and sanctuaries — these are all efforts to slow the loss.

You as a student today will inherit whatever biodiversity remains when you are an adult. The choices made now — about forest cover, pollution, urban planning — will determine what nature looks like for your generation and the next.

**Student:** Thank you, sir. I will look at our environment differently from now on.

**Mentor:** Excellent. Every time you go to a park, a hill station, or a beach, ask yourself — what species live here? Are they thriving or declining? What can I do to help? Biology becomes meaningful when it connects to the real world around you.
