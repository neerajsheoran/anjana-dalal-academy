## How Cells Were First Spotted

Imagine peeling the bark off a tree and slicing it paper-thin. That is exactly what **Robert Hooke** did in 1665. He placed a sliver of cork beneath his handmade magnifying instrument and noticed hundreds of tiny hollow boxes arranged like a honeycomb. These reminded him of the small chambers monks lived in, so he named them **cells** (from the Latin "cella," meaning a small room).

What Hooke saw were actually the empty walls of dead plant cells. Living cells would be observed a few years later.

### Milestones in Understanding Cells

- **Robert Hooke (1665)** -- Spotted cell walls in cork for the first time
- **Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (1674)** -- Observed tiny living creatures in pond water through a more powerful lens he crafted himself
- **Robert Brown (1831)** -- Identified a dense, round body inside cells and named it the **nucleus**
- **Purkinje (1839)** -- Gave the name **protoplasm** to the jelly-like living material filling the cell
- **Schleiden (1838) & Schwann (1839)** -- Put forward the **cell theory**: every plant and animal is built from cells, and the cell is life's basic unit
- **Virchow (1855)** -- Added a crucial idea: every new cell originates from an already existing cell
- **Electron microscope (around 1940)** -- Opened the door to seeing the fine internal architecture of cells

## Living Things and Their Cellular Make-Up

Every organism -- from a banyan tree in a village square to a fish swimming in the Ganga -- is constructed from **cells**. The cell serves as both the building block (structural unit) and the working unit (functional unit) of life.

### One Cell vs Many Cells

- **Unicellular organisms** -- A single cell carries out every task: eating, breathing, growing, reproducing
  - Think of Amoeba, Paramecium, or the bacteria that curdle milk into dahi
- **Multicellular organisms** -- Billions of cells cooperate, each group handling a specific job
  - A mango tree, a cow, or a human being

Every multicellular creature begins its journey as one single cell that keeps dividing. No cell appears out of nowhere; each one comes from a cell that already exists.

### Why Cells Look Different

Cells take on shapes that suit the work they do:
- **Nerve cells** stretch out long, branching arms so they can relay signals across the body quickly
- **Red blood cells** are smooth, round discs that glide easily through narrow blood vessels
- **Muscle cells** are slim and elongated, built for pulling and contracting
- **Amoeba** has no fixed outline -- it constantly reshapes itself to creep and capture food
- **White blood cells** can squeeze and change form to surround invading germs

> **Key idea:** A cell's shape is a direct reflection of its job. Despite looking different, every cell shares the same core internal components.

## How a Cell Is Organised

Strip away the details, and every cell has three essential parts:
1. **Plasma membrane** (cell membrane)
2. **Nucleus**
3. **Cytoplasm**

## The Plasma Membrane -- The Cell's Security Gate

The **plasma membrane** wraps around every cell like a thin, flexible boundary. It decides what gets in and what stays out.

### What It Is Made Of and How It Works

- Built from **lipids and proteins**
- **Selectively permeable** -- lets certain molecules through while blocking others, much like a security guard at a gated colony who checks every visitor
- **Flexible** enough to stretch and fold, allowing cells like Amoeba to engulf food
- So thin it can only be seen clearly with an **electron microscope**

### How Substances Cross the Membrane

#### Diffusion

**Diffusion** is the natural drift of molecules from a crowded region to a less crowded one.

- **Carbon dioxide**, a waste product, builds up inside the cell and drifts out through the membrane to where its concentration is lower
- **Oxygen**, needed for energy release, enters the cell from the outside where it is more abundant
- This movement is vital for the **exchange of gases** between a cell and its surroundings

#### Osmosis

**Osmosis** is the movement of **water molecules** across a selectively permeable membrane, flowing from where water is plentiful to where it is scarce.

Place a cell in different surroundings and watch what happens:

![Cell in hypotonic, isotonic, and hypertonic solutions showing swelling, normal size, and shrinking respectively](osmosis-cell-in-solutions.png)

- **Hypotonic solution** (water concentration higher outside): Water rushes in, and the cell **puffs up**
- **Isotonic solution** (water concentration equal on both sides): Water moves in and out at the same rate; cell size **stays unchanged**
- **Hypertonic solution** (water concentration lower outside): Water flows out, and the cell **shrivels**

**Everyday illustrations of osmosis:**
- Drop a handful of dry kishmish (raisins) into a bowl of water -- they plump up as water seeps in
- Peel a raw egg, place it in plain water, and it swells; put it in a strong salt solution, and it contracts
- Roots of a tulsi plant pull water from damp soil through osmosis

#### Endocytosis

Certain cells, such as **Amoeba**, wrap their flexible membrane around a food particle and swallow it whole. This engulfing action is called **endocytosis**.

> **Key idea:** The plasma membrane acts as a selective gatekeeper. Diffusion and osmosis are the two chief methods by which substances travel across it without the cell spending any energy.

## The Cell Wall -- A Rigid Jacket for Plants

**Plant cells** wear an extra layer called the **cell wall** on the outside of their plasma membrane.

### Features of the Cell Wall

- Composed mainly of **cellulose**, a tough carbohydrate
- Gives the plant **structural support** and a **fixed shape**
- **Freely permeable** -- does not pick and choose what passes through
- Prevents the cell from bursting when it absorbs too much water in a **dilute environment**

### Plasmolysis -- When Plant Cells Lose Water

Place a living plant cell in a **concentrated sugar or salt solution** (hypertonic). Water leaves the cell through osmosis. The inner contents shrink and pull away from the rigid cell wall, like a deflating balloon inside a cardboard box. This phenomenon is **plasmolysis**.

> **Key idea:** Plasmolysis only takes place in living cells. Dead cells have lost their selectively permeable membrane, so controlled water movement no longer occurs.

## The Nucleus -- The Command Centre

The **nucleus** runs the show inside a cell. **Robert Brown** first described it in 1831.

### Inside the Nucleus

- Enclosed by a **double-layered nuclear membrane** dotted with tiny pores for moving materials in and out
- Houses **chromosomes** -- structures shaped like short rods, visible just before the cell divides
- Chromosomes are woven from **DNA** (Deoxyribonucleic Acid) wrapped around **proteins**
- **DNA** holds the master instructions for building the cell and passing traits from parent to offspring
- Individual instruction units on DNA are called **genes**
- Between divisions, DNA spreads out into fine threads known as **chromatin**

### What the Nucleus Does

- Oversees every **chemical reaction** happening in the cell
- Guides the cell as it **grows and matures**
- Orchestrates **cell division** so the organism can grow or reproduce
- Safeguards **hereditary information** and hands it down through generations

### Two Broad Categories of Cells

| Feature | Prokaryotic Cell | Eukaryotic Cell |
|---|---|---|
| **Typical size** | Tiny (1--10 micrometres) | Larger (5--100 micrometres) |
| **Nuclear region** | No true membrane around DNA; region called nucleoid | Clearly defined nucleus wrapped in a nuclear membrane |
| **Number of chromosomes** | Usually one | More than one |
| **Membrane-bound organelles** | Not present | Present |
| **Common examples** | Bacteria, blue-green algae | Plants, animals, fungi |

> **Key idea:** Prokaryotes (like the bacteria in curd) lack a defined nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. Eukaryotes (like the cells of a neem tree or a human) possess both.

## Cytoplasm -- The Cell's Workshop Floor

The **cytoplasm** is the thick, gel-like fluid that fills the space between the plasma membrane and the nucleus. It is the medium in which all organelles float and carry out their tasks.

---

## Cell Organelles -- Specialised Mini-Organs

Each organelle inside a eukaryotic cell has a dedicated role, much like different departments in a large factory.

### 1. Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)

The **ER** is an extensive network of folded membranes stretching through the cytoplasm like a system of internal roads.

**Two varieties:**
- **Rough ER (RER)** -- Studded with **ribosomes** on its surface; its main job is **assembling proteins**
- **Smooth ER (SER)** -- Lacks ribosomes; specialises in manufacturing **lipids** (fats)

**What the ER does:**
- Shuttles materials -- especially freshly made proteins -- from one part of the cell to another
- **Membrane biogenesis** -- produces the lipids and proteins needed to build new cell membranes
- **Detoxification** -- in liver cells, the SER breaks down harmful substances and medicines
- Provides a **scaffold** on which many biochemical reactions take place

### 2. Golgi Apparatus

First described by Italian scientist **Camillo Golgi**, this organelle is a stack of flattened, membrane-enclosed pouches called **cisternae**.

**What it does:**
- **Wraps and labels** products made by the ER, much like a courier service packaging parcels for delivery
- **Stores and fine-tunes** these products before dispatching them
- Builds **complex sugars** from simpler ones
- Produces **lysosomes**

> **Key idea:** Picture the Golgi apparatus as the cell's dispatch centre -- it sorts, packs, and ships materials to the correct address inside or outside the cell.

### 3. Lysosomes

**Lysosomes** are small, membrane-wrapped bags loaded with potent **digestive enzymes** (manufactured by the RER).

**Their roles:**
- **Break down foreign invaders** such as bacteria that sneak into the cell
- **Recycle worn-out organelles** that are no longer functioning
- **Clean up cellular debris**

They earn the nickname **"suicide bags"** because if a cell is badly damaged, the lysosome membranes rupture, spilling enzymes that dissolve the entire cell from within.

### 4. Mitochondria

Often called the **"powerhouses"** of the cell, **mitochondria** are where energy is unlocked from food.

**How they are built:**
- Surrounded by a **double membrane**
- The outer membrane is smooth and porous
- The inner membrane folds inward into ridges called **cristae**, vastly increasing the surface area available for energy-releasing reactions
- Contain their own **DNA and ribosomes**, enabling them to produce some of their own proteins independently

![Cross-section of a mitochondrion showing outer membrane, inner membrane with cristae, and matrix](mitochondria-structure-diagram.png)

**What they do:**
- Convert nutrients into **ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate)**, the molecule every cell uses as its **energy currency**
- The cell spends ATP on building new molecules, moving materials, and powering physical work

### 5. Plastids

**Plastids** are found **exclusively in plant cells**. They come in two broad types:

- **Chromoplasts** (coloured plastids):
  - **Chloroplasts** hold the green pigment **chlorophyll** and are the sites of **photosynthesis**
  - Other chromoplasts carry yellow or orange pigments, painting flowers and fruits with colour

- **Leucoplasts** (colourless plastids):
  - Act as storage units for **starch, oils, and proteins**

**Chloroplast structure:**
- Wrapped in a double membrane
- Filled with stacks of internal membranes bathed in a fluid called **stroma**
- Like mitochondria, they carry their own **DNA and ribosomes**

> **Key idea:** Mitochondria and plastids stand apart from other organelles because they possess their own genetic material and protein-making machinery -- a hint that they may once have been independent organisms.

### 6. Vacuoles

**Vacuoles** are fluid-filled storage compartments.

- **In animal cells** -- typically small and few
- **In plant cells** -- a single massive vacuole can fill 50--90 % of the cell's volume

**What they do:**
- In plants: filled with **cell sap** (a mixture of sugars, salts, amino acids), they keep the cell firm and **turgid**
- In **Amoeba**: food vacuoles temporarily hold captured food for digestion
- In some single-celled pond organisms: contractile vacuoles pump out excess water to maintain balance

## Plant Cell vs Animal Cell -- A Quick Comparison

| Feature | Plant Cell | Animal Cell |
|---|---|---|
| **Cell wall** | Present (cellulose) | Absent |
| **Plastids** | Present (chloroplasts, leucoplasts) | Absent |
| **Vacuoles** | One large central vacuole | Small or absent |
| **Centrosome/Centrioles** | Usually absent | Present |
| **Shape** | Typically rectangular, rigid | Usually rounded or irregular |

## Cell Division -- Making New Cells

The body needs new cells for three main reasons:
- **Growth** -- a baby growing into an adult
- **Repair** -- healing a scraped knee
- **Reproduction** -- producing egg and sperm cells

### Two Modes of Division

**1. Mitosis** -- The parent cell splits into **two daughter cells**, each carrying the **same number of chromosomes** as the original.
- Purpose: **growth and tissue repair**

**2. Meiosis** -- A special division that yields **four daughter cells**, each with **half the chromosome count** of the parent.
- Purpose: producing **gametes** (sperm and ova)
- Involves **two back-to-back divisions**

> **Key idea:** Mitosis keeps the chromosome number steady (for growing bigger), while meiosis cuts it in half (for reproduction). When two gametes unite during fertilisation, the full count is restored.

## Key Terms to Remember

- **Cell** -- The smallest living unit that forms the structure and carries out functions of every organism
- **Plasma membrane** -- The selectively permeable outer boundary of a cell
- **Cell wall** -- A stiff outer layer in plant cells, built from cellulose
- **Nucleus** -- The control hub housing DNA
- **Cytoplasm** -- The gel-like interior where organelles operate
- **Endoplasmic reticulum** -- A membrane network for transport and synthesis
- **Golgi apparatus** -- The packaging and shipping department of the cell
- **Lysosomes** -- Enzyme-filled sacs that digest waste and invaders ("suicide bags")
- **Mitochondria** -- Energy generators ("powerhouses") producing ATP
- **Plastids** -- Plant-exclusive organelles; chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis
- **Vacuoles** -- Storage sacs; enormous in plant cells
- **Osmosis** -- Water movement through a selectively permeable membrane
- **Diffusion** -- Movement of molecules from high to low concentration
- **Prokaryotic** -- A cell without a membrane-bound nucleus (e.g., bacteria)
- **Eukaryotic** -- A cell with a true nucleus (e.g., plant and animal cells)
- **Mitosis** -- Division producing two identical daughter cells
- **Meiosis** -- Division producing four cells with half the chromosome number
