## Introduction

Dalton pictured atoms as solid, unbreakable spheres -- the ultimate building blocks. But by the late 1800s, experiments began to reveal that atoms themselves harbour an inner world of even tinier particles called **sub-atomic particles**. This chapter traces the discovery of these particles and the evolving models scientists built to describe what goes on inside an atom.

---

## Electric Charges Lurking Inside Matter

Rub a plastic ruler on your woollen sweater and bring it near torn bits of paper -- the paper leaps up and clings to the ruler. Rub a glass rod with a silk cloth and it attracts a deflated balloon. These familiar tricks reveal that matter contains **electrical charges** that can be separated by friction.

### How Electrons Were Found

**J.J. Thomson** (1856-1940), working at Cambridge University, passed electricity through gases sealed in glass tubes and discovered a stream of **negatively charged particles** shooting out regardless of which gas was used. He had found the **electron** -- present inside every atom of every element. Thomson received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1906.

### How Protons Were Found

In 1886, **E. Goldstein** noticed a different set of rays in a modified gas discharge tube -- rays travelling in the opposite direction to electrons and carrying a **positive charge**. These were named **canal rays**, and they led to the identification of the **proton**.

### Vital Statistics of Electrons and Protons

| Property | Electron (e-) | Proton (p+) |
|---|---|---|
| **Charge** | -1 (negative) | +1 (positive) |
| **Mass** | Extremely tiny (roughly 1/1840 of a proton) | 1 u (one atomic mass unit) |
| **Where found** | Orbiting outside the nucleus | Packed inside the nucleus |

> **Key idea:** A neutral atom has the same count of electrons and protons, so the positive and negative charges cancel out exactly.

## Models of the Atom

### Thomson's Model (The Watermelon Model)

Thomson proposed the first picture of atomic structure:

- Imagine the atom as a **ball of positive charge** spread uniformly throughout
- **Electrons** are scattered within this positive mass, like seeds dotted inside a watermelon
- The positive and negative charges are **equal**, keeping the atom **neutral** overall

**Why it was eventually set aside:** Thomson's model could not account for what Rutherford observed a few years later in his celebrated gold-foil experiment.

---

### Rutherford's Model (The Nuclear Model)

**Ernest Rutherford** (1871-1937), hailed as the "Father of Nuclear Physics," devised one of history's most illuminating experiments.

#### The Alpha-Particle Scattering Experiment

Rutherford aimed a beam of **alpha particles** (helium nuclei, each with mass 4 u and a double positive charge) at an extremely thin sheet of **gold foil** -- roughly 1000 atoms in thickness.

**What he saw:**
1. The **overwhelming majority** of alpha particles sailed straight through the foil as if nothing were there
2. A **small fraction** veered off course, deflecting through moderate angles
3. An **astonishingly tiny number** (roughly 1 out of every 12,000) ricocheted straight back the way they came

**What this meant:**
1. The interior of an atom is **almost entirely empty space** (most particles met no obstacle)
2. The positive charge is concentrated in a **very small region** (only a few particles were repelled)
3. Virtually all the atom's mass and all its positive charge sit in a minuscule, ultra-dense core -- the **nucleus**

#### Rutherford's Atomic Picture

From his data, Rutherford concluded:

- A tiny, dense, positively charged **nucleus** sits at the atom's heart, holding nearly all of the atom's mass
- **Electrons orbit** the nucleus along circular paths
- The nucleus is roughly **100,000 times smaller** than the atom as a whole -- if the atom were a cricket stadium, the nucleus would be a marble at the centre pitch

**The flaw:** Classical physics dictates that any charged particle racing in a circle must continuously emit energy as radiation. An orbiting electron should therefore steadily lose energy, spiral inward, and crash into the nucleus within a fraction of a second. The atom would collapse. Yet atoms are clearly stable -- we and everything around us exist! Rutherford's model could not resolve this contradiction.

> **Key idea:** Rutherford's experiment conclusively demonstrated that the atom is mostly empty space surrounding a minuscule, heavy, positively charged nucleus.

---

### Bohr's Model (Energy-Level Model)

**Niels Bohr** (1885-1962), a Danish physicist and Nobel laureate (1922), tackled Rutherford's stability problem head-on with a bold new proposal.

**Bohr's key postulates:**

1. Electrons travel only in certain **permitted circular orbits** called **shells** or **energy levels**
2. As long as an electron stays within its designated orbit, it **does not lose or gain energy**
3. An electron **jumps** to a higher orbit by absorbing a precise packet of energy, or **drops** to a lower orbit by releasing one

**The shells are labelled:**
- **K shell** (n = 1) -- nearest to the nucleus
- **L shell** (n = 2)
- **M shell** (n = 3)
- **N shell** (n = 4) -- and further outward

> **Key idea:** Bohr resolved the stability puzzle: electrons in their designated orbits do not radiate energy. Energy transfer occurs only during a jump between orbits.

## The Neutron

In 1932, **James Chadwick** identified a third sub-atomic particle residing inside the nucleus -- the **neutron**.

**Neutron facts:**
- Carries **zero charge** (electrically neutral)
- Has mass approximately **equal to a proton** (1 u)
- Found in the nucleus of every element **except ordinary hydrogen** (protium, which has no neutron)

### The Three Building Blocks of Atoms

| Particle | Symbol | Charge | Mass | Location |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electron | e- | -1 | ~1/1840 u (negligible) | Shells around the nucleus |
| Proton | p+ | +1 | 1 u | Inside the nucleus |
| Neutron | n | 0 | 1 u | Inside the nucleus |

> **Key idea:** Protons and neutrons, collectively called **nucleons**, account for almost the entire mass of an atom. Electrons contribute virtually nothing to the mass.

## How Electrons Fill the Shells

**Bohr and Bury** laid out systematic rules for distributing electrons across the shells of an atom.

### The Three Rules

**Rule 1:** Each shell can accommodate at most **2n squared** electrons, where n is the shell number.

| Shell | n | Maximum Electrons (2n squared) |
|---|---|---|
| K | 1 | 2 x 1 x 1 = **2** |
| L | 2 | 2 x 2 x 2 = **8** |
| M | 3 | 2 x 3 x 3 = **18** |
| N | 4 | 2 x 4 x 4 = **32** |

**Rule 2:** Regardless of the 2n squared formula, the **outermost shell** never holds more than **8 electrons**.

**Rule 3:** Electrons occupy the **innermost available shell first** before moving to the next one outward.

### Electronic Configuration of Selected Elements

| Element | Atomic Number | K | L | M | N |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | 1 | 1 | - | - | - |
| Helium | 2 | 2 | - | - | - |
| Lithium | 3 | 2 | 1 | - | - |
| Carbon | 6 | 2 | 4 | - | - |
| Nitrogen | 7 | 2 | 5 | - | - |
| Oxygen | 8 | 2 | 6 | - | - |
| Neon | 10 | 2 | 8 | - | - |
| Sodium | 11 | 2 | 8 | 1 | - |
| Magnesium | 12 | 2 | 8 | 2 | - |
| Aluminium | 13 | 2 | 8 | 3 | - |
| Silicon | 14 | 2 | 8 | 4 | - |
| Chlorine | 17 | 2 | 8 | 7 | - |
| Argon | 18 | 2 | 8 | 8 | - |
| Potassium | 19 | 2 | 8 | 8 | 1 |
| Calcium | 20 | 2 | 8 | 8 | 2 |

> **Key idea:** Potassium (19 electrons) is written as 2, 8, 8, 1 rather than 2, 8, 9 because the outermost shell cannot exceed 8 electrons. The 19th electron therefore enters the next shell.

## Valency

The electrons sitting in the **outermost shell** are called **valence electrons**. An element's **valency** reflects how many electrons it must gain, lose, or share to reach a stable arrangement (8 electrons in the outer shell, known as an **octet**; 2 for the lightest elements, a **duplet**).

### Determining Valency

- Outermost shell has **1, 2, or 3** electrons --> valency = that number (atom tends to **lose** electrons)
- Outermost shell has **5, 6, or 7** electrons --> valency = 8 minus that number (atom tends to **gain** electrons)
- Outermost shell has **4** electrons --> valency = 4 (atom tends to **share**)
- Outermost shell is **full** (8 electrons, or 2 for helium) --> valency = **0** (element is inert/noble)

**Examples:**
- **Lithium** (2, 1): Valency = 1 (loses 1 electron)
- **Magnesium** (2, 8, 2): Valency = 2 (loses 2 electrons)
- **Aluminium** (2, 8, 3): Valency = 3 (loses 3 electrons)
- **Carbon** (2, 4): Valency = 4 (shares 4 electrons)
- **Nitrogen** (2, 5): Valency = 3 (gains 3 electrons)
- **Oxygen** (2, 6): Valency = 8 - 6 = 2 (gains 2 electrons)
- **Chlorine** (2, 8, 7): Valency = 8 - 7 = 1 (gains 1 electron)
- **Neon** (2, 8): Valency = 0 (completely stable)

> **Key idea:** Noble gases -- helium, neon, argon -- already have filled outermost shells, giving them zero valency. They are among the least reactive elements known.

## Atomic Number and Mass Number

### Atomic Number (Z)

The **atomic number** counts the **protons** in the nucleus and is symbolised by **Z**.

- Every atom of a given element has the **same Z** -- it is the element's identity card
- In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons
- Examples: Hydrogen Z = 1, Carbon Z = 6, Iron Z = 26

> **Key idea:** Atomic number = number of protons = number of electrons (in a neutral atom). Changing Z changes the element itself.

### Mass Number (A)

The **mass number** is the combined count of **protons and neutrons** in the nucleus, symbolised by **A**.

**A = Z + number of neutrons**

Rearranging: **Number of neutrons = A - Z**

**Standard notation:** An element X with mass number A and atomic number Z is written with A as a superscript and Z as a subscript to the left of the symbol.

Example: Oxygen-16 has Z = 8 and A = 16, meaning it contains 8 protons and 8 neutrons.

## Isotopes

**Isotopes** are atoms of the **same element** sharing the **same atomic number** but carrying **different mass numbers** -- in other words, they have different neutron counts.

### The Three Isotopes of Hydrogen

| Name | Protons | Neutrons | Mass Number |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protium | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| Deuterium | 1 | 1 | 2 |
| Tritium | 1 | 2 | 3 |

All three are hydrogen (Z = 1), but their neutron counts differ.

### Further Isotope Examples

- **Carbon:** C-12 (6 protons, 6 neutrons) and C-14 (6 protons, 8 neutrons)
- **Chlorine:** Cl-35 (17 protons, 18 neutrons) and Cl-37 (17 protons, 20 neutrons)

### How Isotopes Behave

- **Chemical properties are identical** -- same electron count means same reactions
- **Physical properties differ** -- different masses lead to different densities, melting points, etc.

### Average Atomic Mass

Because elements often have multiple isotopes in nature, the published atomic mass is a **weighted average**.

**Example:** Chlorine occurs as Cl-35 (75% abundance) and Cl-37 (25% abundance).

Average atomic mass = (35 x 75/100) + (37 x 25/100) = 26.25 + 9.25 = **35.5 u**

### Practical Uses of Isotopes

- **Uranium-235** -- fuel for nuclear power reactors that generate electricity
- **Cobalt-60** -- used in radiation therapy to treat cancer
- **Iodine-131** -- employed in diagnosing and treating thyroid conditions (goitre)

---

## Isobars

**Isobars** are atoms of **different elements** with **different atomic numbers** but the **same mass number**.

**Example:** Calcium has Z = 20 and A = 40. Argon has Z = 18 and A = 40. Both share mass number 40, yet they are entirely different elements with different chemical behaviours.

> **Key idea:** Isotopes -- same element, same Z, different A. Isobars -- different elements, different Z, same A. Do not confuse the two.

## Key Terms to Remember

- **Electron** -- Negatively charged sub-atomic particle; discovered by J.J. Thomson
- **Proton** -- Positively charged sub-atomic particle; identified through canal rays
- **Neutron** -- Electrically neutral sub-atomic particle; discovered by James Chadwick
- **Nucleus** -- The compact, dense, positively charged centre of an atom
- **Shell / Energy level** -- A fixed orbit around the nucleus where electrons reside
- **Valence electrons** -- Electrons in the outermost shell
- **Valency** -- The combining capacity of an element
- **Atomic number (Z)** -- Count of protons in the nucleus
- **Mass number (A)** -- Combined count of protons and neutrons
- **Isotopes** -- Atoms of the same element with the same Z but different A
- **Isobars** -- Atoms of different elements with different Z but the same A
- **Nucleons** -- The collective term for protons and neutrons
