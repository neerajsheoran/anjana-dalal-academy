## Discovering What Is Inside an Atom

**Child:** Maa, Dalton said atoms cannot be broken. But then scientists found electrons, protons, and neutrons hiding inside. How did they figure that out without being able to see inside?

**Mother:** Through brilliant experiments and careful reasoning, beta. Thomson sealed gases inside glass tubes and sent electricity through them. He observed a stream of tiny negative particles shooting out no matter which gas he used. Since the same particle appeared from every element, he concluded it must be a universal building block -- he had discovered the electron. Goldstein, using a slightly different tube design, spotted positive rays travelling in the opposite direction, revealing the proton. These discoveries proved that atoms are not solid, featureless balls -- they have internal structure.

**Child:** Thomson won a Nobel Prize for the electron. What was his picture of the atom?

**Mother:** Thomson imagined the atom as a sphere of positive charge with electrons peppered throughout it, somewhat like seeds scattered inside a watermelon. The positive and negative charges balanced, keeping the atom neutral. It was a sensible first guess, but it fell apart when Rutherford performed his famous experiment.

## Rutherford's Gold Foil Experiment

**Child:** Everyone talks about this experiment, Maa. What made it so special?

**Mother:** Rutherford's team fired alpha particles -- which are helium nuclei, positively charged and fairly heavy -- at a gold foil so thin it was only about a thousand atoms across. Most alpha particles punched straight through without any deflection, as if the foil were nearly invisible. A few swerved at small angles. And an incredibly tiny fraction -- roughly one in twelve thousand -- bounced straight back towards the source.

**Child:** What do those results tell us?

**Mother:** The fact that most particles sailed through proved that an atom is overwhelmingly empty space. The rare deflections at large angles meant there was something positive and massive inside, repelling the positive alpha particles. And the extreme rarity of complete bounce-backs showed that this positive mass was concentrated in a minuscule spot. Rutherford called this spot the nucleus. To give you a sense of scale: if an atom were blown up to the size of the Jawaharlal Nehru Stadium in Delhi, the nucleus would be smaller than a single marble sitting at centre field.

**Child:** But Rutherford's model had a fatal flaw?

**Mother:** Yes. He said electrons orbit the nucleus like planets around the Sun. But physics tells us that any charged particle moving in a curve must radiate energy continuously. The electron would lose energy, slow down, spiral inward, and crash into the nucleus in a tiny fraction of a second. The atom would self-destruct. Since that obviously does not happen, something was missing from Rutherford's picture. That missing piece was supplied by Niels Bohr.

## Bohr's Solution and Electron Shells

**Child:** How did Bohr fix the problem, Maa?

**Mother:** Bohr made a daring claim: electrons do not travel in just any orbit -- they are restricted to certain specific orbits, called shells or energy levels. While an electron stays in its designated shell, it neither gains nor loses energy. Energy changes happen only when an electron jumps from one shell to another -- absorbing energy to jump outward, or releasing it to drop inward. This elegantly explained why atoms are stable.

**Child:** The shells are K, L, M, N. How many electrons fit in each?

**Mother:** Use the formula 2n squared, where n is the shell number. K (n = 1) holds up to 2 electrons. L (n = 2) holds up to 8. M (n = 3) can hold up to 18 by the formula, but when M is the outermost shell, it maxes out at 8. Always fill the inner shells before moving outward.

**Child:** Can you walk me through chlorine's electron arrangement?

**Mother:** Chlorine has atomic number 17, meaning 17 electrons to distribute. K shell gets 2 (full). L shell gets 8 (full). That leaves 7 electrons for the M shell. So chlorine's electronic configuration is 2, 8, 7. Those 7 electrons in the outermost shell mean chlorine wants just one more electron to complete its octet. That eagerness to grab an extra electron is why chlorine is so reactive -- and why its valency is 1.

## Valency, Atomic Number, and Mass Number

**Child:** So valency is all about the outermost shell?

**Mother:** Precisely. Count the valence electrons -- those in the outermost shell. If there are 1, 2, or 3, the atom prefers to give them away, and the valency equals that count. If there are 5, 6, or 7, the atom prefers to accept electrons to reach 8, so the valency is 8 minus the count. With 4 valence electrons, the atom usually shares rather than giving or taking. And if the outer shell already has 8 (or 2, in the case of helium), valency is zero -- the atom is happy as it is.

**Child:** Atomic number Z is the proton count, and mass number A is protons plus neutrons. Isotopes share the same Z but have different A?

**Mother:** Exactly right. Isotopes are versions of the same element with varying neutron counts. Take hydrogen: protium has zero neutrons, deuterium has one, and tritium has two. All three have exactly one proton, so they are all hydrogen. They react the same way chemically because they have the same number of electrons, but their masses differ because of the extra neutrons.

**Child:** And isobars?

**Mother:** Isobars are atoms of completely different elements that just happen to have the same mass number. For example, calcium-40 has 20 protons and 20 neutrons, while argon-40 has 18 protons and 22 neutrons. Same A, different Z. They are different elements with different chemical behaviours. Examiners frequently test whether students can tell isotopes apart from isobars.

## Applications and Exam Strategy

**Child:** The textbook mentions that isotopes have real-world uses. Which ones should I remember for exams, Maa?

**Mother:** Three stand out. Cobalt-60 is used in radiation therapy for cancer patients -- gamma rays from cobalt-60 can target and destroy tumour cells. Iodine-131 helps doctors diagnose and treat thyroid problems such as goitre. And uranium-235 serves as fuel in nuclear reactors that generate electricity. India's nuclear energy programme, launched by the visionary scientist Homi Bhabha, depends on a thorough understanding of uranium and thorium isotopes.

**Child:** How should I prepare this chapter for the board exam?

**Mother:** Focus on five areas. First, know all three atomic models -- Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr -- and what each got right and wrong. Second, be ready to narrate Rutherford's experiment step by step: setup, three observations, three conclusions. Third, practise writing electronic configurations using the 2n squared rule and the outermost-shell-8 cap. Fourth, derive valency confidently from configuration. Fifth, distinguish isotopes from isobars with clear definitions and examples. For numerical questions, remember the golden relationship: neutrons = mass number minus atomic number. Master these five skills and this chapter becomes one of the easiest to score full marks in.
