## Gravitation

Imagine standing on the terrace of your apartment building in Mumbai and dropping a cricket ball. It rushes straight down towards the ground. Now think bigger -- the Moon, hundreds of thousands of kilometres away, continuously circles our planet instead of flying off into deep space. What invisible thread connects these two events? The answer is **gravitation** -- a universal pull that every object in the cosmos exerts on every other object. Sir Isaac Newton was the first to recognise that the same invisible tug responsible for bringing a mango down from its branch also governs the orbits of planets around the Sun.

---

## Universal Law of Gravitation

**Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation** tells us:

Any two objects in the universe pull each other with a force that is:
- **Directly proportional** to the product of their masses
- **Inversely proportional** to the square of the gap between their centres

![Two masses M and m separated by distance d with gravitational force arrows pointing towards each other](universal-law-of-gravitation-diagram.png)

**F = G x M x m / d squared**

Where:
- F = gravitational pull between the two objects
- G = universal gravitational constant = **6.673 x 10 to the power -11 N m squared / kg squared**
- M and m = the masses of the two objects
- d = the centre-to-centre separation

> **Key idea:** This gravitational pull is always attractive -- it never pushes objects apart. It acts along the straight line connecting the centres of the two bodies. Importantly, the pull is mutual: Earth tugs the mango AND the mango tugs Earth back (consistent with Newton's third law).

### Why Doesn't Earth Rush Towards the Mango?

Both the mango and Earth attract each other with exactly the same magnitude of force. However, Earth's mass is colossal -- roughly 6 x 10 to the power 24 kg. Because acceleration equals force divided by mass, Earth's resulting acceleration is absurdly tiny (around 10 to the power -25 m/s squared), while the mango accelerates at about 9.8 m/s squared. So only the mango's motion is noticeable.

### Why Gravitational Force Matters

- Keeps all eight planets tracing their paths around the Sun
- Holds the Moon in its monthly orbit around Earth
- Generates ocean tides through the combined pull of the Moon and Sun
- Prevents our atmosphere from drifting away into space
- Brings raindrops from clouds down to the fields

## Free Fall and Acceleration Due to Gravity

### Free Fall

Picture a coconut detaching from a tall palm tree. If we could somehow switch off air resistance, the coconut would fall purely under Earth's gravitational pull. This motion -- where gravity alone drives the object -- is called **free fall**.

Throughout free fall, the object **speeds up** continuously. The rate at which its velocity grows is termed the **acceleration due to gravity**, symbolised by **g**.

### Calculating g

**g = GM / R squared**

Where:
- G = gravitational constant
- M = mass of the Earth = 6 x 10 to the power 24 kg
- R = radius of the Earth = 6.4 x 10 to the power 6 m

**g = 9.8 m/s squared** (often rounded to 10 m/s squared for quick calculations)

> **Key idea:** Every freely falling object accelerates at the same rate, no matter how heavy or light it is. In a vacuum, a feather and a brick released together from the same height would land at precisely the same instant.

### Equations of Motion for Free Fall

For vertical motion (taking the downward direction as positive):

| General Equation | Free Fall Version (a = g) |
|---|---|
| v = u + at | v = u + gt |
| s = ut + (1/2)at squared | h = ut + (1/2)gt squared |
| v squared = u squared + 2as | v squared = u squared + 2gh |

**Dropped from rest:** u = 0

**Thrown straight up:** At the topmost point, v = 0, and g acts as deceleration (take g as negative)

## Mass and Weight

### Mass

**Mass** measures how much matter an object contains. It is a **scalar quantity**.

- **SI unit:** kilogram (kg)
- Mass stays the **same** whether you are in Delhi, on Mount Everest, or on the Moon
- Mass also represents an object's **inertia** -- its resistance to being pushed or pulled

### Weight

**Weight** is the gravitational pull that a planet or moon exerts on an object. It is a **vector quantity** pointing towards the centre of the attracting body.

**Weight (W) = mass x g = mg**

- **SI unit:** Newton (N)
- Weight **varies** from place to place because g itself varies
- On Earth's surface, g is roughly 9.8 m/s squared

### Weight on the Moon

Because the Moon is both lighter and smaller than Earth, its surface gravity is only about **one-sixth** of Earth's.

**g(moon) = g(earth) / 6**

So your weight on the Moon is just one-sixth of your Earth weight.

**Example:** Suppose Arjun has a mass of 12 kg. On Earth his weight is 12 x 9.8 = 117.6 N. On the Moon his weight becomes 117.6 / 6 = **19.6 N**. His mass, however, remains 12 kg in both places.

> **Key idea:** Mass never changes with location. Weight changes because it depends on g, which differs from one celestial body to another.

| Property | Mass | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| **What it tells us** | Quantity of matter | Gravitational pull on the object |
| **SI Unit** | kg | N (Newton) |
| **Scalar or Vector** | Scalar | Vector |
| **Changes with location?** | No | Yes |
| **Instrument used** | Beam balance (pan balance) | Spring balance |

## Thrust and Pressure

### Thrust

**Thrust** is the total force that acts **perpendicular** to a surface.

### Pressure

**Pressure** tells us how concentrated a force is over an area.

**Pressure = Force / Area = Thrust / Area**

- **SI unit:** Pascal (Pa), where 1 Pa = 1 N/m squared

> **Key idea:** For a given force, shrinking the contact area raises the pressure, while spreading the force over a larger area lowers it.

**Everyday illustrations from Indian life:**
- A freshly sharpened chaaku (knife) slices vegetables effortlessly because the thin blade concentrates force onto a tiny area
- Tractors in Punjab's fields use broad tyres so that their heavy weight spreads over a large area, preventing the wheels from sinking into soft soil
- The pointed tip of a drawing pin pushes into a notice board easily because the force is focussed on a minuscule area
- School bags with wide padded straps distribute the load across your shoulders, reducing pain

### Pressure in Fluids

Liquids and gases (collectively called fluids) push against surfaces in every direction. A fluid presses on the walls of its container, on the base, and on every object placed inside it.

## Buoyancy and Archimedes' Principle

### Buoyant Force

Submerge a brass lota (water pot) in a bucket of water and you feel it becoming lighter. That sensation arises because the water pushes the lota **upward**. This upward push is the **buoyant force** (also called **upthrust**).

![Object submerged in water with weight arrow pointing down and buoyant force arrow pointing up](buoyant-force-on-submerged-object.png)

The magnitude of buoyant force depends on:
- The **density** of the surrounding fluid
- The **volume** of the portion of the object that is under the fluid surface

### Floating and Sinking

- When an object's density is **lower** than the fluid's density, the object **floats**
- When an object's density is **higher** than the fluid's density, it **sinks**

**Example:** A dry neem log floats in a village pond because wood is less dense than water, but a steel spoon sinks because steel is denser than water.

### Archimedes' Principle

**Archimedes' Principle** states: Any body placed (fully or partly) inside a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force whose magnitude equals the **weight of the fluid that the body pushes aside** (displaces).

**Buoyant force = Weight of displaced fluid = Volume displaced x density of fluid x g**

### Real-World Uses of Archimedes' Principle

- **Ship and submarine design** -- the hull is shaped so that enough water is displaced to keep the vessel afloat despite its enormous steel mass
- **Lactometers** -- dairy inspectors across India use these devices to check whether milk has been diluted with water; the instrument sinks deeper in watered-down milk because its density is lower than pure milk
- **Hydrometers** -- laboratory instruments that measure the density of any liquid sample

## Relative Density

**Relative density** of a substance compares its density to the density of water.

**Relative density = Density of substance / Density of water**

Relative density is a pure ratio and therefore has **no unit**.

- Relative density > 1 means the substance is heavier than water and will **sink**
- Relative density < 1 means the substance is lighter than water and will **float**

**Density of water = 1000 kg/m cubed = 1 g/cm cubed**

## Key Formulae

- **Gravitational force:** F = GMm / d squared
- **Acceleration due to gravity:** g = GM / R squared = 9.8 m/s squared
- **Weight:** W = mg
- **Pressure:** P = F / A
- **Buoyant force** = weight of fluid displaced
- **Relative density** = density of substance / density of water

## Key Terms to Remember

- **Gravitation** -- The mutual attraction between any two masses in the universe
- **Gravity** -- Earth's specific gravitational pull on nearby objects
- **Free fall** -- Motion governed solely by gravity (no air drag or other forces)
- **g (acceleration due to gravity)** -- Approximately 9.8 m/s squared at Earth's surface
- **Mass** -- Quantity of matter in a body (remains constant everywhere)
- **Weight** -- Gravitational force acting on a body (depends on g)
- **Thrust** -- Perpendicular force on a surface
- **Pressure** -- Force spread over unit area
- **Buoyant force** -- Upward push exerted by a fluid on a submerged or floating object
- **Archimedes' Principle** -- Buoyant force equals the weight of displaced fluid
- **Relative density** -- How dense a substance is compared to water
