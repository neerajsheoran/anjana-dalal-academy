## The Universal Law of Gravitation

**Child:** Maa, everyone says Newton discovered gravity because an apple fell on his head. Did that really happen?

**Mother:** The apple-on-the-head part is probably a folk exaggeration, beta. But what Newton truly did was remarkable. He watched things fall and asked a deeper question -- could the same invisible force that pulls a mango from a tree also be the reason the Moon keeps circling Earth instead of flying away? He concluded yes, and wrote down a beautiful law: every object in the universe pulls every other object with a force that depends on the product of their masses divided by the square of the distance between them. F equals G times M times m divided by d-squared.

**Child:** G is such a tiny number -- 6.673 times 10 to the power minus 11. What does that mean for everyday life?

**Mother:** It means two ordinary objects barely tug on each other at all. Place two 1-kg dumbbells one metre apart on our dining table, and the gravitational pull between them is only about 6.673 times 10 to the power minus 11 Newtons -- you could never feel that! But when one object is as massive as Earth -- 6 times 10 to the power 24 kg -- the force becomes strong enough to hold us firmly on the ground. That is why we notice Earth's gravity but not the pull of our refrigerator.

**Child:** Maa, by Newton's Third Law, the mango also pulls Earth towards itself, right? Why does only the mango fall?

**Mother:** Excellent thinking! Yes, the forces are equal and opposite. But think about acceleration -- it equals force divided by mass. The mango has very little mass, so it accelerates at 9.8 m/s squared, rushing down fast. Earth has such an enormous mass that its acceleration towards the mango is about 10 to the power minus 25 m/s squared -- unimaginably small. Technically Earth does move towards the mango, but by an amount far too tiny to ever detect.

## Acceleration Due to Gravity and Free Fall

**Child:** If a heavy stone and a light pebble are dropped together, shouldn't the heavier one fall faster since gravity pulls it more?

**Mother:** That is a very natural thought, but nature has a neat trick. Yes, the heavy stone experiences a bigger gravitational force, but it also has more inertia -- it resists acceleration more. These two effects perfectly cancel. The acceleration g equals GM divided by R-squared, and notice the object's own mass does not appear in that formula at all. In a vacuum, a coin and a cotton ball dropped together would hit the ground at the same moment. Astronauts on the Moon demonstrated exactly this with a feather and a hammer during the Apollo 15 mission.

**Child:** Then why does a dupatta fall slower than a cricket ball in real life?

**Mother:** Because of air resistance, beta. The dupatta has a large surface compared to its weight, so air drags against it strongly. The cricket ball is compact and heavy, so air barely slows it. Remove the air -- put them in a vacuum chamber -- and they fall side by side. For your exams, always remember: "in vacuum, all objects fall at the same rate."

**Child:** Can we use the normal equations of motion for free fall?

**Mother:** Absolutely. Just replace the acceleration a with g, which is 9.8 m/s squared, and use h for vertical height instead of s. For something dropped from rest, u is zero: v equals gt, h equals half g t-squared, and v-squared equals 2gh. For something tossed upward, v becomes zero at the peak, and g acts against the motion so you treat it as negative. These substitutions form the backbone of most numerical problems in this chapter.

## Mass, Weight, and the Moon

**Child:** Maa, at the vegetable market, the vendor says "aapka wajan 2 kilo hai" for the potatoes. Is he talking about mass or weight?

**Mother:** Great observation! In daily Hindi or English, people use "weight" loosely. Scientifically, what the vendor measures on a beam balance is actually **mass** -- 2 kilograms of matter. **Weight** is the gravitational force on those potatoes, which would be about 2 times 9.8 equals 19.6 Newtons. Mass is in kilograms, weight is in Newtons. Mass stays the same everywhere; weight changes depending on where you are because g changes.

**Child:** So if I go to the Moon, my mass stays the same but I become lighter?

**Mother:** Precisely. The Moon's surface gravity is about one-sixth of Earth's -- roughly 1.63 m/s squared instead of 9.8. If you weigh 360 N on Earth (mass about 36.7 kg), you would weigh only about 60 N on the Moon. That is why astronauts can take those long, bouncy leaps on the lunar surface. India's Chandrayaan missions actually map the Moon's gravitational field to understand what lies beneath its surface.

**Child:** Will a spring balance give the same reading on the Moon as on Earth?

**Mother:** No! A spring balance measures force -- it stretches more when the pull is greater. On the Moon, with weaker gravity, it will show about one-sixth the Earth reading. A beam balance, on the other hand, compares masses using the local gravity equally on both pans, so it reads the same everywhere. This distinction between the two instruments is a favourite question in board exams.

## Pressure, Buoyancy, and Archimedes' Principle

**Child:** Maa, why does it hurt to step on a tiny Lego brick but not when I stand on the flat floor?

**Mother:** Because pressure equals force divided by area. Your body weight is the same in both cases, but the Lego brick has a very small surface pressing against your foot, creating enormous pressure on that spot. The flat floor spreads your weight over the entire sole, reducing pressure dramatically. This same principle explains why camels have wide, flat feet -- their weight spreads over a large area so they do not sink into desert sand. And it is why your school bag has broad straps -- to reduce pressure on your shoulders.

**Child:** Now tell me about Archimedes' Principle. How does a massive steel ship float while a small steel spoon sinks?

**Mother:** Archimedes' Principle says that the buoyant force on a submerged object equals the weight of the fluid it pushes aside. A steel spoon is solid -- its density is far greater than water's, so it cannot displace enough water to support its own weight, and it sinks. A ship is hollow inside, filled with air. Its overall density -- total mass divided by total volume including all that air space -- is less than water's. It displaces a huge volume of water whose weight matches the ship's weight, and so it floats happily.

**Child:** What about relative density?

**Mother:** Relative density is simply how dense something is compared to water. Gold has a relative density around 19.3, which means it is 19.3 times denser than water -- it sinks immediately. Cork has a relative density of about 0.2, so it floats easily. The lactometer that dairy inspectors use across India works on this principle -- it checks whether milk has been mixed with water by measuring the milk's density. Pure milk is denser than diluted milk, so the lactometer sits at different levels depending on purity.
