## From One Cell to Many Working Together

In Chapter 2, we saw that life begins with a single cell. That single cell divides, divides again, and within a few weeks of development, it becomes a being made of millions of cells. But these millions of cells do not all do the same thing -- some become skin, some become muscle, some become bone, some become nerve. Cells that do similar work group together and form a **tissue**.

> **Key idea:** A **tissue** is a group of cells of similar structure that work together to perform a specific function. Tissues are how the body achieves **division of labour** at the cellular level.

In your own body, muscle tissue handles movement, nervous tissue carries messages, and connective tissue holds everything together. In a mango tree, **xylem** transports water from the roots to the topmost leaves, and **phloem** carries sugars from the leaves to the rest of the plant. Different jobs, different tissues -- but always the same principle: many specialised cells, one task.

---

## Why Plant and Animal Tissues Are Different

You may have noticed that a banyan tree stands still its whole life, while a sparrow flits across the sky. This single difference shapes the entire tissue architecture of plants and animals.

**Plants are rooted.** They need rigid support to stand upright against gravity and wind. They have **cell walls** made of cellulose, which is why a tree trunk can hold up tons of branches.

**Animals move.** They cannot be locked in place. Their cells are flexible, without rigid walls. Their tissues are built to bend, contract, and reshape -- which makes locomotion possible.

There is another deep difference. Plants make their own food (autotrophs), so they have tissues for **photosynthesis** (chloroplasts in green tissues) and **transport** (xylem and phloem). Animals find food (heterotrophs), so they have tissues for **digestion**, **circulation**, and **locomotion**.

## Plant Tissues — The Growing and the Grown

Plant tissues are broadly of two kinds:

- **Meristematic tissues** -- actively dividing cells that drive growth.
- **Permanent tissues** -- cells that have specialised and no longer divide. They include the conducting and protective tissues.

### Meristematic tissue — where plants grow

Walk past a sapling and notice the tiny new leaves at the very tip of a shoot. That is where growth is happening, in a tissue called the **meristem**. Meristematic cells are small, thin-walled, and constantly dividing.

There are three kinds, depending on where they sit:

| Type | Location | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Apical meristem** | At the tips of roots and shoots | Increases length |
| **Lateral meristem (cambium)** | Along the sides of stems and roots | Increases girth (thickness) |
| **Intercalary meristem** | At the base of leaves or above nodes (e.g., grasses) | Allows regrowth after grazing or cutting |

> **Key idea:** Apical meristem makes a plant **taller**. Lateral meristem (cambium) makes it **thicker**. Intercalary meristem lets grass and bamboo **regrow** after being cut.

### Permanent tissues — three big jobs

Once meristematic cells finish dividing, they specialise. There are three families of permanent tissue:

**Simple permanent tissues** -- one type of cell, doing one job:

- **Parenchyma** -- soft, loosely packed cells that store food (in potatoes, in fruits), and do photosynthesis when they contain chloroplasts.
- **Collenchyma** -- gives flexible support (you see it in young stems and leaf stalks).
- **Sclerenchyma** -- very strong, dead at maturity, gives rigidity (the husk of coconut, the gritty bits in pear flesh).

**Complex permanent tissues** -- many cell types, doing one specialised job:

- **Xylem** -- transports water and dissolved minerals upward from roots to leaves. Made of long hollow tubes (vessels), often dead at maturity. The "wood" of any tree is mostly old xylem.
- **Phloem** -- transports food (sugars from photosynthesis) from leaves to the rest of the plant. Made of living cells.

**Protective tissue** -- the outer layer:

- **Epidermis** -- a single layer of cells covering the entire plant, often coated with a waxy **cuticle** to prevent water loss. Stomata (tiny pores) on leaves are gaps in the epidermis for gas exchange.

> **Key idea:** **Xylem** carries water up. **Phloem** carries food everywhere. Together they are the plant's circulatory system.

## Animal Tissues — Four Big Families

Animal tissues are sorted into four big groups:

### 1. Epithelial tissue — coverings and linings

Wherever there is a boundary -- the outside of your skin, the lining of your stomach, the inside of your blood vessels -- there is an **epithelial tissue**. Cells of epithelial tissue are packed tightly with very little space between them.

Types based on shape and arrangement:
- **Squamous epithelium** -- thin, flat cells. Lines the alveoli of the lungs (very thin so gases can pass).
- **Cuboidal epithelium** -- cube-shaped cells. Lines kidney tubules and salivary gland ducts.
- **Columnar epithelium** -- tall column-like cells. Lines the small intestine, sometimes with little "brush border" projections to absorb nutrients.
- **Ciliated epithelium** -- columnar cells with hair-like cilia. Lines the windpipe and helps sweep dust out.

### 2. Connective tissue — fills, supports, and connects

These tissues "connect" other tissues to each other. They have widely spaced cells separated by a non-living **matrix** (which can be jelly-like, fibrous, or hard).

- **Blood** -- liquid matrix (plasma). Carries oxygen, nutrients, waste, and immune cells everywhere.
- **Bone** -- hard, mineral-rich matrix. Forms the skeleton.
- **Cartilage** -- smooth and flexible matrix. Found at joints, in the outer ear, and the tip of your nose.
- **Adipose tissue** -- fat cells that store energy and insulate the body.
- **Ligaments** -- connect bone to bone.
- **Tendons** -- connect muscle to bone.

> **Key idea:** Blood is a connective tissue. Surprising, but true -- it has cells separated by a liquid matrix, which fits the definition.

### 3. Muscular tissue — for movement

Muscles produce movement by **contracting**.

- **Skeletal muscles** -- attached to bones. They are **voluntary** (you control them). The biceps in your arm, the muscles in your thigh -- all skeletal.
- **Smooth muscles** -- found in walls of the stomach, intestines, blood vessels. They are **involuntary** (you cannot control them consciously). They push food through your digestive tract.
- **Cardiac muscle** -- found only in the heart. Involuntary, never tires, contracts about 70-80 times every minute of your life.

### 4. Nervous tissue — for control and coordination

Nervous tissue is made of **neurons** (nerve cells), which are designed to send electrical signals over long distances. A neuron has a cell body, branched **dendrites** that receive signals, and a long thin **axon** that sends signals to the next cell.

When you touch a hot pan and pull your hand away in a flash, that is your nervous tissue at work -- a signal travelled from your skin to your spinal cord and back to your arm muscles in milliseconds.

> **Key idea:** Four animal tissue families: **epithelial** (coverings), **connective** (filling and support), **muscular** (movement), **nervous** (signals).

## Indian Scientists Who Studied Tissues

**B.G.L. Swamy** was a celebrated Indian botanist whose detailed studies of plant anatomy contributed enormously to our understanding of plant tissues. His Kannada book *Hasuru Honnu* describes botanical journeys through the Western Ghats and won the Sahitya Akademi Award in 1978 -- a science book that was also great literature.

**J.C. Bose** (whom you met in Class 6) showed using his crescograph that plant tissues respond to stimuli like cuts and burns with electrical signals -- a discovery that connected plant biology to neuroscience for the first time.

## Summary

- A **tissue** is a group of similar cells working together for a specific function.
- **Plant tissues** are of two kinds: **meristematic** (actively dividing, drives growth) and **permanent** (specialised, often no longer dividing).
- **Apical meristem** → length; **lateral meristem (cambium)** → thickness; **intercalary meristem** → regrowth.
- Simple permanent tissues: **parenchyma** (storage), **collenchyma** (flexible support), **sclerenchyma** (rigid strength).
- Complex permanent tissues: **xylem** (water up) and **phloem** (food everywhere).
- Protective tissue: **epidermis** with waxy cuticle and stomata for gas exchange.
- **Animal tissues** are four kinds:
  - **Epithelial** — coverings and linings (squamous, cuboidal, columnar, ciliated)
  - **Connective** — blood, bone, cartilage, adipose, ligaments, tendons
  - **Muscular** — skeletal (voluntary), smooth (involuntary), cardiac (heart only)
  - **Nervous** — neurons that carry electrical signals
- Plants and animals have different tissues because they make a living differently — plants are rooted and make food; animals move and find food.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**The Indian medical industry runs on tissue knowledge**. When a doctor at PGIMER Chandigarh diagnoses kidney disease, they often examine a tiny biopsy of kidney tissue under a microscope. Different damage patterns in epithelial cells of kidney tubules point to different diseases. Tissue analysis is the backbone of modern diagnosis.

**Tissue culture in agriculture**. ICAR labs across India grow whole banana, sugarcane, and orchid plants from tiny pieces of meristematic tissue. A single mother plant gives rise to thousands of identical young plants. This is how India became one of the world's largest banana producers.

**Sports medicine**. When a Mumbai Indians fast bowler tears a ligament, the recovery plan is shaped by tissue biology. Ligaments heal slowly because they have poor blood supply -- a fact every Indian physiotherapist works around.

### Try it at home — Watch xylem in action

**What you'll need:** A white carnation flower (or rajnigandha / white rose) from any flower vendor, a glass of water, red or blue food colouring (or a few crushed beetroot pieces in water).

**Steps:**
1. Fill the glass with water and stir in plenty of food colouring until the water is deeply coloured.
2. Trim the flower stem cleanly and place the flower in the coloured water.
3. Leave for 12-24 hours.

**What to look for:**
- The flower's petals slowly take on the colour of the water.
- If you slice the stem, you will see coloured streaks running along its length -- the **xylem** carrying water (and food colour) upward to the petals.

You have watched plant transport biology happen in your own room. Same xylem that lifts water 30 metres up a coconut palm, working in front of your eyes.
