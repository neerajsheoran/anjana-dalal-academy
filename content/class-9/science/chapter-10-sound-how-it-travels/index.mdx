## What Is Sound?

You wake up to your mother calling your name. You hear a temple bell as you pass. A bus horns at a busy junction. A radio plays an old Lata Mangeshkar song. These are all sounds -- one of the most common sensations of our lives.

But what IS sound? In physics, sound is a form of energy that travels as **vibrations** through a medium -- air, water, or solid. Without something vibrating, there is no sound. Without a medium to carry the vibration, the sound cannot reach our ears.

In this chapter, we will explore how sound is made, how it travels, why some sounds are loud and others soft, and how our ears turn these vibrations into the rich world of sound we experience every day.

---

## How Sound Is Produced

**All sounds are produced by vibrating objects.**

- Strum a sitar string -- the string vibrates and produces sound.
- Bang a drum -- the drum membrane vibrates.
- Speak -- your vocal cords vibrate.
- Clap your hands -- your hands and the air between them vibrate.
- Ring a temple bell -- the metal of the bell vibrates.

You can FEEL the vibration if you touch a speaker playing loud music. You can SEE the vibration if you sprinkle salt grains on a drum and bang another drum nearby -- the grains will jump.

Stop the vibration, and the sound stops.

> **Key idea:** Sound is produced by vibrating objects. The more rapidly the object vibrates, the higher the sound; the more it moves with each vibration, the louder the sound.

## How Sound Travels — A Wave of Compressions

When a tuning fork vibrates, its prongs move back and forth. As the prong moves forward, it **compresses** the air just in front of it, pushing the air molecules together. As the prong moves backward, it **rarefies** the air, leaving the molecules slightly spread out.

This pattern of compression and rarefaction travels outward through the air as a **wave**.

Each air molecule does not travel far -- it just oscillates back and forth in its place, passing the disturbance along to its neighbour. Sound is the disturbance that moves; the air just transmits it.

Think of a long line of dominos: knock the first one and a wave of falling travels down the line. The dominos themselves only fall a little, but the wave reaches the far end.

### Sound is a longitudinal wave

A wave where the particles vibrate **back and forth in the same direction the wave is moving** is called a **longitudinal wave**. Sound is longitudinal.

Compare with a **transverse wave**, where particles vibrate **perpendicular** to the direction of motion -- like a wave on a rope, or light waves.

> **Key idea:** Sound travels as a longitudinal wave -- compressions and rarefactions of the medium. The medium (air, water, solid) carries the sound; the medium itself does not travel.

---

## Sound Needs a Medium

Sound cannot travel through **vacuum**. With nothing to compress and rarefy, the sound has nowhere to go.

This is why astronauts cannot hear each other directly in space -- they speak through radios that send electromagnetic waves (which travel through vacuum). If two astronauts touched helmets, they could hear vibrations transmitted through the solid material. But the empty space between them is silent.

### Speed of sound in different media

Sound travels at different speeds in different materials.

| Medium | Approximate speed of sound (m/s) |
|---|---|
| Air at 20°C | 343 |
| Air at 0°C | 331 |
| Water | 1500 |
| Steel | 5960 |
| Granite | 6000 |

Sound travels FASTER in solids and liquids than in gases, because the particles in solids and liquids are closer together and pass the vibration more efficiently.

When you put your ear against a railway track, you can sometimes hear an approaching train before you hear it through the air. The vibration travels through the steel rail much faster.

### Sound speed and temperature

In any given medium, sound also travels faster as temperature rises -- because warmer particles move faster and transmit the disturbance more rapidly. So a hot summer afternoon in Chennai has slightly faster sound than a cool winter morning in Shimla.

## Frequency, Pitch, and the Hearing Range

**Frequency** is the number of vibrations per second of the source. The SI unit is the **hertz (Hz)** -- 1 Hz = 1 vibration per second.

The frequency of a sound wave determines its **pitch** -- how high or low it sounds.

- **Low frequency** → low pitch (a tabla bass note, a thunder rumble)
- **High frequency** → high pitch (a flute, a whistle, a baby's cry)

### Human hearing range

The human ear can detect frequencies from about **20 Hz to 20,000 Hz** (20 kHz).

- Sounds below 20 Hz are called **infrasound** -- elephants and whales produce and detect these.
- Sounds above 20,000 Hz are called **ultrasound** -- bats, dolphins, and dogs can hear these; we cannot.

As we age, our ability to hear high frequencies fades. Most people over 50 cannot hear sounds above 12,000 Hz.

## Amplitude and Loudness

**Amplitude** is the maximum displacement of particles from their resting position during the vibration. Larger amplitude = larger vibration = louder sound.

The loudness of sound is measured in **decibels (dB)**.

| Sound | Approximate level (dB) |
|---|---|
| Threshold of hearing | 0 |
| Whisper at 1 metre | 30 |
| Quiet library | 40 |
| Normal conversation | 60 |
| Busy traffic | 80 |
| Mumbai local train horn at platform | 90 |
| Loudest cricket stadium roar | 110 |
| Jet engine at 100 m | 130 |
| Permanent hearing damage threshold | 140 |

Decibels are logarithmic -- every increase of 10 dB is 10 times more intense in physical terms. So 80 dB traffic is 10 times more intense than 70 dB. A 100 dB rock concert is 1000 times more intense than a 70 dB conversation.

> **Key idea:** Frequency determines pitch (high or low); amplitude determines loudness (soft or loud).

## Reflection of Sound — Echoes

When sound waves strike a hard, smooth surface, they bounce back. This bouncing is called **reflection**, and the reflected sound is called an **echo**.

For a clear echo, three things must hold:

1. The reflecting surface must be **large** (a wall, a cliff, the floor of an empty hall).
2. The reflecting surface must be at a distance of at least **17 metres** from the source. (Why 17? Because at 343 m/s, the sound takes about 0.1 second to travel 17 m and another 0.1 second to come back, which is the minimum time the human ear needs to distinguish the original sound from the echo.)
3. The intensity of the reflected sound must be high enough to be heard.

### Multiple reflections — reverberation

In a large empty hall, sound can reflect off many walls, ceiling, and floor in quick succession, blending into a long sustained echo. This is called **reverberation**.

A small amount of reverberation enriches music (which is why concert halls and Indian classical music theatres are often designed with hard wood panels). Too much reverberation makes speech hard to understand (which is why classrooms and auditoriums often have soft absorbing materials on the walls).

---

## Uses of Multiple Reflections

### Megaphone and stethoscope

A megaphone (loudspeaker cone) sends sound forward in one direction using reflection inside the cone. A stethoscope uses tubes and multiple reflections inside the chest piece to amplify the very faint sounds of a heartbeat.

### Soundboards in auditoriums

Many concert halls have a curved soundboard behind the stage. Sound from the performer reflects off this surface and reaches the audience without losing intensity. The Saraswati hall at the National Centre for the Performing Arts in Mumbai uses this principle.

### SONAR (Sound Navigation and Ranging)

Used by submarines, fishing boats, and oceanographers to map the ocean floor and detect underwater objects. A pulse of sound is sent down; the echo from objects below is recorded. Knowing the speed of sound in water (~1500 m/s) and the time taken, the depth can be calculated.

The Indian Navy uses SONAR on its submarines and naval ships to detect underwater threats.

### Ultrasound in medicine

**Ultrasound waves** (frequencies above 20 kHz) are used in:

- **Medical imaging** -- pregnancy scans use ultrasound to make images of the foetus inside the mother's womb. The waves are reflected off internal structures and the echoes are converted into images.
- **Cleaning** -- ultrasound cleaners shake dirt off jewellery, dental instruments, and precision parts using high-frequency vibrations.
- **Breaking kidney stones** -- focused ultrasound waves can break stones in the kidney without surgery (a procedure called **lithotripsy**, used in major Indian hospitals).

## The Human Ear

The ear has three main parts that work together to convert sound waves into electrical signals for the brain.

### Outer ear

- The visible **pinna** collects sound and funnels it into the **ear canal**.
- The sound waves travel down the canal to the **eardrum**.

### Middle ear

- The eardrum vibrates with the sound.
- These vibrations are passed through three tiny bones: the **hammer (malleus)**, **anvil (incus)**, and **stirrup (stapes)** -- the smallest bones in the human body.
- These three bones amplify the vibrations about 20 times.

### Inner ear

- The amplified vibrations enter the **cochlea**, a spiral fluid-filled structure.
- Hair cells inside the cochlea vibrate with specific frequencies and produce electrical signals.
- The **auditory nerve** carries these signals to the brain, which interprets them as sound.

### Protecting your hearing

- Avoid prolonged exposure to loud sounds above 80 dB.
- Wear earplugs at noisy events (concerts, festivals, construction sites).
- Keep music volume on headphones below 60% for no more than 60 minutes at a time -- a guideline from the World Health Organisation.

## Summary

- **Sound** is energy that travels as vibrations through a medium (air, water, solid).
- All sounds are produced by **vibrating objects**.
- Sound travels as a **longitudinal wave** -- particles vibrate parallel to the wave direction.
- Sound cannot travel through **vacuum** (no medium to vibrate).
- Speed of sound varies with medium: fastest in solids (~6000 m/s in steel), slower in liquids (~1500 m/s in water), slowest in gases (~343 m/s in air at 20°C).
- **Frequency** = vibrations per second (in hertz). Determines **pitch**.
- **Amplitude** = maximum displacement. Determines **loudness**.
- Human hearing range: **20 Hz to 20,000 Hz**. Below 20 Hz = infrasound; above 20,000 Hz = ultrasound.
- **Echo** = reflected sound. Needs a hard surface at least 17 m from the source.
- **Reverberation** = multiple reflections blending into a sustained sound.
- **SONAR** uses sound reflection to detect underwater objects.
- **Ultrasound** is used for medical imaging, cleaning, and lithotripsy (breaking kidney stones).
- The **human ear**: outer ear collects, middle ear amplifies, inner ear converts to electrical signals.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Antenatal scans in every Indian district hospital**. When an expectant mother visits her doctor for a pregnancy check, ultrasound waves (about 3-7 million Hz) are sent into her abdomen. The waves reflect off the developing baby and other tissues; a computer turns the echoes into an image. The mother sees her baby for the first time — a marvel of physics translating into medicine.

**The Indian Navy's submarines** use SONAR to navigate underwater and detect other vessels. By sending sound pulses and timing the echoes, the submarine builds a map of the surrounding ocean. Same physics that bats use to navigate caves and that you can demonstrate with a clap in an empty hall.

**Why concert halls sound great**. India's premier classical music halls — Music Academy in Chennai, Nehru Centre in Mumbai, Kamani Auditorium in Delhi — are carefully engineered with sound reflection in mind. Curved soundboards behind the stage, wood panelling on the walls, and the right amount of soft absorbing material on the seats all combine to give every member of the audience clear sound. Same physics — different design choices for different goals.

### Try it at home — Make a paper-cup telephone

**What you'll need:** Two empty paper cups, about 5 metres of cotton string or fishing line, a pin or thin nail.

**Steps:**
1. Punch a small hole in the bottom centre of each cup.
2. Thread the string through one hole from the outside, knot it inside so it cannot pull through. Repeat with the other cup so the cups are connected.
3. Hold one cup. Have a friend hold the other. Walk apart until the string is TAUT but not stretched.
4. One person talks softly into their cup. The other puts their cup to their ear.

**What you should hear:** The talker's voice clearly, even though they are too far away to hear normally at that whisper level.

**Why it works:** The talker's voice vibrates the bottom of the first cup. These vibrations travel along the taut string as a wave. They reach the second cup and vibrate its bottom, which then vibrates the air inside the cup near the listener's ear. The string is a much better conductor of sound than the open air at that distance, because sound travels faster and more efficiently through solids (and string is closer to solid than air).

If the string is loose, the wave dies before reaching the other end. If you pluck the string, it now becomes a stringed instrument too — its own vibration creates its own sound.

You have just demonstrated that sound is a vibration, that it travels through different media at different speeds, and that solids carry sound efficiently — all in a kitchen experiment.
