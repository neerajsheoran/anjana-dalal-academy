## What Does It Mean to Move?

Look around -- autorickshaws weave through traffic, kites soar over rooftops, the Yamuna flows toward the sea, and Earth swings around the Sun. An object is in **motion** whenever its position changes over time.

### The Need for a Reference Point

To pin down where something is, you first need a fixed marker called the **reference point** (or **origin**). For instance, "the chai stall is 500 metres south of the bus stand" uses the bus stand as the reference point.

> **Key idea:** Motion is always relative. A passenger sitting in a moving Rajdhani Express is at rest for a fellow passenger, but appears to zoom past a farmer standing beside the tracks. Whether something is "moving" depends entirely on who is watching.

## Distance and Displacement

### Distance

**Distance** is the complete length of the path an object actually travels. It is always a positive number and carries no information about direction. Distance is a **scalar quantity**.

### Displacement

**Displacement** is the **straight-line gap** between where the object started and where it ended up, together with the direction of that gap. It is a **vector quantity** (magnitude + direction).

**How they compare:**

| Property | Distance | Displacement |
|---|---|---|
| What it measures | Total path taken | Straight line from start to finish |
| Direction | None | Has a specific direction |
| Can it be zero? | Only if the object never moves | Yes -- if it comes back to the start |
| Can it be negative? | Never | Yes |
| Type | Scalar | Vector |

![Diagram showing an object moving from O to A and back to B, illustrating the difference between distance and displacement](distance-vs-displacement-diagram.png)

**Worked example:** Priya walks 4 km east from her home to the market, then turns around and walks 1.5 km west toward the park.
- Distance covered = 4 + 1.5 = **5.5 km**
- Displacement = 4 - 1.5 = **2.5 km east** of home

If Priya walks all the way back home:
- Distance = 4 + 4 = **8 km**
- Displacement = **0 km** (start and finish are the same spot)

> **Key idea:** Distance is always at least as large as the magnitude of displacement. They are equal only when the object travels in a perfectly straight line without turning back.

## Uniform and Non-Uniform Motion

- **Uniform motion:** The object covers **equal distances in every equal time interval** (e.g., exactly 10 m every second)
- **Non-uniform motion:** The object covers **different distances in equal time intervals** (e.g., a scooter weaving through rush-hour traffic in Jaipur)

## Speed

**Speed** tells us how quickly an object covers distance.

**Speed = Distance / Time**

**v = s / t**

- **SI unit:** metre per second (m/s or m s⁻¹)
- Also commonly given in km/h
- Speed is a **scalar** -- it has size but no direction

### Average Speed

Real journeys rarely happen at a constant pace. For such trips we use:

**Average speed = Total distance covered / Total time taken**

**Handy conversion:** 1 km/h = 5/18 m/s (multiply km/h by 5/18 to get m/s)

**Example:** An auto covers 15 km in half an hour. Average speed = 15 / 0.5 = 30 km/h. The auto may have crawled at traffic lights and sped up on open stretches.

---

## Velocity

**Velocity** is speed paired with a **direction**. It is a **vector quantity**.

**Velocity = Displacement / Time**

### Average Velocity

When an object accelerates at a steady rate, its average velocity is simply the midpoint of its starting and finishing velocities:

**Average velocity = (u + v) / 2**

where u = initial velocity, v = final velocity.

> **Key idea:** Speed answers "how fast?" Velocity answers "how fast and which way?" A cyclist pedalling at a steady 12 km/h around a circular park has constant speed but changing velocity, because the direction keeps shifting.

---

## Acceleration

**Acceleration** measures how rapidly velocity changes.

**Acceleration = (Final velocity − Initial velocity) / Time**

**a = (v − u) / t**

- **SI unit:** m/s² (m s⁻²)
- **Positive acceleration** -- object speeds up
- **Negative acceleration (deceleration or retardation)** -- object slows down
- **Zero acceleration** -- velocity is unchanging (uniform motion)

### Uniform vs Non-Uniform Acceleration

- **Uniform acceleration:** Velocity changes by the **same amount every second** (e.g., a mango falling freely from a tree)
- **Non-uniform acceleration:** Velocity changes by **different amounts** each second

## Reading Graphs of Motion

### Distance–Time Graphs

| What the Graph Looks Like | What It Tells You |
|---|---|
| Flat horizontal line | Object is standing still (distance stays the same) |
| Straight line sloping upward | Uniform speed (equal distance every second) |
| Upward-curving line | Non-uniform speed (speeding up) |

- The **slope** (steepness) of a distance–time graph equals the **speed**
- A steeper line means a faster object

![Distance-time graphs showing uniform speed (straight line) and non-uniform speed (curved line)](distance-time-graphs.png)

### Velocity–Time Graphs

| What the Graph Looks Like | What It Tells You |
|---|---|
| Flat horizontal line | Constant velocity (zero acceleration) |
| Straight line sloping upward | Uniform acceleration |
| Straight line sloping downward | Uniform deceleration |
| Curve | Non-uniform acceleration |

- The **slope** of a velocity–time graph equals the **acceleration**
- The **area enclosed** between the line and the time axis equals the **distance travelled**

![Velocity-time graphs showing uniform velocity, uniform acceleration, and uniform deceleration with shaded area representing distance](velocity-time-graphs.png)

> **Key idea:** Graphs pack a lot of information. Slope of a distance–time graph → speed. Slope of a velocity–time graph → acceleration. Area under a velocity–time graph → distance.

## The Three Equations of Motion

For any object moving with **uniform acceleration**, these three equations connect initial velocity (u), final velocity (v), acceleration (a), time (t), and displacement (s):

### First Equation

**v = u + at**

Links velocity, initial velocity, acceleration, and time.

### Second Equation

**s = ut + ½ at²**

Links displacement, initial velocity, acceleration, and time.

### Third Equation

**v² = u² + 2as**

Links velocity, initial velocity, acceleration, and displacement.

**Symbols:**
- u = initial velocity (m/s)
- v = final velocity (m/s)
- a = acceleration (m/s²)
- t = time (s)
- s = displacement (m)

> **Key idea:** Each equation involves four quantities. Know any three and you can find the fourth. Pick the equation that contains the unknown you need.

---

## Deriving the Equations

### First Equation: v = u + at

Start from the definition: a = (v − u) / t

Rearrange: **v = u + at**

### Second Equation: s = ut + ½ at²

Distance = area under the velocity–time graph (a trapezium for uniform acceleration):

s = ½ (u + v) × t

Replace v with (u + at):

s = ½ (u + u + at) × t = ½ (2u + at) × t

**s = ut + ½ at²**

### Third Equation: v² = u² + 2as

From the first equation: t = (v − u) / a

Substitute into s = ½ (u + v) × t:

s = (u + v)(v − u) / (2a) = (v² − u²) / (2a)

Rearrange: **v² = u² + 2as**

## Uniform Circular Motion

When an object traces a **circular path** at **constant speed**, it undergoes **uniform circular motion**.

The speed stays the same, yet the **velocity is always changing** because the direction of travel shifts at every point on the circle. This means the motion is **accelerated** -- the acceleration always points toward the centre.

![Object moving in a circular path with velocity arrows showing changing direction at different points](uniform-circular-motion-diagram.png)

### Where You See Circular Motion

- The **Moon** circling Earth
- **Earth** orbiting the Sun
- A child on a **merry-go-round** at a park
- The tip of the **minute hand** of a wall clock
- A **stone** whirled on a string

### Calculating Speed on a Circular Path

For one complete revolution of radius r in time t:

**Speed = 2πr / t**

**Example:** A cyclist rides around a circular track of radius 70 m, completing one lap in 44 s.

Speed = 2 × 3.14 × 70 / 44 = 439.6 / 44 ≈ **10 m/s**

> **Key idea:** Constant speed ≠ constant velocity when the path is curved. In uniform circular motion, the direction keeps changing, so there is always acceleration pointing inward.

## Solved Examples

**Example 1:** A delivery van covers 45 km in 1.5 hours. Average speed = 45 / 1.5 = **30 km/h** = 30 × 5/18 = **8.33 m/s**

**Example 2:** A metro train starts from a station (rest) and reaches 36 m/s in 6 s. Acceleration = (36 − 0) / 6 = **6 m/s²**. Distance = 0 × 6 + ½ × 6 × 36 = **108 m**

**Example 3:** A bicycle moving at 15 m/s is brought to a halt in 5 s. Acceleration = (0 − 15) / 5 = **−3 m/s²**. Distance = 15 × 5 + ½ × (−3) × 25 = 75 − 37.5 = **37.5 m**

## Key Terms to Remember

- **Distance** -- Total length of the path travelled (scalar)
- **Displacement** -- Shortest straight-line gap from start to finish, with direction (vector)
- **Speed** -- Distance per unit time (scalar)
- **Velocity** -- Displacement per unit time (vector)
- **Acceleration** -- Rate at which velocity changes
- **Uniform motion** -- Equal distances covered in every equal time interval
- **Non-uniform motion** -- Unequal distances in equal time intervals
- **Equations of motion** -- v = u + at, s = ut + ½ at², v² = u² + 2as
- **Uniform circular motion** -- Travelling in a circle at constant speed
