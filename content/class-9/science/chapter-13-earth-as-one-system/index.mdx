## Planet Earth — A Single Connected System

When astronauts first photographed Earth from space, they did not see the borders on a political map. They saw one connected planet: blue oceans, green forests, brown deserts, white clouds, white polar ice. The atmosphere a thin halo around it all. No fences, no countries, no separate "regions". Just one Earth.

From up there, it becomes obvious -- the rivers, the air, the soil, the living things, the climate, are not separate things. They are all parts of **one giant system**, constantly exchanging energy and matter.

In this chapter, we will look at Earth as that single system. We will see how its four major "spheres" interact -- and we will face the most urgent question of our generation: what happens when human activity disturbs the system.

> **Key idea:** Earth is one connected system. The air, water, rock, soil, and living things are constantly exchanging energy and matter. Disturb one part of the system, and the whole system responds.

## The Four Spheres of Earth

Scientists divide the Earth system into four major **spheres**, each closely linked to the others.

### 1. Geosphere — the solid Earth

The geosphere is the solid Earth -- the rocks, soils, mountains, plains, the deep interior, and the seafloor. It includes:

- **The crust** -- the thin outer layer (about 5-70 km thick) on which we live.
- **The mantle** -- a thick layer of hot semi-solid rock beneath the crust.
- **The outer core** -- liquid iron and nickel, in motion, which generates Earth's magnetic field.
- **The inner core** -- solid iron-nickel at the centre, even though temperatures exceed 5000°C, because the pressure is so high.

The geosphere is the stage on which all the other spheres play out. Landforms shape climate, soil supports plants, mountains direct rivers.

### 2. Hydrosphere — all the water

The hydrosphere is all of Earth's water -- oceans, rivers, lakes, glaciers, groundwater, and the water held in clouds and atmosphere.

About **97% of Earth's water is in the oceans**. Most of the remaining 3% is locked in **ice caps and glaciers**. Only a tiny fraction is **freshwater** in lakes, rivers, and groundwater that we use for drinking, agriculture, and industry.

The hydrosphere is in constant motion through the **water cycle** -- evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and runoff -- moving water from oceans to clouds to rivers to oceans again.

### 3. Atmosphere — the air

The atmosphere is the blanket of gases surrounding Earth. Its composition by volume:

- **78% nitrogen (N₂)**
- **21% oxygen (O₂)**
- **0.9% argon**
- **0.04% carbon dioxide (CO₂)** -- rising due to human activity
- Trace amounts of water vapour, methane, ozone, neon, helium, krypton

The atmosphere extends from the ground up to about 1000 km (gradually fading into space). It is divided into layers:

- **Troposphere** (0-12 km) -- where all weather happens; we live here.
- **Stratosphere** (12-50 km) -- contains the protective ozone layer; jets fly here.
- **Mesosphere** (50-80 km) -- where most meteors burn up.
- **Thermosphere** (80-700 km) -- contains the auroras.
- **Exosphere** (700+ km) -- fades into space.

The atmosphere protects life by blocking harmful radiation, traps heat (greenhouse effect), and provides the air for living things to breathe.

### 4. Biosphere — all life

The biosphere is the layer of Earth where life exists -- from microscopic bacteria deep in the soil and ocean floor to whales in the deep ocean to plants at high altitudes.

The biosphere is incredibly thin -- a layer just a few kilometres thick across the planet. Most life concentrates in even thinner zones: the top few metres of soil, the photic zone of the ocean (where light penetrates), and the lower part of the atmosphere.

Yet life shapes the entire Earth system. Plants pulled CO₂ out of the atmosphere over hundreds of millions of years, locked it into coal, oil, and limestone. The oxygen we breathe today is the product of photosynthesis by plants and algae over billions of years.

## How the Spheres Interact

The four spheres are not separate -- they are constantly exchanging energy and matter.

### Energy flow

The Sun provides nearly all the energy that drives Earth's systems.

- Sunlight warms the **atmosphere**, driving winds and the water cycle.
- Sunlight warms the **oceans**, evaporating water into clouds.
- Sunlight powers **photosynthesis** in plants, which sustains all the food chains.
- Sunlight warms the **land**, creating temperature contrasts that drive winds.

Earth's interior also contributes energy -- radioactive decay in the core and mantle provides heat that drives plate tectonics, volcanoes, and earthquakes.

### Matter cycles — the great connectors

Matter cycles between the spheres in great loops. Three of the most important:

#### The water cycle

Sun heats the **ocean** → water **evaporates** into **atmosphere** as vapour → vapour cools and **condenses** into clouds → falls as **rain/snow** onto land → flows through **rivers** back to ocean.

Same water has been cycling for billions of years. The water you drink today contains molecules that have been part of every ocean, every glacier, every dinosaur, every Indus Valley civilisation pot.

#### The carbon cycle

CO₂ in the **atmosphere** → absorbed by **plants** through photosynthesis → animals eat plants → animals breathe out CO₂ back to atmosphere. Dead plants and animals get buried, become coal, oil, gas (over millions of years). Volcanic eruptions release CO₂ from the deep Earth back to the atmosphere. Forests and oceans absorb CO₂.

Humans have hugely disturbed this cycle by burning fossil fuels, releasing carbon stored over hundreds of millions of years in just two centuries.

#### The nitrogen cycle

Atmospheric N₂ is unreactive. **Nitrogen-fixing bacteria** in soil (and in plant roots) convert it into nitrogen compounds plants can use. Plants take up these compounds; animals eat plants. When organisms die and decompose, nitrogen returns to the atmosphere as N₂.

The nitrogen cycle is why fertilisers matter. Most farms cannot produce enough food without adding nitrogen compounds — the basis of India's huge fertiliser industry.

> **Key idea:** Matter cycles continuously between the four spheres. The Earth system has been running on solar energy and recycling matter for billions of years.

## The Greenhouse Effect — Earth's Natural Thermostat

Earth's surface is, on average, about 15°C. Without an atmosphere, it would be about -18°C — too cold for liquid water, too cold for life as we know it.

Why are we warmer? Because of the **greenhouse effect**.

How it works:

1. Sunlight (mostly visible light) reaches Earth's surface and heats it.
2. Earth then emits **infrared radiation** (heat) back outward.
3. Some atmospheric gases — **carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), water vapour (H₂O), nitrous oxide (N₂O)** — absorb this infrared radiation.
4. These "greenhouse gases" re-emit the energy in all directions, warming the atmosphere.

A glass greenhouse traps heat the same way. Sun gets in; heat gets trapped. The Earth's atmosphere does the same job, on a planetary scale.

This natural greenhouse effect is essential. Without it, Earth would be frozen.

## Climate Change — When We Disturb the Thermostat

The problem is that humans have added a lot of extra greenhouse gases to the atmosphere over the past 200 years.

The CO₂ level in 1750 (before the Industrial Revolution) was about 280 parts per million (ppm). Today it is over 420 ppm — and rising about 2 ppm every year.

This extra CO₂ comes from:
- **Burning fossil fuels** (coal, oil, gas) for energy, transport, electricity
- **Deforestation** (cutting trees that would have absorbed CO₂)
- **Cement production**
- **Agriculture** (rice fields, cattle release methane)

The result: Earth's average temperature is rising. The 1.1°C warming since pre-industrial times sounds small, but its effects are enormous:

- Glaciers melting — Indian Himalayas have lost large amounts of ice
- Sea levels rising — coastal Indian cities face increasing floods
- Monsoon patterns shifting — extreme rains in some areas, droughts in others
- More extreme heat waves — increasingly severe in north India
- Coral bleaching — affecting reefs in Indian waters
- Species moving — many cannot adapt fast enough

### India's contribution and challenge

India is the world's third-largest emitter of CO₂ (after China and USA). But per person, our emissions are much lower than developed countries. India faces a difficult balance:

- Provide energy and development for 1.4 billion people
- Reduce emissions to avoid worsening climate change
- Adapt to climate impacts that are already happening

India is investing heavily in **solar power**, **wind energy**, and **electric vehicles**. The International Solar Alliance, founded by India, brings together over 100 sun-rich countries to expand solar energy globally. Programmes like Swachh Bharat and Namami Gange address pollution and river health.

> **Key idea:** Climate change is the most serious environmental challenge of our generation. India has both a responsibility (significant emissions) and a right (need for development) — managing this balance is one of the defining tasks of our century.

## Summary

- **Earth is one connected system** of four major spheres: **geosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, biosphere**.
- The **geosphere** is solid Earth (crust, mantle, core). The **hydrosphere** is all water (oceans, rivers, ice). The **atmosphere** is the layer of gases. The **biosphere** is the zone where life exists.
- The Sun drives most energy flow on Earth; Earth's interior contributes some.
- Matter cycles between spheres through the **water cycle, carbon cycle, and nitrogen cycle**.
- The **natural greenhouse effect** keeps Earth warm enough for life (without it, ~-18°C).
- Human activity (burning fossil fuels, deforestation) is adding extra CO₂, methane, and other greenhouse gases.
- The result is **climate change** — rising temperatures, melting glaciers, rising sea levels, shifting monsoons, more extreme weather.
- India is investing in solar, wind, and other clean energy to balance development with sustainability.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Glaciers in the Himalayas**. The 9,500 glaciers of the Indian Himalayas feed the Ganga, Brahmaputra, and other major rivers. As they melt due to warming temperatures, they initially feed more water into rivers, but eventually the rivers will carry less water in dry seasons — affecting hundreds of millions of people who depend on them.

**The International Solar Alliance**. Founded by India in 2015, the ISA now includes over 100 countries. It promotes solar energy adoption to help reduce CO₂ emissions globally. India's National Solar Mission aims to install 500 GW of renewable energy capacity by 2030.

**Mumbai's flooding**. Mumbai and other coastal cities face increasing flooding from a combination of rising sea levels and more intense monsoon rains. Engineers are redesigning drainage systems and building sea walls to adapt.

**The 2010 Indus floods, 2015 Chennai floods, 2018 Kerala floods, 2023 Sikkim flash floods**. Increasingly extreme weather is one of the most visible signs of climate change affecting India. Disaster preparedness and rapid response have become essential parts of state planning.

### Try it at home — Tracking your family's carbon footprint

**What you'll need:** Your wonder notebook, pen, your family's monthly electricity bill, your family's gas/LPG bill, and an honest day's observation.

**Steps:**
1. Read your last electricity bill. Note the units (kWh) used.
2. Read your last LPG bill. Note the cylinders used per month.
3. Add up family travel (car, scooter, bus, train) in a typical week.
4. Estimate other items: AC use, fan hours, water heater use, frequency of takeaway food.

**Some rough calculations:**
- 1 kWh of electricity in India causes about 0.7 kg of CO₂ (mostly from coal power plants).
- 1 LPG cylinder (14.2 kg) releases about 42 kg of CO₂ when burned.
- 1 litre of petrol in a scooter or car releases about 2.3 kg of CO₂.

A typical Indian family of 4 might use 200 kWh/month (electricity) + 1 LPG cylinder + 100 litres of petrol = 140 + 42 + 230 = about **412 kg of CO₂ per month** = about **5,000 kg of CO₂ per year**. The Indian average is about 1.9 tonnes per person; rich-country averages are 15-20 tonnes per person.

**Reflect:**
- Where could your family reduce? (Cleaner travel? More efficient appliances?)
- What does India look like if all families do small changes?
- What are the larger industrial and policy changes that need to happen too?

You have just calculated something that climate scientists call a "household carbon footprint" — a measure of how your daily life connects to Earth's atmosphere. Each of us is part of the Earth system. Each of us can choose to be part of the solution.
