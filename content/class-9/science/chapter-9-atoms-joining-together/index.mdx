## When Atoms Get Together

In Chapter 8, we saw that an atom is the smallest piece of an element. But most things you encounter in daily life are not single atoms -- they are **combinations** of atoms.

A water droplet is made of trillions of water molecules, each one a combination of 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom. Common salt is sodium atoms linked with chlorine atoms. Sugar is a combination of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. The air you breathe is a mixture of oxygen molecules (O₂), nitrogen molecules (N₂), and many other combinations.

When atoms join together, they form **molecules** and **compounds**. In this chapter, we will learn the rules that govern these combinations and the chemical language we use to describe them.

---

## The Laws of Chemical Combination

Even before atoms were proven to exist, scientists noticed striking patterns in how substances combined. Two of these patterns are so important that they earned the name **laws of chemical combination**.

### Law of conservation of mass (Lavoisier, 1789)

**In any chemical reaction, the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.** No mass is created or destroyed.

If you burn 10 grams of magnesium ribbon in oxygen, you do not end up with 10 grams of magnesium oxide -- you end up with more, because the oxygen that combined with the magnesium also contributes to the mass. But if you carefully weigh the magnesium plus the oxygen that combined with it (before reaction) and compare to the magnesium oxide (after), the two are equal.

This law was first proved by the French chemist **Antoine Lavoisier**, often called the father of modern chemistry, just before he was guillotined during the French Revolution in 1794.

### Law of constant proportions (Proust, 1799)

**In a pure compound, the elements are always present in the same fixed ratio by mass, regardless of where it came from or how it was made.**

Pure water -- whether from a Himalayan glacier, the Ganga at Varanasi, the Indian Ocean (after desalination), or made by burning hydrogen -- is always 11.1% hydrogen and 88.9% oxygen by mass. Always.

Sodium chloride is always 39.3% sodium and 60.7% chlorine, whether you make it in a lab or get it from a Gujarati saltpan.

These two laws, together, were a clue: they only make sense if matter is made of discrete particles that combine in fixed whole-number proportions. Soon, Dalton's atomic theory gave the explanation.

> **Key idea:** Mass is conserved in chemical reactions, and elements combine in fixed mass ratios in any pure compound. These two laws are the foundation of all chemistry.

## Molecules

A **molecule** is the smallest unit of a substance that can exist independently and shows the properties of that substance. Molecules are formed when two or more atoms join together.

### Molecules of elements

Some elements exist as molecules even though they are not mixed with anything else.

- **Oxygen (O₂)** -- two oxygen atoms joined together. The oxygen in the air is O₂.
- **Hydrogen (H₂)** -- two hydrogen atoms.
- **Nitrogen (N₂)** -- two nitrogen atoms; about 78% of air.
- **Ozone (O₃)** -- three oxygen atoms; found in the upper atmosphere.

The number of atoms in a molecule of an element is called its **atomicity**.

- Monatomic (1 atom): noble gases (He, Ne, Ar)
- Diatomic (2 atoms): H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂
- Triatomic (3 atoms): O₃
- Tetra-atomic (4 atoms): P₄ (white phosphorus)

### Molecules of compounds

When atoms of **different** elements combine in a fixed ratio, we get a **compound**.

- **Water (H₂O)** -- 2 hydrogen atoms + 1 oxygen atom
- **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)** -- 1 carbon atom + 2 oxygen atoms
- **Methane (CH₄)** -- 1 carbon atom + 4 hydrogen atoms
- **Ammonia (NH₃)** -- 1 nitrogen atom + 3 hydrogen atoms

Compounds have completely different properties from the elements that compose them. Hydrogen burns; oxygen supports burning; but together as water, they put out fires.

## Ions and Ionic Compounds

When some atoms react, they do not share electrons -- they **transfer** them. The atom that loses electrons becomes a **positive ion (cation)**; the atom that gains electrons becomes a **negative ion (anion)**.

- Sodium loses 1 electron → Na⁺ (sodium ion)
- Chlorine gains 1 electron → Cl⁻ (chloride ion)
- These two are oppositely charged and attract each other → form **sodium chloride (NaCl)**

### Common ions and their charges

**Cations (positive ions):**

| Ion | Symbol | Charge |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | H⁺ | +1 |
| Sodium | Na⁺ | +1 |
| Potassium | K⁺ | +1 |
| Calcium | Ca²⁺ | +2 |
| Magnesium | Mg²⁺ | +2 |
| Aluminium | Al³⁺ | +3 |
| Iron(II) | Fe²⁺ | +2 |
| Iron(III) | Fe³⁺ | +3 |

**Anions (negative ions):**

| Ion | Symbol | Charge |
|---|---|---|
| Chloride | Cl⁻ | -1 |
| Hydroxide | OH⁻ | -1 |
| Nitrate | NO₃⁻ | -1 |
| Bicarbonate | HCO₃⁻ | -1 |
| Sulphate | SO₄²⁻ | -2 |
| Carbonate | CO₃²⁻ | -2 |
| Phosphate | PO₄³⁻ | -3 |

## Writing Chemical Formulas

A **chemical formula** tells you which elements (and how many atoms of each) are in a compound.

### How to write a simple ionic formula

1. Write the symbols of the two ions, positive first.
2. Use the magnitude of each charge as the subscript of the OTHER ion.
3. Simplify if both subscripts have a common factor.

**Examples:**

- Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl (subscripts cancel)
- Ca²⁺ + Cl⁻ → CaCl₂ (calcium chloride)
- Al³⁺ + O²⁻ → Al₂O₃ (aluminium oxide)
- Mg²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → MgSO₄ (magnesium sulphate, the "Epsom salt" of medicine)
- Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CaCO₃ (calcium carbonate, the limestone of the Earth's crust)

> **Key idea:** A compound is electrically neutral overall. The subscripts in a chemical formula balance the charges of the ions.

## Atomic Mass and Molecular Mass

### Atomic mass

The mass of an atom is incredibly tiny -- on the order of 10⁻²⁷ kg. Working with such small numbers is inconvenient, so chemists use a **relative scale**.

The **atomic mass unit (u)** is defined as 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. On this scale:

- Hydrogen (H): 1 u
- Carbon (C): 12 u
- Nitrogen (N): 14 u
- Oxygen (O): 16 u
- Sodium (Na): 23 u
- Magnesium (Mg): 24 u
- Sulphur (S): 32 u
- Chlorine (Cl): 35.5 u
- Calcium (Ca): 40 u
- Iron (Fe): 56 u

### Molecular mass

The **molecular mass** of a compound is the sum of the atomic masses of all the atoms in one molecule.

- Water (H₂O): 2(1) + 16 = **18 u**
- Carbon dioxide (CO₂): 12 + 2(16) = **44 u**
- Methane (CH₄): 12 + 4(1) = **16 u**
- Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄): 2(1) + 32 + 4(16) = **98 u**
- Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆): 6(12) + 12(1) + 6(16) = **180 u**

## The Mole Concept

A single atom is so small that no chemist works with them one at a time. Instead, chemists count atoms in HUGE BATCHES called **moles**.

**1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles**

This number is called **Avogadro's number** (N_A), after the Italian scientist who first proposed it.

- 1 mole of atoms = 6.022 × 10²³ atoms
- 1 mole of molecules = 6.022 × 10²³ molecules
- 1 mole of ions = 6.022 × 10²³ ions

### Molar mass

The genius of the mole is that **the mass of one mole of a substance in grams equals its atomic or molecular mass in u**.

- 1 mole of H atoms weighs 1 g
- 1 mole of O atoms weighs 16 g
- 1 mole of H₂O molecules weighs 18 g
- 1 mole of CO₂ molecules weighs 44 g
- 1 mole of glucose weighs 180 g

So 18 g of water (about 3 spoonfuls) contains 6.022 × 10²³ molecules of water -- 600 sextillion molecules. The mole lets you count atoms by weighing!

> **Key idea:** The mole is chemistry's counting unit. 1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles, and the mass of 1 mole in grams = the atomic/molecular mass in u.

---

## Summary

- **Law of conservation of mass**: Mass is neither created nor destroyed in chemical reactions.
- **Law of constant proportions**: In any pure compound, elements are present in fixed mass ratios.
- A **molecule** is the smallest particle of a substance that can exist independently.
- **Atomicity** = number of atoms in one molecule of an element.
- **Compounds** are made of two or more different elements bonded in a fixed ratio.
- **Ions** are charged atoms or groups of atoms. **Cations** are positive (lose electrons); **anions** are negative (gain electrons).
- A chemical formula uses the charges of ions to balance them (criss-cross method).
- **Atomic mass** is measured in atomic mass units (u), with 1 u = 1/12 the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
- **Molecular mass** = sum of atomic masses of all atoms in the molecule.
- **1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles** (Avogadro's number).
- **Molar mass in grams = molecular mass in u**.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Indian fertiliser industry**. India's huge agriculture depends on synthetic fertilisers like urea (NH₂CONH₂), ammonium sulphate ((NH₄)₂SO₄), and DAP (di-ammonium phosphate). Every bag of fertiliser produced at IFFCO and KRIBHCO plants has its chemical formula and composition guaranteed by the laws of constant proportion. Farmers in Punjab can trust that 50 kg of urea always contains the same proportion of nitrogen.

**Indian pharmaceutical industry**. India is one of the world's largest producers of generic medicines. Companies like Cipla, Dr Reddy's, and Sun Pharma make tablets where each pill has an exact, measurable amount of active ingredient — often a few milligrams. The mole concept and molar mass calculations are central to drug formulation. A 500 mg paracetamol tablet contains exactly the right number of moles of paracetamol (about 3.3 × 10⁻³ moles), calculated from the molar mass.

**Cement production at UltraTech, Ambuja, ACC**. Indian cement plants combine limestone (CaCO₃), clay (mostly Al₂Si₂O₅(OH)₄), and gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) in fixed mass proportions. The exact ratios are dictated by chemistry, calculated using atomic and molecular masses.

### Try it at home — Molecular mass calculations

**What you'll need:** A pen, paper, and atomic masses from this chapter.

**Try these:**

1. **Calcium chloride (CaCl₂)** — used in winter to melt ice. Calculate its molecular mass.
   - Calcium: 40, Chlorine: 35.5
   - CaCl₂ = 40 + 2(35.5) = 40 + 71 = **111 u**

2. **Ammonium nitrate (NH₄NO₃)** — a common fertiliser. Calculate its molecular mass.
   - Nitrogen: 14, Hydrogen: 1, Oxygen: 16
   - NH₄NO₃ = 14 + 4(1) + 14 + 3(16) = 14 + 4 + 14 + 48 = **80 u**

3. **Aspirin (C₉H₈O₄)** — the famous painkiller. Calculate its molecular mass.
   - Carbon: 12, Hydrogen: 1, Oxygen: 16
   - C₉H₈O₄ = 9(12) + 8(1) + 4(16) = 108 + 8 + 64 = **180 u**

4. How many molecules are in **0.5 moles of water**?
   - 0.5 × Avogadro's number = 0.5 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **3.011 × 10²³ molecules**.

You have just done what every pharmaceutical and chemical engineer in India does daily — turning chemical formulas into mass and number calculations.
