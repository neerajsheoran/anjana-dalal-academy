## Introduction

The notion that everything in the world is built from incredibly tiny, indivisible building blocks has deep roots. Around 600 BCE, the Indian sage **Maharishi Kanad** reasoned that if you keep breaking a piece of matter into smaller and smaller pieces, you must eventually reach a particle so small it cannot be broken any further. He named this ultimate particle **Parmanu**. His contemporary, **Pakudha Katyayama**, added that these tiny particles usually stick together rather than existing alone.

Half a world away, the Greek thinker **Democritus** arrived at a strikingly similar conclusion. He coined the term **atomos** (meaning "uncuttable"), from which the modern word **atom** derives.

For over two thousand years, these remained philosophical ideas. It was only in the early 1800s that experimental science caught up, and researchers began uncovering the precise rules by which atoms combine.

## Laws of Chemical Combination

### 1. Law of Conservation of Mass

> **Key idea:** During any chemical reaction, mass is neither generated nor lost. The total mass you start with equals the total mass you end with.

**Illustration:** Suppose 4.0 g of sodium hydroxide reacts with 7.3 g of hydrochloric acid, producing 5.85 g of sodium chloride and 5.45 g of water (numbers are simplified for learning):
- Total mass before reaction = 4.0 + 7.3 = **11.3 g**
- Total mass after reaction = 5.85 + 5.45 = **11.3 g**

The figures match perfectly, confirming that mass was conserved.

### 2. Law of Constant Proportions (Law of Definite Proportions)

> **Key idea:** A given chemical compound always contains the same elements united in an identical ratio by mass, no matter how or where the compound is prepared.

**Illustrations:**
- In **water (H2O)**, hydrogen and oxygen are always present in a **1:8 mass ratio**. Decompose 18 g of water and you invariably get 2 g of hydrogen and 16 g of oxygen.
- In **carbon dioxide (CO2)**, carbon and oxygen are always joined in a **3:8 mass ratio**.

Whether you collect water from a Himalayan stream or produce it in a Delhi laboratory, the ratio never changes.

## Dalton's Atomic Theory

In 1808, English schoolteacher **John Dalton** wove together the existing laws of chemical combination into a coherent theory. His central postulates:

1. All matter is composed of exceedingly tiny particles called **atoms**
2. Atoms cannot be **created, divided, or destroyed** during a chemical reaction
3. All atoms of a particular element are **identical** in mass and properties
4. Atoms of different elements **differ** in mass and properties
5. Atoms unite in **simple whole-number ratios** to produce compounds
6. In a given compound, the **types and numbers of atoms** are always the same

> **Key idea:** Dalton's theory neatly accounted for the law of conservation of mass (atoms are neither born nor annihilated) and the law of constant proportions (atoms combine in fixed ratios).

**A modern note:** We now know that atoms are themselves made of still tinier particles -- electrons, protons, and neutrons. We also know about isotopes (atoms of the same element with different masses). Despite these refinements, Dalton's foundational insight -- that matter comes in discrete units -- remains the bedrock of chemistry.

## What Is an Atom?

An **atom** is the tiniest unit of an element that still behaves chemically like that element. Atoms are the fundamental construction blocks of all material things.

### The Mind-Boggling Smallness of Atoms

- Scientists measure atomic dimensions in **nanometres (nm)**: 1 nm = 10 to the power -9 m
- Line up roughly ten million atoms side by side and you would barely span the width of your fingernail
- A single grain of rice contains more atoms than the number of grains of rice that could fill the entire Indian Ocean

### Element Symbols

**Dalton** drew circular symbols for elements, but these proved cumbersome. Later, the Swedish chemist **Berzelius** proposed a cleaner system using letters from each element's name.

**Convention:**
- The **first letter** is always written in **uppercase**
- If a **second letter** is needed, it is always **lowercase**

**Examples:**
- Hydrogen = **H**, Nitrogen = **N**, Oxygen = **O**
- Calcium = **Ca** (never CA), Aluminium = **Al** (never AL)
- Cobalt = **Co** (note: CO with capital O would mean carbon monoxide!)
- Bromine = **Br**, Silicon = **Si**

Several symbols trace back to **Latin names**:
- Iron = **Fe** (Latin: ferrum)
- Gold = **Au** (Latin: aurum)
- Silver = **Ag** (Latin: argentum)
- Sodium = **Na** (Latin: natrium)
- Potassium = **K** (Latin: kalium)
- Copper = **Cu** (Latin: cuprum)
- Tin = **Sn** (Latin: stannum)

The international body **IUPAC** (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) officially approves element names and symbols.

### Atomic Mass

Because individual atoms are far too light to weigh directly, scientists compare their masses to a standard reference.

**One atomic mass unit (u)** is defined as exactly **1/12th of the mass of one carbon-12 atom**.

| Element | Atomic Mass (u) |
|---|---|
| Hydrogen (H) | 1 |
| Carbon (C) | 12 |
| Nitrogen (N) | 14 |
| Oxygen (O) | 16 |
| Sodium (Na) | 23 |
| Magnesium (Mg) | 24 |
| Sulphur (S) | 32 |
| Chlorine (Cl) | 35.5 |
| Calcium (Ca) | 40 |
| Iron (Fe) | 56 |

> **Key idea:** Atomic mass is a relative measure -- it tells you how many times heavier an atom is compared to 1/12th of a carbon-12 atom. The symbol "u" stands for unified atomic mass unit.

### Can Atoms Exist Alone?

The vast majority of atoms **do not roam free**. They latch onto other atoms to form **molecules** or **ions**. These molecules and ions then cluster in enormous numbers to build the substances we see, touch, and use daily.

## What Is a Molecule?

A **molecule** is the smallest independent unit of an element or compound. It consists of two or more atoms bonded together and carries all the characteristic properties of that substance.

### Molecules of Elements

When atoms of the **same element** bond together, they produce molecules of that element.

| Category | Element | Atoms per Molecule (Atomicity) | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monoatomic | Helium, Neon | 1 | He, Ne |
| Diatomic | Hydrogen | 2 | H2 |
| Diatomic | Oxygen | 2 | O2 |
| Diatomic | Nitrogen | 2 | N2 |
| Diatomic | Chlorine | 2 | Cl2 |
| Triatomic | Ozone | 3 | O3 |
| Tetra-atomic | Phosphorus | 4 | P4 |
| Octa-atomic | Sulphur | 8 | S8 |

**Atomicity** = the count of atoms within one molecule of an element.

### Molecules of Compounds

When atoms of **different elements** join in a definite ratio, they create molecules of compounds.

| Compound | Constituent Elements | Mass Ratio | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | Hydrogen, Oxygen | 1:8 | H2O |
| Ammonia | Nitrogen, Hydrogen | 14:3 | NH3 |
| Carbon dioxide | Carbon, Oxygen | 3:8 | CO2 |

## What Is an Ion?

Atoms sometimes **gain or lose electrons**, acquiring an electrical charge in the process. Such charged particles are called **ions**.

- **Cation** -- carries a positive charge (the atom gave away one or more electrons)
  - Examples: Na+ (sodium ion), K+ (potassium ion), Ca2+ (calcium ion)
- **Anion** -- carries a negative charge (the atom accepted one or more electrons)
  - Examples: Cl- (chloride ion), S2- (sulphide ion), O2- (oxide ion)

A **polyatomic ion** is a cluster of atoms that collectively bears a charge.

**Common polyatomic ions:**
- Ammonium: NH4+
- Hydroxide: OH-
- Nitrate: NO3-
- Carbonate: CO3 2-
- Sulphate: SO4 2-
- Phosphate: PO4 3-

> **Key idea:** Ionic compounds are built from cations and anions. The total positive charge must perfectly cancel the total negative charge, rendering the compound electrically neutral overall.

## Writing Chemical Formulae

The **chemical formula** of a compound is a shorthand that reveals which elements are present and the number of atoms of each in one formula unit.

### Guiding Principles

1. **Charges must balance** -- total positive charge equals total negative charge
2. Write the **metal (or cation) first**, then the **non-metal (or anion)**
3. If a **polyatomic ion** appears more than once, enclose it in **brackets** with the appropriate subscript

### Valency

**Valency** is the combining power of an element -- it indicates how many atoms of another element one atom can bond with.

| Valency | Metals | Non-metals | Polyatomic Ions |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Na+, K+, Ag+ | H+, Cl-, Br- | NH4+, OH-, NO3- |
| 2 | Mg2+, Ca2+, Zn2+, Cu2+ | O2-, S2- | CO3 2-, SO4 2- |
| 3 | Al3+, Fe3+ | N3- | PO4 3- |

### The Criss-Cross Technique

**Step 1:** Place the symbols of the two elements or ions side by side.

**Step 2:** Note their valencies.

**Step 3:** Swap (criss-cross) the valencies -- the valency of one becomes the subscript of the other.

**Step 4:** Simplify if both subscripts share a common factor.

**Worked examples:**

**Potassium oxide:** K has valency 1, O has valency 2. Criss-cross gives **K2O**.

**Magnesium chloride:** Mg has valency 2, Cl has valency 1. Criss-cross gives **MgCl2**.

**Aluminium oxide:** Al has valency 3, O has valency 2. Criss-cross gives **Al2O3**.

**Calcium chloride:** Ca has valency 2, Cl has valency 1. Criss-cross gives **CaCl2**.

**Zinc oxide:** Zn has valency 2, O has valency 2. Criss-cross yields Zn2O2, which simplifies to **ZnO**.

**Sodium hydroxide:** Na has valency 1, OH has valency 1. Formula is **NaOH**.

**Magnesium hydroxide:** Mg has valency 2, OH has valency 1. Formula is **Mg(OH)2** -- brackets around OH because two hydroxide groups are needed.

**Aluminium sulphate:** Al has valency 3, SO4 has valency 2. Formula is **Al2(SO4)3** -- brackets around SO4 with subscript 3.

**Ammonium phosphate:** NH4 has valency 1, PO4 has valency 3. Formula is **(NH4)3PO4**.

> **Key idea:** Whenever a polyatomic ion must appear more than once in a formula, wrap it in brackets and put the count as a subscript outside. For instance, Ca(OH)2 means one calcium paired with two hydroxide units.

## Molecular Mass and Formula Unit Mass

### Molecular Mass

The **molecular mass** of a substance equals the sum of the atomic masses of every atom present in one molecule.

**Example 1:** Molecular mass of water (H2O)
= 2 x 1 + 1 x 16 = **18 u**

**Example 2:** Molecular mass of sulphuric acid (H2SO4)
= 2 x 1 + 1 x 32 + 4 x 16 = 2 + 32 + 64 = **98 u**

**Example 3:** Molecular mass of sulphur molecule (S8)
= 8 x 32 = **256 u**

### Formula Unit Mass

**Ionic compounds** do not form discrete molecules -- they build vast crystal lattices. So we speak of a **formula unit** instead of a molecule, and calculate the **formula unit mass** in the same way.

**Example:** Formula unit mass of NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = **58.5 u**

**Example:** Formula unit mass of MgCl2 = 24 + 2 x 35.5 = 24 + 71 = **95 u**

**Example:** Formula unit mass of Na2O = 2 x 23 + 16 = 46 + 16 = **62 u**

**Example:** Formula unit mass of CaCO3 = 40 + 12 + 3 x 16 = 40 + 12 + 48 = **100 u**

> **Key idea:** Whether you call it molecular mass or formula unit mass, the arithmetic is the same: add up the atomic masses of every atom in the formula.

## Key Terms to Remember

- **Atom** -- The smallest unit of an element that keeps the element's chemical character
- **Molecule** -- The smallest independent unit of a substance, formed by bonding two or more atoms
- **Ion** -- An atom or group of atoms carrying an electrical charge
- **Cation** -- A positively charged ion
- **Anion** -- A negatively charged ion
- **Polyatomic ion** -- A group of bonded atoms that collectively carries a charge
- **Valency** -- An element's combining capacity
- **Atomic mass unit (u)** -- Defined as 1/12th the mass of a single carbon-12 atom
- **Molecular mass** -- Total of atomic masses of all atoms in one molecule
- **Formula unit mass** -- Total of atomic masses of all atoms in one formula unit of an ionic compound
- **Atomicity** -- The count of atoms in a single molecule of an element
- **Chemical formula** -- A concise notation showing which elements and how many atoms make up a compound
