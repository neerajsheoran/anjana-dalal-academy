## What is Sound?

Close your eyes for a moment and listen. Perhaps you hear an auto-rickshaw honking, your mother calling from the kitchen, or the hum of a ceiling fan. **Sound** is a form of energy that triggers the sensation of hearing. But where does it come from, and how does it travel from its source all the way to your ears?

### How is Sound Produced?

Every sound originates from a **vibrating object**. The vibrating body disturbs the particles of the surrounding medium (such as air), and these disturbances propagate outward as waves.

**Everyday examples:**
- Plucking the wire of a sitar sets the string into rapid vibration, producing musical notes
- Striking a dholak makes its stretched membrane shake back and forth
- When you speak or sing, your **vocal cords** in the throat vibrate
- Tapping a metal glass with a spoon makes the glass walls vibrate and ring

> **Key idea:** No vibration, no sound. The moment vibrations cease, the sound dies away. Place your fingers on a tabla while it is being played -- you can feel the vibrations directly.

## Sound as a Wave

### How Does Sound Travel?

When a vibrating body pushes the air molecules closest to it, those molecules bump into their neighbours, which bump into the next set, and so on. The disturbance spreads outward through the medium like a chain of dominoes. The individual air molecules do not journey from the source to your ear; they merely jiggle back and forth in place.

### Longitudinal Waves

Sound waves belong to the category of **longitudinal waves**. In such a wave, the particles of the medium oscillate **back and forth** along the **same line** as the wave's direction of travel.

This back-and-forth motion produces alternating zones:
- **Compression** -- particles crowd together (region of high density and high pressure)
- **Rarefaction** -- particles spread apart (region of low density and low pressure)

A sound wave is essentially a train of compressions and rarefactions marching through the medium.

![Longitudinal wave showing alternating compressions (dense regions) and rarefactions (sparse regions) with direction of wave travel](longitudinal-wave-compressions-rarefactions.png)

> **Key idea:** In a longitudinal wave, particle vibration is parallel to wave propagation. Sound is always a longitudinal wave when travelling through air.

### Transverse Waves

In a **transverse wave**, particles move **perpendicular** to the wave's travel direction. Ripples on a pond surface and waves travelling along a rope are transverse. Light also behaves as a transverse wave.

**Sound cannot travel through a vacuum.** It requires a material medium -- solid, liquid, or gas. The classic bell-jar experiment proves this: place a ringing electric bell inside a glass jar and gradually pump out the air. As the air thins, the ringing fades, and in near-complete vacuum the bell can be seen vibrating but produces no audible sound at all.

## Characteristics of Sound Waves

### 1. Wavelength

**Wavelength** is the gap between two successive compressions (or two successive rarefactions). It is symbolised by the Greek letter **lambda**.

**SI unit:** metre (m)

### 2. Frequency

**Frequency** counts how many complete oscillations occur every second.

**Frequency = Number of oscillations / Time**

**f = 1 / T**

- **SI unit:** Hertz (Hz)
- 1 Hz means one complete vibration per second
- **T** is the **time period** -- the duration of a single complete oscillation

### 3. Time Period

**Time period (T)** is the time needed for one full oscillation cycle.

**T = 1 / f**

**SI unit:** second (s)

### 4. Amplitude

**Amplitude** is the farthest a vibrating particle moves from its rest (equilibrium) position. It governs the **loudness** of the sound.

![Sound wave diagram showing wavelength, amplitude, time period, compression and rarefaction labeled](sound-wave-characteristics-labeled.png)

**SI unit:** metre (m)

## Speed of Sound

The **speed of sound** is the distance the wave covers per unit time.

**Speed = Wavelength x Frequency**

**v = lambda x f**

### Speed of Sound in Different Media

Sound races through different materials at very different speeds:
- **Solids:** fastest (roughly 5960 m/s in steel)
- **Liquids:** moderate (roughly 1531 m/s in water)
- **Gases:** slowest (roughly 346 m/s in air at room temperature)

> **Key idea:** Particles in a solid are packed tightly, transmitting vibrations swiftly. Particles in a gas are far apart and pass vibrations along more slowly. This is why you can hear a distant train sooner by pressing your ear to the steel rail than by listening through the air.

The speed of sound in air rises with **temperature**. At 0 degrees C it is about 331 m/s; at roughly 22 degrees C (a typical Indian room temperature) it is about 344 m/s.

## Loudness and Pitch

### Loudness

**Loudness** is governed by the **amplitude** of the sound wave. Bigger amplitude means louder sound.

- Loudness is expressed in **decibels (dB)**
- Normal talking: around 60 dB
- A soft whisper: around 20 dB
- Sounds beyond 80 dB can damage hearing over time

Loudness also diminishes as you move further from the source.

### Pitch

**Pitch** is controlled by the **frequency** of the sound wave.

- **High frequency** = high pitch (shrill) -- e.g., a referee's whistle, a sparrow's chirp
- **Low frequency** = low pitch (deep/bass) -- e.g., a dhol's boom, thunder rumbling

Women and children typically have higher-pitched voices than adult men because their vocal cords vibrate at greater frequencies.

### Quality (Timbre)

**Quality** or **timbre** lets you distinguish two sounds that have identical pitch and loudness but come from different sources. It depends on the **shape of the sound wave** (its waveform).

Timbre is why you can instantly tell whether a harmonium or a bansuri is playing the same note.

## Audible and Inaudible Sounds

### Range of Hearing

- **Audible range** for humans: **20 Hz to 20,000 Hz** (20 kHz)
- Frequencies below 20 Hz are **infrasound** -- elephants and rhinoceroses communicate using these low rumbles
- Frequencies above 20,000 Hz are **ultrasound** -- bats and dolphins rely on these high-pitched signals for navigation

### Applications of Ultrasound

Ultrasound is tremendously useful in modern life:
- **Ultrasonography (sonography)** -- creates images of internal body organs; widely used during pregnancy to monitor the developing baby
- **Echocardiography** -- uses ultrasound to examine the heart's chambers and valves
- **Industrial cleaning** -- ultrasonic vibrations dislodge grime from intricate machinery parts
- **Lithotripsy** -- focused ultrasound pulses shatter kidney stones into tiny fragments that pass naturally
- **Flaw detection** -- ultrasonic pulses detect hidden cracks inside metal castings and welded joints

## Reflection of Sound

Sound waves follow the same **laws of reflection** as light:
1. The angle at which sound arrives equals the angle at which it bounces off
2. The incoming wave, the reflected wave, and the perpendicular to the surface all lie in one plane

### Echo

An **echo** is a distinct repetition of a sound caused by its **reflection** off a hard surface such as a cliff, a tall building, or a boundary wall.

**Requirements for hearing a clear echo:**
- The reflected sound must arrive at the listener's ear **at least 0.1 seconds** after the original
- At room temperature (speed of sound about 344 m/s), the reflecting surface must be at least about **17.2 m** away

**Quick derivation:** Total distance = speed x time = 344 x 0.1 = 34.4 m. Since sound goes to the wall and returns, minimum one-way distance = 34.4 / 2 = 17.2 m.

### Reverberation

In an enclosed space like a large hall, sound bounces off walls, ceiling, and floor multiple times. These overlapping reflections blend with the original sound, creating a drawn-out, lingering effect called **reverberation**.

Strategies to control reverberation in auditoriums and cinema halls:
- Line walls and ceilings with **sound-absorbing materials** -- thick curtains, acoustic foam, perforated boards
- Avoid large curved surfaces that focus reflected sound into one spot

---

## Uses of Multiple Reflection of Sound

- **Megaphones and loudspeaker horns** -- funnel-shaped tubes that channel sound forward, reducing sideways spreading
- **Stethoscope** -- the rubber tube guides sound via multiple internal reflections from the patient's chest to the doctor's ears
- **Soundboards in concert halls** -- large curved panels behind the stage reflect performers' voices towards the audience seated far away

## SONAR

**SONAR** stands for **Sound Navigation And Ranging**. It employs ultrasonic pulses to measure distances, locate underwater objects, and map the seabed.

![SONAR working principle: ship sending ultrasonic waves to ocean floor and receiving reflected waves](sonar-working-principle.png)

**How SONAR operates:**
1. A transmitter aboard a ship fires a burst of ultrasonic waves downward into the water
2. The waves travel through the water, strike an object (submarine, rock, school of fish, or the ocean floor), and bounce back
3. A receiver picks up the returning echo
4. The elapsed time between transmission and reception is recorded
5. Distance is calculated: **d = v x t / 2** (dividing by 2 because the pulse travels to the target and back)

### Uses of SONAR

- Charting **ocean depth** for navigation safety
- Detecting **submarines** and underwater mines
- Locating **schools of fish** for fishing fleets
- Mapping the **sea floor** for geological research

## Auditory System: How We Hear

Our **ear** converts sound waves into signals the brain can interpret. It has three main sections:

![Labeled diagram of the human ear showing outer ear (pinna), middle ear (hammer, anvil, stirrup), and inner ear (cochlea)](human-ear-structure-labeled.png)

**Outer ear (pinna)** -- the visible flap that gathers sound waves and funnels them through the ear canal towards the eardrum

**Middle ear** -- houses three minuscule bones nicknamed the hammer, anvil, and stirrup (the tiniest bones in the human body). When sound waves strike the eardrum (tympanic membrane), it vibrates. These three bones amplify and relay the vibrations inward.

**Inner ear (cochlea)** -- a snail-shaped, fluid-filled chamber lined with thousands of microscopic hair cells. These cells transform mechanical vibrations into electrical impulses, which race along the auditory nerve to the brain. The brain then interprets these signals as recognisable sounds.

> **Key idea:** The ear is a transducer -- it converts mechanical wave energy into electrical signals for the brain.

## Key Terms to Remember

- **Compression** -- High-pressure, high-density zone in a longitudinal wave
- **Rarefaction** -- Low-pressure, low-density zone in a longitudinal wave
- **Wavelength** -- Centre-to-centre distance between two consecutive compressions or rarefactions
- **Frequency** -- Count of complete oscillations per second (measured in Hz)
- **Amplitude** -- Maximum displacement of a particle from its equilibrium position
- **Loudness** -- Perceived volume of sound; depends on amplitude (measured in dB)
- **Pitch** -- Perceived shrillness or deepness; depends on frequency
- **Timbre** -- The unique tonal quality that differentiates two instruments playing the same note
- **Echo** -- A reflected sound heard distinctly after a noticeable delay
- **Reverberation** -- Prolonged persistence of sound due to rapid successive reflections in an enclosed space
- **Ultrasound** -- Sound with frequency above 20,000 Hz
- **Infrasound** -- Sound with frequency below 20 Hz
- **SONAR** -- Technology using ultrasound for underwater detection and distance measurement
