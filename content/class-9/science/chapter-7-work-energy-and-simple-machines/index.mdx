## Work, Energy, and Power — The Physics Definitions

You hear the words **work**, **energy**, and **power** in conversation every day. A teacher gives you "work" to do for tomorrow. A friend feels "low on energy" after a long day. A leader is described as having "great power" over people. These are all valid uses of the words in everyday English -- but in physics, each has a very specific, very different meaning.

In this chapter, you will learn what physics means by these words. The definitions are precise and powerful enough that they describe everything from a falling apple to a satellite in orbit.

---

## Work — More Than Just Effort

In physics, **work is done only when a force causes an object to move**. Pushing a wall as hard as you can for an hour does no work in the physics sense, because the wall does not move.

**Work = Force × Displacement (in the direction of the force)**

**W = F × s**

- **W** = work done (in joules, J)
- **F** = force applied (in newtons, N)
- **s** = displacement in the direction of the force (in metres, m)

The SI unit of work is the **joule (J)**. **1 joule = 1 newton × 1 metre**. If you push a box with a force of 10 N and it moves 2 m in the direction of your push, the work done is 20 J.

### When is work zero?

Three situations where physics says no work was done:

1. **No displacement** -- pushing a wall that does not move. W = 0.
2. **No force** -- a stone rolling on a perfectly smooth surface. W = 0.
3. **Force perpendicular to motion** -- carrying a bag while walking horizontally. The carrying force is upward; the motion is sideways. They are at 90° to each other, so no work is done against gravity.

> **Key idea:** Work is done only when a force moves an object in the direction of that force. No movement means no work, no matter how hard you push.

## Energy — The Ability to Do Work

**Energy is the capacity to do work.** Anything that can do work has energy. Energy is measured in the same unit as work -- the **joule (J)**.

Energy comes in many forms, but for this chapter the two key ones are kinetic energy and potential energy.

### Kinetic energy — energy of motion

A moving object has **kinetic energy**. The faster it moves and the more mass it has, the more kinetic energy it has.

**KE = ½ × m × v²**

- **m** = mass (in kg)
- **v** = velocity (in m/s)

A 2 kg ball moving at 3 m/s has KE = ½ × 2 × 3² = 9 J.

Notice the **v²** -- doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy. This is why a car at 60 km/h has four times the kinetic energy of the same car at 30 km/h, and why high-speed crashes are so much more dangerous than low-speed ones.

### Potential energy — stored energy

An object can have energy because of its **position** or **state**. A book held high above the floor has stored energy -- if released, it will fall and gain kinetic energy. This stored energy is called **potential energy**.

The most common form in school physics is **gravitational potential energy**:

**PE = m × g × h**

- **m** = mass (kg)
- **g** = acceleration due to gravity (≈ 9.8 m/s², often rounded to 10 m/s²)
- **h** = height above the reference point (m)

A 5 kg textbook on a shelf 2 m high has PE = 5 × 10 × 2 = 100 J.

Other forms of potential energy include:
- **Elastic PE** in a stretched bow or a compressed spring
- **Chemical PE** in food, fuel, batteries
- **Nuclear PE** in atomic nuclei

> **Key idea:** **Kinetic energy** depends on mass and velocity (½mv²). **Potential energy** depends on position or state (mgh for height). Both are measured in joules.

## The Law of Conservation of Energy

**Energy can be transformed from one form to another, but it cannot be created or destroyed. The total energy in a closed system remains constant.**

This is one of the deepest laws in all of physics. Energy moves around, changes form, but the total never changes.

### Energy transformations you see every day

| Activity | Energy transformation |
|---|---|
| **Diwali sparkler burns** | Chemical PE → light + heat + sound |
| **Ceiling fan running** | Electrical energy → kinetic energy of blades + heat |
| **Solar cooker heats food** | Light (solar) energy → heat |
| **Bow shoots an arrow** | Elastic PE → kinetic energy of arrow |
| **A ball falls** | Gravitational PE → kinetic energy |
| **You ride a bicycle** | Chemical PE (food) → kinetic energy of cycle + heat (friction) |
| **Hydroelectric dam** | Gravitational PE of water → kinetic energy → electrical energy |

### A bouncing ball — a beautiful example

Hold a ball at height **h**. It has PE = mgh and KE = 0.

Let it fall. As it falls:
- PE decreases (height is decreasing)
- KE increases (speed is increasing)
- **PE + KE stays the same** (until it hits the ground)

At the moment it hits the ground, all the PE has become KE. The ball is moving fastest at this point.

After bouncing, KE goes back into PE as the ball rises. In an ideal world the ball would bounce back to exactly the same height. In reality, some energy is "lost" -- but not really lost, it has been converted to **heat and sound** through friction and impact. The total energy is still conserved.

> **Key idea:** Energy never disappears. When it seems to disappear, it has been transformed into heat, sound, or some other form. Total energy is always conserved.

## Power — The Rate of Doing Work

**Power is how fast work is done (or how fast energy is transferred).**

**Power = Work done ÷ Time taken**

**P = W / t**

The SI unit of power is the **watt (W)**. **1 watt = 1 joule per second**.

If two people climb the same flight of stairs (same work done, mgh), the one who climbs faster has more **power**. They did the same work but in less time.

### Common power values

| Activity | Approximate power |
|---|---|
| Resting human body | 100 W |
| Walking | 250 W |
| Cyclist (steady pace) | 200 W |
| Sprinting athlete | 1500 W (briefly) |
| LED bulb | 9 W |
| Ceiling fan | 60 W |
| Refrigerator | 200 W |
| Hair dryer | 1500 W |
| Air conditioner | 1500 W |
| Maruti Suzuki Alto engine | 35 kW (35,000 W) |
| Mumbai-Pune railway engine | 4500 kW |

### Kilowatt and unit of electrical energy

A **kilowatt (kW)** is 1000 watts. The "unit" on your electricity bill is the **kilowatt-hour (kWh)** -- the energy used by a 1 kW appliance running for 1 hour.

**1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ joules** = 3.6 million joules.

A 100 W bulb running for 10 hours uses 100 × 10 = 1000 Wh = 1 kWh = 1 unit.

> **Key idea:** **Power** = work done per unit time. More power means doing the same work in less time. Unit: watt (W).

## Simple Machines — Multiplying Force

A **simple machine** is a device that changes the magnitude or direction of a force, making a task easier to do (though not necessarily reducing total work).

There are six classical simple machines, and almost every complex machine is built from them.

### 1. Lever

A rigid bar that pivots on a point called the **fulcrum**. Examples: see-saw, scissors, crowbar, claw of a hammer, your forearm.

A small force applied far from the fulcrum can lift a large load close to the fulcrum. The trade-off: you have to move your end through a longer distance.

### 2. Inclined plane (ramp)

A sloped surface that lets you raise a load gradually rather than lifting it straight up. The total work to raise the load is the same, but you spread it over a longer path -- so the force you need at any moment is smaller.

Examples: any ramp at a building entrance, road on a hill, the slope going up to a railway platform.

### 3. Wheel and axle

A wheel attached to a smaller axle. Force applied to the wheel turns the axle (with a multiplied force), or force on the axle turns the wheel (with a multiplied distance and speed).

Examples: door handles, steering wheels, screwdrivers, water taps, bicycle pedals.

### 4. Pulley

A wheel with a groove around which a rope can pass. A single pulley changes the direction of force. A system of multiple pulleys (a **block and tackle**) reduces the force needed but increases the rope length you pull.

Examples: well buckets, flag poles, construction site hoists, gym weight machines.

### 5. Wedge

Two inclined planes back to back. Pushed in one direction, it splits objects apart.

Examples: axe head, knife blade, chisel, your front teeth.

### 6. Screw

An inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder. Each rotation moves the screw forward by a small amount.

Examples: every screw and bolt, jar lids, water tap valves, vice grips.

### Mechanical advantage

The ratio of load to effort is called the **mechanical advantage**. A simple machine that lets you lift a 500 N load with 100 N of effort has a mechanical advantage of 5.

Important: Simple machines reduce the FORCE you need but never reduce the WORK. In an ideal frictionless world, work in = work out. In reality, friction always wastes some energy as heat.

## Summary

- **Work** = Force × Displacement in the direction of force. Unit: **joule (J)**.
- No work is done when there is no movement, no force, or force is perpendicular to motion.
- **Energy** = capacity to do work. Same unit as work, joule.
- **Kinetic energy** (motion): **KE = ½ × m × v²**.
- **Potential energy** (position/state): **PE = m × g × h** for gravitational PE.
- **Conservation of energy**: Energy can change form but is never created or destroyed.
- **Power** = work done per unit time = energy transferred per unit time. Unit: **watt (W)**.
- **1 kWh** (electricity bill unit) = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.
- **Six simple machines**: lever, inclined plane, wheel and axle, pulley, wedge, screw.
- Simple machines reduce the force needed but cannot reduce the total work required.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Indian hill-station roads** are long zig-zags rather than direct straight climbs. Why? An inclined plane (the road) reduces the force needed for vehicles, even though the total work to gain the same altitude is unchanged. The road is longer but the gradient is gentler — exactly the principle of the inclined plane scaled up to mountains.

**Hydroelectric stations in Tehri, Bhakra, and Sardar Sarovar** convert the gravitational potential energy of stored water into kinetic energy as it falls through turbines, then into electrical energy. Conservation of energy at colossal scale — the chapter's equations describe every megawatt produced.

**Electricity bill at home**. The kWh on your monthly bill is a real-world unit of energy. A 60 W ceiling fan running for 8 hours per day for 30 days uses 60 × 8 × 30 = 14,400 Wh = 14.4 kWh = 14.4 units of electricity. At ₹7 per unit, that is ~₹100 per fan per month. The same chapter equations let you calculate your home's monthly bill.

### Try it at home — Inclined plane in your kitchen

**What you'll need:** A wooden plank or sturdy book (about 60-90 cm long), a heavy book to lift, a kitchen scale, a small rope or string.

**Steps:**
1. Tie the string around the heavy book.
2. Use the kitchen scale (pulling the string) to lift the book straight up by 30 cm. Read the force in newtons (1 kg ≈ 10 N).
3. Now prop the plank at an angle so its top is 30 cm above the table.
4. Pull the same book UP the inclined plank to the same height. Read the force again.

**What to look for:**
- Pulling straight up requires the full weight (≈ 30 N for a 3 kg book).
- Pulling up the inclined plank requires less force (perhaps 15-20 N, depending on the angle).
- The book ends up at the same height — same potential energy gained.
- BUT you pulled it over a longer distance, so the total work (force × distance) is roughly the same (slightly more in reality, because of friction).

You have just verified that an inclined plane reduces the force at the cost of increased distance. The same principle as a road climbing a hill.
