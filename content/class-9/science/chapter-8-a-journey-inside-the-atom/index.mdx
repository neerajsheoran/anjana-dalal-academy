## What Is Everything Made Of?

Look around you. The walls of your room, the air you are breathing, the water in your glass, the leaves on a tree, your own body -- everything is made of **matter**. And all matter, no matter how varied it looks, is built from extremely tiny building blocks called **atoms**.

A single grain of sand contains over 30 quintillion atoms (that is 30 followed by 18 zeros). A single drop of water contains about 1.5 sextillion molecules of H₂O. Atoms are unimaginably small -- so small that until just over a century ago, no one had ever seen one.

In this chapter, we will trace the remarkable story of how scientists figured out what atoms are made of, and we will meet the three particles that build every atom: the **electron**, the **proton**, and the **neutron**.

---

## A Short History of Atomic Theory

The idea of atoms is over 2500 years old.

### Ancient ideas — India and Greece

Around 600 BCE, the Indian philosopher **Kanada** proposed that all matter is made of tiny indivisible particles which he called **paramanu** ("smallest particle"). At nearly the same time in Greece, **Democritus** and **Leucippus** suggested that matter was made of indivisible bits, which they named **atomos** -- "uncuttable" in Greek. Both ideas were brilliant guesses, but neither could be tested at the time.

For 2000 years, these ideas remained more philosophy than science.

### Dalton's atomic theory (1808)

The English chemist **John Dalton** revived the atomic idea with a scientific foundation. His main points:

1. All matter is made of tiny indivisible particles called atoms.
2. Atoms of an element are all identical in mass and properties.
3. Atoms of different elements differ.
4. Compounds are formed when atoms of different elements combine in fixed whole-number ratios.
5. Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in chemical reactions.

Most of Dalton's theory is still considered correct today -- only the idea that atoms are indivisible was eventually overturned.

### J.J. Thomson and the discovery of the electron (1897)

While studying gas discharge in vacuum tubes, the British scientist **J.J. Thomson** discovered tiny negatively charged particles that came from atoms. These were the first **subatomic particles** ever discovered. He called them **electrons**.

Thomson proposed his model of the atom: a sphere of positive charge with negatively charged electrons embedded in it like raisins in a pudding. This became known as the **plum pudding model**.

### Rutherford and the discovery of the nucleus (1911)

**Ernest Rutherford** decided to test Thomson's model with a famous experiment. He fired positively charged alpha particles at a thin sheet of gold foil. According to Thomson's pudding model, the alpha particles should have passed straight through with only minor deflections.

What Rutherford observed astonished him:
- Most alpha particles did pass straight through.
- Some were deflected by small angles.
- **A few bounced almost straight back**, as if hitting something solid.

Rutherford said it was "as if you fired a 15-inch artillery shell at a piece of tissue paper and it bounced back at you".

His conclusions:
- The atom is mostly **empty space** (most particles passed through).
- All the positive charge and almost all the mass is concentrated in a tiny **nucleus** at the centre.
- The negatively charged electrons orbit around this nucleus.

This was a revolutionary idea: the atom has a tiny, dense, positive **nucleus** at its centre, with **electrons** orbiting around in nearly empty space.

### Bohr's model (1913)

The Danish physicist **Niels Bohr** refined Rutherford's model. He proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus only in specific allowed paths called **shells** or **energy levels**. Electrons stay in these shells without losing energy. They jump from one shell to another only when they gain or lose specific amounts of energy.

This explains why atoms emit specific colours of light (line spectra), and why all atoms of an element behave identically.

### The modern picture

Today we know the atom is even stranger than Bohr imagined. Electrons do not move in neat circles -- they exist as fuzzy "clouds" of probability called **orbitals**. The nucleus contains protons AND **neutrons** (discovered by James Chadwick in 1932). And both protons and neutrons themselves are made of even smaller particles called quarks.

For Class 9 chemistry, the Bohr model with electrons in shells is enough.

## The Three Subatomic Particles

Every atom is built from three kinds of particles:

| Particle | Charge | Mass (relative) | Location |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Proton** | +1 | 1 unit | Nucleus |
| **Neutron** | 0 (neutral) | 1 unit | Nucleus |
| **Electron** | -1 | ~1/1836 unit | Shells around the nucleus |

- **Protons** are positively charged. The number of protons defines what element the atom is. Hydrogen always has 1 proton; carbon always has 6.
- **Neutrons** have no charge. They sit in the nucleus with protons. Different numbers of neutrons can exist in the same element (these are called isotopes).
- **Electrons** are tiny, negatively charged particles in orbit around the nucleus. They are about 2000 times less massive than protons.

In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons, so the charges balance and the overall atom has no net electric charge.

> **Key idea:** Atoms have a tiny positive nucleus (protons + neutrons) surrounded by negative electrons. The number of protons determines what element it is.

## Atomic Number, Mass Number, and Symbols

Every element has a unique **atomic number (Z)** = the number of protons in its nucleus.

- Hydrogen: Z = 1
- Helium: Z = 2
- Carbon: Z = 6
- Oxygen: Z = 8
- Sodium: Z = 11
- Iron: Z = 26
- Silver: Z = 47
- Gold: Z = 79

The **mass number (A)** = number of protons + number of neutrons.

The number of neutrons = A − Z.

### Element symbols

Each element has a one or two-letter symbol. Some come straight from the English name (H for hydrogen, O for oxygen). Some come from Latin names:
- **Au** for gold (from *aurum*)
- **Ag** for silver (from *argentum*)
- **Fe** for iron (from *ferrum*)
- **Na** for sodium (from *natrium*)
- **K** for potassium (from *kalium*)
- **Pb** for lead (from *plumbum*)

The atomic number is written as a subscript before the symbol, and the mass number as a superscript:

**₆C¹²** means carbon with atomic number 6 and mass number 12 (so 6 protons + 6 neutrons + 6 electrons).

## How Electrons Are Arranged — Shells

Electrons orbit the nucleus in **shells** at increasing distances. The maximum number of electrons in each shell follows a rule: **2n²**, where n is the shell number.

- Shell 1 (K shell, n=1): up to 2 × 1² = **2 electrons**
- Shell 2 (L shell, n=2): up to 2 × 2² = **8 electrons**
- Shell 3 (M shell, n=3): up to 2 × 3² = **18 electrons**
- Shell 4 (N shell, n=4): up to 2 × 4² = **32 electrons**

The outermost shell can hold a maximum of 8 electrons regardless of the 2n² rule (except for the first shell, which holds at most 2).

### Examples

| Element | Z | Electron arrangement |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen (H) | 1 | 1 |
| Helium (He) | 2 | 2 |
| Lithium (Li) | 3 | 2, 1 |
| Carbon (C) | 6 | 2, 4 |
| Oxygen (O) | 8 | 2, 6 |
| Sodium (Na) | 11 | 2, 8, 1 |
| Magnesium (Mg) | 12 | 2, 8, 2 |
| Chlorine (Cl) | 17 | 2, 8, 7 |
| Argon (Ar) | 18 | 2, 8, 8 |
| Calcium (Ca) | 20 | 2, 8, 8, 2 |

> **Key idea:** Electrons fill shells from the innermost outward. Shells hold a maximum of 2n² electrons, except the outermost which caps at 8.

## Valency — Why Atoms Combine

Atoms tend to be most stable when their outermost shell has **8 electrons** (or 2, in the case of helium). This is called the **octet rule**.

The number of electrons an atom **needs to gain, lose, or share** to achieve a stable outer shell is called its **valency**.

### Examples

- **Sodium (Na)** has 1 electron in its outer shell. To become stable, it loses that 1 electron → valency = 1.
- **Magnesium (Mg)** has 2 electrons in its outer shell. It loses 2 → valency = 2.
- **Aluminium (Al)** has 3 outer electrons. It loses 3 → valency = 3.
- **Carbon (C)** has 4 outer electrons. It shares 4 (with other atoms) → valency = 4.
- **Oxygen (O)** has 6 outer electrons. It needs 2 more for an octet → valency = 2.
- **Chlorine (Cl)** has 7 outer electrons. It needs 1 more → valency = 1.

The **noble gases** (helium, neon, argon, etc.) already have full outer shells. They do not need to gain, lose, or share electrons. Their valency is **0**. This is why they are so chemically unreactive.

> **Key idea:** Valency = the number of electrons an atom gains, loses, or shares to complete its outer shell. It determines how the atom bonds with others.

## Isotopes

Atoms of the same element can have **different numbers of neutrons** -- and so different mass numbers. Such atoms are called **isotopes**.

### Examples

- **Hydrogen** has three isotopes:
  - Protium (¹H): 1 proton, 0 neutrons (the common one)
  - Deuterium (²H or D): 1 proton, 1 neutron
  - Tritium (³H or T): 1 proton, 2 neutrons (radioactive)
- **Carbon** has isotopes including ¹²C (most common), ¹³C (slightly heavier, used in chemistry research), and ¹⁴C (radioactive, used in carbon dating).

All isotopes of an element have the same number of protons (and so the same atomic number and the same chemical behaviour), but different masses.

### Uses of isotopes

- **Carbon-14 dating** -- archaeologists use the slow decay of ¹⁴C to find the age of organic fossils.
- **Cobalt-60** -- used in cancer treatment (radiotherapy).
- **Iodine-131** -- used in treating thyroid disorders.
- **Uranium-235** -- the fuel of nuclear reactors and atomic bombs.

## Summary

- All matter is made of tiny particles called **atoms**.
- Atomic theory has evolved: Dalton (indivisible spheres) → Thomson (plum pudding) → Rutherford (tiny dense nucleus) → Bohr (electrons in shells) → modern (quantum cloud model).
- An atom has a tiny **nucleus** at its centre, containing **protons** (positive) and **neutrons** (no charge). **Electrons** (negative) orbit around in shells.
- **Atomic number (Z)** = number of protons. Defines the element.
- **Mass number (A)** = protons + neutrons.
- Electrons fill shells from inside out. Maximum per shell: **2n²** (n = shell number); outermost shell holds at most **8**.
- **Valency** = number of electrons an atom needs to gain, lose, or share to complete its outer shell. Determines chemical behaviour.
- **Isotopes** = atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. Same chemical behaviour, different mass.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Nuclear medicine at Tata Memorial Hospital, Mumbai**. The hospital treats thousands of cancer patients each year using radioactive isotopes. Cobalt-60 emits gamma rays that destroy cancer cells. Iodine-131 is given to thyroid cancer patients because the thyroid gland naturally absorbs iodine. The very physics of these isotopes — same chemical behaviour as ordinary iodine, but radioactive — saves lives.

**ISRO's nuclear-powered missions**. India's Chandrayaan and Mars missions use solar panels, but planned future missions to outer planets will rely on radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs) powered by plutonium isotopes. Beyond Jupiter, sunlight is too weak; isotopes provide power for decades.

**Carbon dating Indian artefacts**. Archaeologists studying ancient sites at Lothal, Harappa, and Indus Valley use carbon-14 dating to determine how old wooden tools, bones, and charcoal are. The slow predictable decay of carbon-14 is a clock that lets us date events from thousands of years ago.

### Try it at home — Calculate electron arrangements

**What you'll need:** A pen, paper, and the periodic table (any chemistry textbook).

**Try these:**

1. **Magnesium** has atomic number 12. Write its electron arrangement.
   - Answer: 2, 8, 2 (2 in K shell, 8 in L shell, 2 in M shell)

2. **Phosphorus** has atomic number 15. Write its electron arrangement.
   - Answer: 2, 8, 5

3. What is the valency of each of these? Use the rule: valency = number of electrons in the outer shell, OR 8 minus that number, whichever is smaller.
   - Magnesium (2 outer): valency = 2
   - Phosphorus (5 outer): valency = 3 (gains 3 electrons)
   - Calcium (Z = 20, outer shell has 2): valency = 2

You have just predicted the chemical behaviour of atoms using nothing but their atomic number and the shell-filling rule. This is exactly how every chemist around the world thinks.
