## Why Living Things Reproduce

Every individual organism, no matter how strong or successful, has a finite lifespan. A mango tree may live 200 years, a sparrow 10, a butterfly a few weeks, an amoeba a few days. Without some mechanism to replace those who die, life on Earth would have ended billions of years ago.

That mechanism is **reproduction** -- the biological process by which living things produce new individuals of their own kind. Reproduction continues life from one generation to the next, even as individuals come and go.

> **Key idea:** Reproduction is how species persist over time. No individual organism is immortal, but a species can last for millions of years through continuous reproduction.

In this chapter, we explore the two great strategies of reproduction -- **asexual** and **sexual** -- and look closely at how plants and humans reproduce.

---

## Two Big Strategies — Asexual and Sexual

### Asexual reproduction

In **asexual reproduction**, a single parent produces offspring that are genetically identical (or nearly so) to itself. No mixing with another individual is involved.

Advantages:
- Fast (no need to find a partner)
- Reliable (when conditions are good, identical offspring will also do well)
- Can produce many offspring quickly

Disadvantage:
- No genetic variety; if the environment changes, all the identical offspring may suffer together.

### Sexual reproduction

In **sexual reproduction**, two parents contribute genetic material (usually via sperm and egg cells), and the offspring inherit a mix of both parents' genes.

Advantages:
- Produces variety; offspring differ from parents and from each other
- Variety helps the species adapt to changing environments
- Some traits from one parent can combine with traits from the other

Disadvantage:
- Slower (requires finding a partner, mating, gestation)
- Lower number of offspring per attempt

Most simple organisms reproduce asexually. Most complex animals and flowering plants reproduce sexually. Many organisms (like yeast, hydra, and some plants) can do both, depending on conditions.

## Methods of Asexual Reproduction

### Binary fission

Used by **amoeba** and **bacteria**. The parent cell simply divides into two roughly equal daughter cells. Each daughter is genetically identical to the parent.

Bacteria can divide every 20 minutes under good conditions -- a single bacterium can become over a billion in 10 hours.

### Budding

Used by **yeast** and **hydra**. A small bud grows from the parent's body. The bud develops, then either detaches and lives independently (yeast) or stays attached for a while (hydra).

You can see yeast budding in idli batter under a microscope -- tiny daughter cells sprout from parent cells.

### Fragmentation

Used by **Spirogyra** (a thread-like green alga in ponds). The body breaks into pieces; each piece grows into a new individual.

A pond covered in Spirogyra demonstrates fragmentation -- the algae literally tear apart in many places, and each fragment becomes a new alga.

### Regeneration

Some animals can regrow lost body parts. **Planaria** (a flatworm) can be cut into pieces, and each piece grows into a new flatworm. **Starfish** can grow back lost arms; an arm with part of the central disk can grow into a whole new starfish.

### Vegetative propagation

In plants, parts of the plant (not seeds) give rise to new plants.

- **Cuttings** -- a piece of stem from rose or chameli can grow roots and become a new plant.
- **Layering** -- branches of jasmine or guava are bent down and partly buried; they grow roots and can be separated.
- **Grafting** -- a branch of one mango variety is joined to the rootstock of another. Same trick used to combine Alphonso flavour with hardy rootstocks.
- **Tubers** -- the "eyes" on a potato can each grow into a new plant.
- **Bulbs** -- onions and garlic reproduce from underground bulbs.

Indian agriculture relies heavily on vegetative propagation. Most banana plants, sugarcane, mango varieties, and rose cultivars in India are produced by these methods rather than from seed.

## Sexual Reproduction in Plants — Flowers

The flower is the reproductive organ of a flowering plant. Many flowers contain BOTH male and female parts, but some plants have separate male and female flowers (or even separate male and female plants, like papaya).

### Parts of a flower

| Part | Function |
|---|---|
| **Petals** | Often colourful; attract pollinators (bees, butterflies, birds) |
| **Sepals** | Green leaf-like structures that protect the flower bud |
| **Stamens** | Male reproductive organs. Each consists of an **anther** (produces pollen) and a **filament** (supports anther) |
| **Pistil (carpel)** | Female reproductive organ. Has a **stigma** (sticky top, catches pollen), **style** (a narrow tube), and **ovary** (contains ovules) |
| **Ovules** | Develop into seeds after fertilisation |

### Pollination

**Pollination** is the transfer of pollen from the anther (male part) to the stigma (female part).

Two kinds:
- **Self-pollination** -- pollen from a flower lands on the same flower's stigma or another flower on the same plant.
- **Cross-pollination** -- pollen is transferred from one plant's flower to another plant's flower.

Pollinators include bees, butterflies, beetles, birds (sunbirds and hummingbirds), bats, and wind. Honeybees are the most important commercial pollinators worldwide and in India.

> **Key idea:** Pollination is the transfer of pollen from male to female parts. It precedes fertilisation; without it, no seeds form.

### Fertilisation and seed formation

After pollination, the pollen grain grows a tube down through the style to reach the ovule. The male reproductive cell (in the pollen tube) fuses with the female egg cell (in the ovule). This fusion is **fertilisation**.

After fertilisation:
- The ovule develops into a **seed**.
- The ovary develops into a **fruit**.

When you bite into a mango, the flesh is the swollen ovary wall; the inner stone is the seed.

### Germination

Under suitable conditions of water, warmth, and oxygen, a seed sprouts -- this is **germination**. The seed splits open, a root emerges first (downward), then a shoot (upward). Within days the young plant is doing photosynthesis on its own.

## Sexual Reproduction in Humans

In humans, two parents are involved. Male and female reproductive systems produce specialised cells called **gametes** (sperm in males, eggs in females). At fertilisation, a sperm meets an egg, and a new individual begins to develop.

### Female reproductive system

Main parts:

- **Ovaries** -- two small organs that produce egg cells (ova) and the hormone oestrogen. From puberty, one egg is usually released every month (ovulation).
- **Fallopian tubes (oviducts)** -- tubes connecting each ovary to the uterus. Fertilisation usually happens here.
- **Uterus (womb)** -- a muscular pear-shaped organ where a fertilised egg implants and grows into a baby over nine months.
- **Vagina** -- the passage connecting the uterus to outside the body.

### Male reproductive system

Main parts:

- **Testes** -- two oval glands that produce sperm cells and the hormone testosterone. They are housed in a sac called the **scrotum**, just outside the main body cavity (because sperm need a slightly cooler temperature than body temperature).
- **Sperm ducts** -- tubes that carry sperm from the testes.
- **Penis** -- the external organ through which sperm are delivered.

### Fertilisation and pregnancy

When a sperm reaches an egg in the fallopian tube and fuses with it, fertilisation has occurred. The fertilised egg (called a **zygote**) begins to divide rapidly as it travels to the uterus, where it implants in the uterine wall.

Over the next nine months, the zygote develops into an embryo, then a foetus, then a fully formed baby. The mother's body supplies nutrition through the **placenta** -- an organ that develops to connect the mother's blood supply to the developing baby's.

After about nine months, the uterus contracts strongly and the baby is born.

## Adolescence and Puberty

Humans go through a long period of physical and emotional change between childhood and adulthood. This period -- roughly from age 10 to 19 -- is called **adolescence**.

The biological changes specifically associated with becoming sexually mature are called **puberty**.

### Changes during puberty

- **In both girls and boys**: growth spurts in height, weight gain, body hair growth, mood changes.
- **In girls**: breasts develop, hips broaden, the menstrual cycle begins (typically between ages 10-14).
- **In boys**: voice deepens, facial and body hair grow, muscles develop, sperm production begins.

These changes are driven by hormones -- chemical messengers produced by glands. Oestrogen in girls and testosterone in boys are the main puberty hormones.

### Menstruation

In girls, after puberty, the uterus prepares itself each month for a possible pregnancy by thickening its lining. If no fertilised egg arrives, the lining is shed through the vagina along with some blood -- this is the **menstrual period**. It lasts 3-7 days and the cycle repeats roughly every 28 days.

Menstruation is a normal, healthy process. Indian society has historically attached many myths to it; we now understand it as a marker of a fully functioning reproductive system.

## Reproductive Health

Reproductive health includes not just the ability to have children, but the overall well-being of the reproductive system.

### Common health issues
- **Sexually transmitted infections (STIs)** -- HIV/AIDS, syphilis, gonorrhoea, herpes. Can be prevented by safe practices.
- **Reproductive cancers** -- cervical cancer (preventable by HPV vaccine), prostate cancer.
- **Menstrual disorders** -- irregular periods, heavy bleeding, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS).

### Why population matters

India is the world's most populous country. Sustainable population growth requires informed choices about when and how many children to have. Family planning programmes encourage couples to make informed decisions and have access to safe methods.

> **Key idea:** Reproductive health is about more than just having children — it includes preventing infections, managing reproductive cancers, and making informed life choices.

## Summary

- **Reproduction** is how species continue across generations.
- **Asexual reproduction** involves one parent; offspring are nearly identical clones.
  - Methods: binary fission (bacteria), budding (yeast, hydra), fragmentation (Spirogyra), regeneration (planaria, starfish), vegetative propagation (plants).
- **Sexual reproduction** involves two parents and produces variety in offspring.
- In **flowering plants**: pollination (transfer of pollen) → fertilisation (sperm fuses with egg) → ovule becomes seed, ovary becomes fruit.
- In **humans**: male testes produce sperm, female ovaries produce eggs. Fertilisation in the fallopian tube → embryo develops in uterus → birth after ~9 months.
- **Adolescence** is the transition between childhood and adulthood. **Puberty** is the biological maturation; driven by oestrogen (girls) and testosterone (boys).
- **Menstruation** is the monthly shedding of the uterine lining if no pregnancy occurs.
- **Reproductive health** = ability to have healthy children + freedom from STIs + ability to make informed choices.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Indian banana plantations rely on cloning, not seeds**. In Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, and Andhra Pradesh, banana plants are produced through tissue culture — taking cells from a single high-yielding mother plant and growing millions of identical young plants. Same chapter — asexual reproduction at industrial scale.

**India's agriculture seed banks**. ICAR and the National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources in Delhi store seeds from thousands of varieties of crops. Different rice cultivars, varieties of wheat from Punjab to Bihar, all stored as seeds — the product of sexual reproduction — for use in breeding programmes and to preserve diversity.

**Hospitals and family planning**. Every primary health centre in India provides family planning counselling and reproductive health services. The choices that families make now will determine population trends in the coming decades.

**National Immunisation Programme**. The HPV vaccine, given to adolescent girls, prevents the human papillomavirus that causes cervical cancer. Indian states like Sikkim and Punjab have led HPV vaccination drives, showing how reproductive health science meets public health.

### Try it at home — Watch a seed germinate (full life cycle)

**What you'll need:** A few seeds (moong, chickpea, or any fresh whole seed), a wet cotton cloth, a saucer or plate, a small pot with soil, 2-3 weeks of patience.

**Steps:**
1. Soak the seeds in water for 8-12 hours overnight.
2. Place them on the wet cloth on a plate. Cover loosely. Keep at room temperature.
3. After 1-2 days, the seeds will sprout — the white root emerges first.
4. After 3-4 days, a green shoot appears. Two seed leaves (cotyledons) unfurl.
5. Transfer to a small pot with soil, water gently, place near a window.
6. Over the next 2-3 weeks, watch the seedling grow into a young plant.

**What to look for:**
- The root grows downward (toward gravity, away from light).
- The shoot grows upward (toward light).
- The seed itself shrinks as the seedling consumes its stored food.
- Once leaves are fully open, photosynthesis kicks in.

You have just watched the result of sexual reproduction — a single seed, containing a fully formed but dormant embryonic plant — develop into a fresh young plant. The cycle of life, condensed into a few weeks in your kitchen.
