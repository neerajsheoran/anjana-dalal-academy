## Why Reproduce at All?

**Meera:** Arjun, this is a strange question to start with, but why do living things reproduce at all? Why not just keep living?

**Arjun:** Because nothing is immortal. Every organism has a finite lifespan. If a species did not reproduce, it would end the moment the current generation died.

**Meera:** So reproduction is basically how a species buys time across generations.

**Arjun:** Yes. The mango tree in your courtyard is descended from mango trees that were around before any human civilisation existed. The individual trees came and went, but the species kept going through reproduction.

**Meera:** And the chapter also says reproduction is what drives evolution. Each generation has small differences, and over millions of years those add up.

**Arjun:** Right. The huge variety of species on Earth today — tigers, frogs, banyan trees, fungi — all came from billions of years of reproduction with tiny variations. No reproduction, no evolution, no biodiversity.

**Meera:** So individuals are temporary, but species, through reproduction, can last for millions of years.

**Arjun:** That actually puts our short little 80-year lives into perspective.

## Asexual vs Sexual — Two Different Bets

**Meera:** Arjun, why do bacteria just split in two but humans need two parents? Is one method better?

**Arjun:** They are two different strategies. Each has strengths and weaknesses.

**Meera:** Tell me asexual first.

**Arjun:** Asexual is fast and reliable. A single bacterium can become a billion in about 10 hours. A cutting from jasmine grows into a new plant in weeks. The offspring are exact genetic copies of the parent. So if the parent is doing well in its environment, the offspring should do well too.

**Meera:** Sounds great. What is the catch?

**Arjun:** All the offspring are identical. So if the environment changes — a new disease, a new predator, a sudden climate shift — every single one is equally vulnerable. They can all wipe out together.

**Meera:** And sexual reproduction?

**Arjun:** Slower. You need to find a mate, mate, gestate. Fewer offspring per attempt. But each offspring inherits a unique mix of genes from both parents. Each one is genetically different.

**Meera:** So if the environment changes, the variety is the safety net. Some of the offspring might have a useful combination of traits that helps them survive.

**Arjun:** Exactly. Asexual is high speed, high copy fidelity, low variety. Sexual is slower, more careful, high variety. Neither is "better" — it depends on the environment.

**Meera:** And complex animals and plants mostly went with sexual reproduction?

**Arjun:** Because their environments have many threats. Variety is the survival advantage. Bacteria can get by on sheer speed of multiplication. We can't.

## Indian Plants and Vegetative Propagation

**Meera:** Arjun, my dadaji has a small mango orchard near Ratnagiri. He told me they never grow Alphonso from seed — only from grafting. Why?

**Arjun:** Because seeds give you genetic variety. For a wild plant, that is great. But for a farmer who wants every tree to produce the same flavour and size of fruit, variety is bad.

**Meera:** Right, a seed has mixed genes from two parents. So an Alphonso seed might give a tree whose fruit tastes nothing like Alphonso.

**Arjun:** And grafting is essentially vegetative propagation. You take a cutting from the parent Alphonso tree and attach it to a hardy rootstock. The new tree is a genetic copy. Same flavour, same fruit, same season.

**Meera:** So bananas, sugarcane, roses, mangoes — all those big Indian plantations are basically clones of each other?

**Arjun:** Pretty much. And there is a speed bonus too. A grafted mango fruits in 3 to 4 years. A seedling takes 7 to 10.

**Meera:** Predictability over variety. But that feels risky.

**Arjun:** It is. A whole plantation of identical clones is wide open to a single disease. The world's Cavendish banana variety almost got wiped out by a fungal disease called Panama disease. India's banana industry was in real danger.

**Meera:** So farmers want sameness, but nature pushes back through disease.

**Arjun:** Which is why plant scientists at ICAR and other institutes now work to keep at least some genetic diversity within commercial crops. It is a balance — not all clones, not all wild types.

## The Beauty of Pollination

**Meera:** Arjun, I was watching bees on the gulmohar tree at our school. They were going from flower to flower. They were not eating the flowers, right?

**Arjun:** No, they are collecting nectar — that sweet sugary liquid the flower makes. The flower is not being generous though. It is paying the bee for a service.

**Meera:** Which service?

**Arjun:** Transport of pollen. The bee lands, drinks nectar, gets dusted with pollen on its legs and body. Flies to the next flower, some pollen rubs off on that flower's sticky stigma. Pollination done.

**Meera:** So colour and smell of flowers are basically advertisements to attract pollinators?

**Arjun:** Exactly. Bright yellow and blue attract bees. Red and orange attract birds. White flowers that open at night attract bats. The flower's whole appearance is a marketing strategy refined over millions of years.

**Meera:** That is wild. Neither bee nor flower planned it, but they evolved into perfect partners.

**Arjun:** Co-evolution. They are now so dependent on each other that if pollinators disappear, most flowering plants cannot reproduce, and if flowers disappear, the pollinators starve.

**Meera:** And bee populations are declining worldwide, na? I read something about it in The Hindu last month.

**Arjun:** Yes. Pesticides, habitat loss, climate change. ICAR and Indian beekeepers are watching it closely. If bees collapse, large parts of Indian agriculture — fruits, oilseeds, pulses — would collapse with them.

**Meera:** So saving bees is not just sentimental. It is food security.

**Arjun:** Which is why the next time my cousin tries to smash a bee, I will stop him.

## The Human Reproductive Story

**Meera:** Arjun, the chapter compares human reproduction to a mango tree's. The contrast is striking.

**Arjun:** It really is. A mango tree might produce thousands of fruits in its lifetime, each with at least one seed. Out of maybe a million seeds, very few ever become a new mango tree. The strategy is: huge numbers of offspring, very low success per seed.

**Meera:** And humans?

**Arjun:** One or a few children at a time, often years apart. Each one requires nine months of pregnancy and years of dependent care. Very few offspring, but enormous investment in each one.

**Meera:** Both strategies work — just opposite ends of the spectrum.

**Arjun:** Exactly. And the strategy fits the species. A mango tree cannot care for its seeds, so it produces millions and trusts probability. A human family can care for a child for two decades, so it makes more sense to have fewer with more attention each.

**Meera:** Why does human pregnancy take so long?

**Arjun:** Because human babies are born remarkably underdeveloped. A baby antelope can run within hours of birth. A human baby takes 12 to 15 months just to walk.

**Meera:** That is because of our brain, right?

**Arjun:** Yes. The brain — our defining feature — takes years to mature. The nine months of pregnancy are just enough to build the basic body. The brain keeps growing for years afterwards. Which is also why human childhood is so long compared to other mammals.

**Meera:** That is a serious evolutionary trade-off. Our intelligence costs us a very long, vulnerable childhood.

**Arjun:** And it costs the parents two decades of effort per child. Quality over quantity, biologically speaking.

## Adolescence and Why It Feels Confusing

**Meera:** Arjun, can I be honest? Some days I feel completely normal and other days everything feels overwhelming. Same person, no big reason. Is something wrong with me?

**Arjun:** No, this is exactly what the chapter is talking about. Adolescence is one of the most intense periods of biological change in our entire life. Hormones are surging, our bodies are changing fast, our brains are literally rewiring for adult thinking.

**Meera:** So the ups and downs are biology, not weakness?

**Arjun:** Mostly biology. Mood swings, sudden new interests, awkwardness about how our bodies are changing, random anger or sadness — these are normal during this phase. Almost everyone our age is going through some version of it.

**Meera:** With Class 9 boards next year and all the pressure about subject choices, it sometimes feels like a lot at once.

**Arjun:** Yaar, that is real. The biology of adolescence plus the academic pressure plus deciding science or commerce — it is a heavy combination. The chapter's advice is solid though. Sleep enough, eat properly, talk to trusted adults, be kind to yourself. Most of this calms down by early twenties.

**Meera:** And menstruation? The chapter talks about it openly, but at school some boys still snigger when the topic comes up.

**Arjun:** That is exactly the problem the chapter is trying to fix. Menstruation is a normal, healthy biological process. It is a sign the reproductive system is working properly. There is nothing impure or shameful about it.

**Meera:** Some of my friends still cannot enter their kitchens or temples during their periods. Their families say it is tradition.

**Arjun:** And the science says there is no biological basis for those restrictions. Girls deserve respect, privacy when needed, and access to safe menstrual products. The government's National Health Mission is now distributing free pads in many states, and Indian schools are starting to address this properly.

**Meera:** Things are slowly changing, but for our generation it has to change faster.

**Arjun:** Which starts with us treating it as ordinary biology. When boys our age learn the science properly, the old taboos stop making sense.

**Meera:** That feels like the right note to end on.

**Arjun:** This chapter is not just exam material. It is literally our own bodies and our own future. Worth taking seriously.
