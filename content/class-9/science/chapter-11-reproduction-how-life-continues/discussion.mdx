## Why Reproduce at All?

**Student:** Sir, why do living things reproduce? Isn't being alive enough?

**Mentor:** A deep question. The short answer is that no individual is immortal. Every organism, however successful, has a finite lifespan. Without reproduction, the species would end the moment all current individuals died.

Through reproduction, a species continues across generations even as individuals come and go. The bacteria in your gut today are descended from bacteria that lived billions of years ago. The mango tree in your courtyard is descended from mango trees that grew before any human civilisation existed.

Reproduction is also the engine of evolution. Each generation has slight differences, and over millions of years those differences accumulate. Today's biodiversity — the millions of species on Earth — is the result of billions of years of reproduction with variation.

**Student:** So reproduction is what keeps life going forward through time?

**Mentor:** Exactly. Individuals are temporary; species, through reproduction, can persist for millions of years.

## Asexual vs Sexual — Two Different Bets

**Student:** Sir, why do some organisms reproduce asexually and others sexually? Is one strategy better?

**Mentor:** Each has its strengths. They are different bets on how to survive in a changing world.

ASEXUAL REPRODUCTION is fast and reliable. A single bacterium can become a billion in 10 hours. A piece of jasmine stem can grow into a new plant in weeks. The offspring are genetic copies of the parent — so if the parent thrives, the offspring should too.

The catch: if the environment changes (a new disease, a new climate, a new predator), all the identical offspring are equally vulnerable. They all die together.

SEXUAL REPRODUCTION is slower. It requires finding a mate, mating, and gestation. Fewer offspring per attempt. But the offspring inherit a MIX of both parents' genes. Each offspring is genetically unique.

The catch becomes the strength: if the environment changes, the variety of offspring means at least some are likely to have a useful new combination of traits and survive.

So asexual = high speed, high reliability, low variety; sexual = slower, more careful, high variety. Both are valid strategies; which is better depends on the environment.

**Student:** Why do most complex animals and plants use sexual reproduction?

**Mentor:** Because in complex environments with many threats — predators, diseases, climate variations — variety is a survival advantage. Sexual reproduction's variety has been a winning bet for hundreds of millions of years. Bacteria and simpler organisms can survive on speed alone; complex organisms need variety to keep up.

## Indian Plants and Vegetative Propagation

**Student:** The chapter says Indian agriculture uses vegetative propagation a lot. Why?

**Mentor:** Because it solves a real problem. When you grow a plant from seed, you get genetic variety — but for a farmer, this can be bad. A mango grown from a seed of an Alphonso tree may or may not produce the same flavour. The seed has mixed genes from two parents (Alphonso and whatever pollinated it).

Vegetative propagation gives you genetic copies. A cutting from an Alphonso mango tree, grafted onto a hardy rootstock, produces an Alphonso tree. Same flavour, same fruit size, same season. Plantations of bananas, sugarcane, mangoes, and roses across India are produced this way.

Vegetative propagation is also faster than growing from seed. A grafted mango tree can produce fruit in 3-4 years; a seedling takes 7-10 years.

**Student:** So farmers trade off variety for predictability?

**Mentor:** Yes. Variety is good for nature and evolution. Predictability is good for farming. Each has its place.

But the trade-off is dangerous if pursued too far. A plantation of identical clones is vulnerable to disease. India's banana industry once faced a near-disaster when a fungal disease threatened the Cavendish variety used everywhere. Now plant scientists work to maintain at least some genetic diversity even within commercial crops.

## The Beauty of Pollination

**Student:** Sir, I have seen bees flying around flowers. They are not eating the flowers, are they?

**Mentor:** They are collecting nectar — a sugary liquid the flowers produce specifically to attract them. The flowers do this not out of generosity, but because the bees inadvertently carry POLLEN from one flower to another.

A bee visits a flower, drinks nectar, and gets dusted with pollen on its legs and body. When the bee visits the next flower, some of that pollen rubs off onto the new flower's sticky stigma. Pollination has happened. The flowers offer nectar; the bees provide transport.

It is one of the most beautiful partnerships in nature — neither bee nor flower planned it, but over millions of years, both have evolved to benefit each other.

**Student:** So the colour and smell of flowers are advertising for bees?

**Mentor:** Essentially yes. Bright colours and sweet smells attract insects. Some flowers are coloured specifically to attract bees (yellow, blue), others birds (red, orange), others bats (white, opening at night). The flower's appearance is a marketing strategy refined over millions of years.

Without pollinators, most flowering plants could not reproduce sexually. Without flowers, those pollinators would starve. The two have co-evolved into deep dependence.

This is also why the decline of pollinators worldwide is a real concern. India's beekeepers and the Indian Council of Agricultural Research watch bee populations carefully — without bees, large parts of our agriculture would collapse.

## The Human Reproductive Story

**Student:** Sir, what makes human reproduction different from a mango tree's?

**Mentor:** The principles are similar — two parents, gametes meeting, a new individual forming. But the strategy is very different.

A mango tree produces thousands of fruits, each with at least one seed. Out of perhaps a million seeds in a tree's lifetime, only a few may become new mango trees. The strategy is many, many offspring with very low success per seed.

Humans produce one or a few children at a time, often years apart. Each child requires nine months of pregnancy and many years of dependent care. The strategy is few offspring with intensive investment per child.

Both work — just very different bets.

**Student:** Why does the human pregnancy take so long?

**Mentor:** Because a human baby is born remarkably underdeveloped compared to most mammals. Within hours of birth, a baby antelope can run. A baby human takes 12-15 months just to walk. The brain — our defining feature — takes years to mature.

The nine months of pregnancy are the time needed to build all of this. The mother provides nutrients through the placenta; the baby's organs develop in protected conditions. By birth, the basic body is ready, but the brain still has years of growing to do.

## Adolescence and Why It Feels Confusing

**Student:** Sir, growing up sometimes feels really difficult. Am I normal?

**Mentor:** Yes. Adolescence is one of the most intense periods of biological change in a human's entire life. Hormones surge through your body, your physical appearance changes rapidly, your brain rewires itself for adult thinking. None of this is gentle.

Many adolescents feel emotional ups and downs, sudden interest in topics that did not interest them before, awkwardness about their changing bodies, occasional anger or sadness with no obvious cause. All of this is normal — your body is changing in major ways, and your mind is adjusting.

What matters is to be kind to yourself, talk to trusted adults, eat well, sleep enough, and remember that this is a phase. By your early twenties, the storm calms down. The person you are becoming is the result of all this growth.

**Student:** And menstruation? Some classmates make fun of girls during periods.

**Mentor:** That is a problem in our society that science is helping to correct. Menstruation is a normal, healthy biological process — a sign that the reproductive system is working. There is nothing impure or shameful about it. Girls deserve respect, privacy when they need it, and access to safe menstrual products.

In recent years, Indian schools and government programmes have started addressing menstrual health openly. The Indian government distributes free menstrual pads in many states; the National Health Mission promotes menstrual hygiene. Slowly, the old taboos are giving way to scientific understanding.

**Student:** Thank you, sir. This chapter feels very real to me.

**Mentor:** It is real. You are at the age where this chapter is not just academic — it is your own body and your own future. Take it seriously. Be respectful of yourself and others. And remember: knowing the science is the first step to making wise choices.
