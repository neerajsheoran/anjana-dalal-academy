## What Makes Things Move?

In Chapter 4, we learned how to **describe** motion -- position, velocity, acceleration. But we never asked **why** anything moves. Why does a ball roll? Why does a car speed up? Why does a kite stay in the sky? Why does anything stop?

The answer is always the same word -- **force**. A force is a push or a pull. Every change in motion -- a start, a stop, a turn, a speedup -- is caused by some force.

> **Key idea:** A **force** is a push or a pull. Forces cause objects to start moving, stop, change direction, speed up, slow down, or change shape.

Some forces you see clearly -- a child pushing a swing, a player kicking a football. Others you do not see but feel -- gravity pulling you toward the ground, the wind pushing a kite, friction slowing a rolling ball.

---

## How Do We Measure a Force?

The SI unit of force is the **newton (N)**, named after the English scientist Sir Isaac Newton, who built the foundation of modern mechanics.

One newton is roughly the force you feel when you hold a small apple in your hand against gravity. So if your school bag weighs 5 kg, the gravitational pull on it is about 50 newtons.

Forces have **magnitude** (how strong) and **direction** (which way they push or pull) -- they are vectors. When you write a force on paper, you draw an arrow: length shows magnitude, the arrow's direction shows where the force points.

---

## Balanced and Unbalanced Forces

Many things have forces on them yet do not move. Your book lies on the table -- gravity is pulling it down, but the table is pushing it up with an equal force. The two forces **balance** each other. The net force is zero. Nothing happens.

A tug-of-war where both teams pull with equal strength -- the rope does not move. **Balanced forces** keep the object at rest (or moving uniformly, if it was already in motion).

Now imagine one team pulls harder than the other. The forces no longer balance. There is a **net force** in one direction. The rope -- and the losing team -- moves that way.

> **Key idea:** When the forces on an object are **balanced**, the object's motion does not change. When forces are **unbalanced**, there is a net force, and the object accelerates in the direction of the net force.

## Newton's First Law of Motion — Inertia

This is one of the most important sentences in all of physics:

**An object continues in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a straight line, unless acted upon by an unbalanced force.**

In plain English: things keep doing what they are doing unless something pushes or pulls them otherwise.

A book on a table stays at rest unless someone pushes it. A hockey puck on a frictionless ice rink will keep sliding in a straight line forever unless something stops it. In deep space, far from any planet or star, a spaceship with its engines off will keep coasting in the same direction, possibly for millions of years.

The natural tendency of any object to resist a change in its state of motion is called **inertia**.

### Inertia in everyday life

- When a bus suddenly starts, you lurch backward. Your body has inertia of rest and tries to stay where it is.
- When a bus suddenly stops, you lurch forward. Your body has inertia of motion and tries to keep moving.
- When you hit a ketchup bottle to get the ketchup out -- you stop the bottle suddenly, and the ketchup's inertia of motion carries it down and out.
- A coin placed on a card on top of a glass -- flick the card horizontally, and the coin (with no horizontal push) drops straight down into the glass.

Inertia depends on **mass**. The more mass an object has, the more inertia it has, the harder it is to change its motion. A pen on a table is easy to push. A car is harder. A loaded truck is harder still.

> **Key idea:** Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist a change in its motion. Mass measures the amount of inertia an object has. More mass = more inertia.

## Newton's Second Law of Motion — F = ma

If forces cause changes in motion, by how much? Newton's second law tells us.

**The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force applied to it and inversely proportional to its mass.**

In equation form:

**F = m × a**

Where:
- **F** = net force on the object (in newtons)
- **m** = mass of the object (in kilograms)
- **a** = acceleration of the object (in m/s²)

### What this equation tells you

- If you double the force on an object (and keep mass the same), the acceleration doubles.
- If you double the mass (and keep force the same), the acceleration halves.
- A small force on a small mass causes the same acceleration as a large force on a large mass, if the ratio is right.

### Worked example

You push a 50 kg cart with a force of 100 newtons. What is its acceleration?

a = F / m = 100 / 50 = **2 m/s²**

Now push a 200 kg fully loaded cart with the same 100 N force.

a = 100 / 200 = **0.5 m/s²**

Same force, four times the mass, one-fourth the acceleration. The cart hardly moves.

> **Key idea:** **F = ma**. The bigger the force, the bigger the acceleration. The bigger the mass, the smaller the acceleration for the same force.

### Defining the newton

This equation also defines what 1 newton means: **1 newton is the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s²**.

## Newton's Third Law of Motion — Action and Reaction

**For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.**

If you push a wall with 50 N of force, the wall pushes back on you with exactly 50 N of force. Always. Without exception. The reaction is the same size as the action and in the exact opposite direction.

### Examples around you

- **Walking**: Your foot pushes back on the ground. The ground pushes forward on your foot. You move forward.
- **Swimming**: You push water backward with your hands. The water pushes you forward.
- **Rocket launch**: Hot gases shoot out of the rocket downward. The reaction force pushes the rocket up. This is how every Indian PSLV launch works.
- **Recoil of a gun**: A bullet shoots forward; the gun pushes backward (recoil) on the shooter.
- **A boat moves backward when you step off** (toward a jetty). You pushed the boat back; the boat pushed you forward.

### A common confusion

If action and reaction are equal and opposite, why is the world not stuck in a perfectly balanced standstill?

Because the action and reaction act on **different objects**. The action acts on one object; the reaction acts on the other. They never cancel each other out -- they affect two different things.

When you push a wall, you exert force on the wall. The wall exerts force on you. Both feel the push, but each only feels the half meant for them.

> **Key idea:** Action and reaction are equal and opposite, but they act on **different objects** -- so they do not cancel.

## The Force of Friction

Friction is the force that opposes the relative motion of two surfaces in contact. Without friction, the world as we know it would be unliveable.

### When does friction help us?

- **Walking** -- friction between your shoes and the ground stops you from slipping.
- **Writing** -- friction between the pen tip and paper transfers ink.
- **Braking a car** -- friction in the brake pads slows the wheels.
- **Lighting a matchstick** -- friction between the match head and the rough surface ignites it.
- **Holding objects** -- friction between your fingers and the object lets you grip.

### When does friction trouble us?

- **Engine wear** -- friction between moving parts in an engine causes wear and heat.
- **Energy loss** -- friction in machines wastes energy as heat.
- **Fuel inefficiency** -- friction between car tyres and the road slows the car, requiring more fuel.

### Reducing or increasing friction

- **Reduce friction**: oil moving machine parts; use ball bearings; smoothen surfaces; design streamlined shapes (cars, aeroplanes).
- **Increase friction**: rough tyre treads for grip; sand on icy roads; rubber soles on sports shoes; rough grip on cricket bat handles.

> **Key idea:** Friction is necessary but also costly. The goal is to manage it intelligently — increase where helpful, reduce where wasteful.

## Momentum — Mass in Motion

**Momentum** combines mass and velocity:

**Momentum = mass × velocity** (symbol: p, unit: kg·m/s)

A heavy truck moving slowly can have more momentum than a fast bicycle. The momentum of a stationary object is zero.

Newton's second law can be restated more accurately as **the rate of change of momentum**. Force = change in momentum / time.

In any collision or interaction where there are no external forces, the **total momentum is conserved**. Before and after, the total adds up to the same value. This is one of the deepest laws in physics.

Example: A girl on roller skates throws a ball forward. The ball gains momentum forward; she gains equal momentum backward. She rolls back slowly. Total momentum stays zero (it was zero before she threw the ball).

---

## Summary

- A **force** is a push or a pull, measured in **newtons (N)**.
- **Balanced forces** = no net force → no change in motion. **Unbalanced forces** = net force → acceleration.
- **Newton's first law**: An object continues at rest or in uniform motion unless an unbalanced force acts on it. (Law of inertia.)
- **Inertia** = tendency to resist change in motion. More mass = more inertia.
- **Newton's second law**: **F = m × a**. Force equals mass times acceleration.
- **Newton's third law**: For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction (on a different object).
- **Friction** opposes the relative motion of two surfaces. It is useful for walking, writing, gripping; wasteful in engines.
- **Momentum** = mass × velocity. Total momentum is conserved in interactions without external forces.

## Apply What You Have Learned

### Where you see this in real life

**Why does ISRO's PSLV need so many stages?** Newton's third law explains rocket flight — hot gases shoot down, the rocket goes up. Newton's second law explains why staged rockets work — as fuel burns, the rocket's mass drops, so the same thrust produces ever-greater acceleration. By the time the final stage burns, the rocket is so light that it can reach orbital speed.

**Why airbags?** Newton's second law (in momentum form) says force = change in momentum / time. In a crash, your momentum changes from "fast" to "zero" in a fraction of a second. The shorter the time, the bigger the force on your body. An airbag stretches that time interval to several hundredths of a second instead of milliseconds — the force on you is much smaller, and injury is reduced.

**Why do trains have couplings between coaches?** When the engine accelerates, the inertia of every coach behind it resists the change. The coupling transfers the engine's force backward and allows each coach to accelerate together. The same physics shapes every Indian railway design.

### Try it at home — Inertia card trick

**What you'll need:** A drinking glass (with a wide mouth), a thin postcard or playing card, a small coin (1 or 2 rupee).

**Steps:**
1. Place the postcard flat on top of the glass.
2. Place the coin on top of the postcard, near the centre.
3. With one finger, **flick** the postcard sharply sideways. Do not push the coin — only the card.

**What to look for:**
The card flies away horizontally; the coin drops straight down into the glass.

**Why:** The card moves horizontally because of your push. But your push acts only on the card (and only for a tiny moment), not on the coin. The coin has inertia — it had no horizontal push, so by Newton's first law it stays where it is horizontally, and gravity simply pulls it straight down into the glass.

Try it slowly first (the card will drag the coin with it because friction has time to act). Then try a sharp flick. Newton's first law in your kitchen.
