## What Does "Pure" Really Mean?

Walk into any grocery shop in India and you will find bottles labelled "pure mustard oil" or packets marked "pure desi ghee." In everyday language, **pure** simply means no adulteration -- nothing unwanted has been added.

But in chemistry, the word **pure** carries a stricter meaning:

**A pure substance consists of only one kind of particle. Every particle in it shares the same chemical identity.**

Consider these examples:
- **Copper wire** is a pure substance -- every atom in it is a copper atom
- **Distilled water** is a pure substance -- every molecule in it is H2O
- **Chai** is NOT pure in the chemical sense -- it is a blend of water, tannins from tea leaves, sugar, milk proteins, and more

> **Key idea:** When a chemist says "pure," it means the substance is made of a single type of particle. Most everyday materials around us are actually mixtures of two or more pure substances combined together.

## Mixtures

A **mixture** is what you get when two or more pure substances come together without undergoing any chemical reaction. The individual substances retain their own properties and can be separated by physical means.

**Examples:** ocean water, garden soil, the atmosphere, sherbet, buttermilk

### Two Categories of Mixtures

#### 1. Homogeneous Mixtures

A **homogeneous mixture** looks the same throughout. No matter which portion you examine, the composition appears identical.

**Examples:**
- Sherbet (sugar + water + lemon juice -- looks uniform throughout)
- Brass jewellery (copper + zinc -- uniform metallic appearance)
- The atmosphere (nitrogen + oxygen + other gases -- uniform at any point)
- Vinegar (acetic acid dissolved in water)

Even though the composition is uniform, it can vary -- you can make sweeter or less sweet sherbet by adjusting the sugar.

#### 2. Heterogeneous Mixtures

A **heterogeneous mixture** is visibly uneven. You can distinguish different parts with your eyes.

**Examples:**
- Soil (sand grains, clay, pebbles, organic matter -- all visible)
- Pickle in oil (pieces of mango or lemon floating in masala oil)
- Sand stirred into water
- A bowl of fruit chaat

> **Key idea:** Homogeneous mixtures appear uniform and their components are indistinguishable to the eye. Heterogeneous mixtures show distinct regions or visible separate parts.

## Solutions

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. It is the type of mixture we encounter most frequently in daily life.

### Solvent and Solute

Every solution has two roles:
- **Solvent** -- the substance that does the dissolving (typically the larger quantity)
- **Solute** -- the substance that gets dissolved (typically the smaller quantity)

**Examples:**

| Solution | Solute | Solvent |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose drip (hospital IV) | Glucose (solid) | Water (liquid) |
| Tincture of iodine | Iodine (solid) | Alcohol (liquid) |
| Fizzy lemonade | Carbon dioxide (gas) | Water (liquid) |
| Atmosphere | Oxygen and other gases | Nitrogen (gas) |

### Characteristics of a Solution

- A solution is **homogeneous** throughout
- Solute particles are **tinier than 1 nanometre** in diameter -- invisible even under a microscope
- A solution **does not scatter a beam of light** -- a laser pointer's beam remains invisible as it passes through
- The solute **cannot be removed by filtering**
- A solution is **stable** -- particles remain dispersed indefinitely

### Alloys -- Solid Solutions

**Alloys** are solutions where the components are metals (or a metal and a non-metal), and the result is a solid. They are classified as mixtures because their composition can vary and their constituents show their own properties.

**Common alloys:**
- **Bronze** = copper + tin (used in statues and temple bells)
- **Stainless steel** = iron + chromium + carbon (used in utensils and surgical instruments)
- **Brass** = copper + zinc (used in decorative items and hardware)

## How Concentrated Is a Solution?

The **concentration** of a solution describes the proportion of solute relative to the solution.

### Categories by Concentration

- **Dilute solution** -- relatively little solute compared to solvent
- **Concentrated solution** -- a generous amount of solute relative to solvent
- **Saturated solution** -- the maximum amount of solute has dissolved at a given temperature; adding more will not dissolve
- **Unsaturated solution** -- more solute can still be dissolved

> **Key idea:** The maximum mass of solute that dissolves in a fixed quantity of solvent at a particular temperature to form a saturated solution is called the substance's **solubility**. Solubility typically rises with increasing temperature.

### Expressing Concentration Numerically

Three common formulas:

**1. Mass by mass percentage**

Concentration = (Mass of solute / Mass of solution) x 100

**2. Mass by volume percentage**

Concentration = (Mass of solute / Volume of solution) x 100

**3. Volume by volume percentage**

Concentration = (Volume of solute / Volume of solution) x 100

**Worked example:** A solution is prepared by dissolving 25 g of sugar in 225 g of water.

Mass of solution = 25 + 225 = 250 g

Mass percentage = (25 / 250) x 100 = **10%**

## Suspensions

A **suspension** is a heterogeneous mixture where small solid particles are scattered throughout a liquid but remain undissolved.

**Examples:** chalk powder shaken in water, flour mixed into water, muddy river water

### Features of a Suspension

- It is a **heterogeneous mixture**
- Particles are **large enough to be seen** with the naked eye
- Particles **scatter light** -- a beam of light passing through becomes visible
- Particles are **unstable** -- left undisturbed, they gradually settle to the bottom
- Particles can be **trapped by a filter**

> **Key idea:** A suspension is inherently unstable. Leave a glass of muddy water on a shelf, and after some time the mud sinks to the bottom.

---

## Colloidal Solutions (Colloids)

A **colloid** occupies the middle ground between a true solution and a suspension. Its particles are bigger than those in a solution but smaller than those in a suspension.

**Examples:** milk, fog, smoke, ink, blood, shaving foam, butter, gelatin dessert

### Features of a Colloid

- It is **heterogeneous** at the particle level, even though it may look uniform to the eye
- Particles **cannot be seen** without special instruments
- Particles **scatter a beam of light** -- this scattering is known as the **Tyndall effect**
- Particles are **stable** -- they do not settle out over time
- Particles **pass through filter paper** but can be separated by **centrifugation**

### The Tyndall Effect

Shine a torch through a glass of milk diluted in water and the beam becomes clearly visible. Do the same with a glass of saltwater and the beam remains invisible. The colloidal milk particles scatter the light in all directions, making the beam's path easy to see. This phenomenon is the **Tyndall effect**.

**Where you see the Tyndall effect in daily life:**
- Sunbeams slanting through a gap in the curtains in a dusty room -- dust particles in the air form a colloid and scatter the light
- Vehicle headlights creating visible cones of light on a foggy winter morning -- fog is a colloid of tiny water droplets in air

> **Key idea:** The Tyndall effect distinguishes colloids from true solutions. True solutions do not scatter light; colloids do.

### Parts of a Colloid

- **Dispersed phase** -- the substance whose particles are spread out (analogous to a solute)
- **Dispersion medium** -- the substance in which the particles are spread (analogous to a solvent)

### Varieties of Colloids

| Dispersed Phase | Dispersion Medium | Common Name | Everyday Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liquid | Gas | Aerosol | Fog, clouds |
| Solid | Gas | Aerosol | Smoke from a dhoop stick |
| Gas | Liquid | Foam | Froth on lassi |
| Liquid | Liquid | Emulsion | Milk, cold cream |
| Solid | Liquid | Sol | Ink, muddy pond (fine particles) |
| Gas | Solid | Foam | Sponge, bread |
| Liquid | Solid | Gel | Aam papad, cheese |
| Solid | Solid | Solid sol | Ruby glass, certain gemstones |

## Side-by-Side: Solution vs Suspension vs Colloid

| Property | Solution | Suspension | Colloid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Uniformity** | Homogeneous | Heterogeneous | Looks homogeneous but is heterogeneous |
| **Particle size** | Below 1 nm | Above 100 nm | Between 1 nm and 100 nm |
| **Visibility of particles** | Invisible | Visible to naked eye | Invisible to naked eye |
| **Tyndall effect** | Not observed | Observed | Observed |
| **Stability** | Completely stable | Unstable (particles settle) | Stable |
| **Filtration** | Cannot be filtered | Can be filtered | Cannot be filtered |
| **Example** | Saltwater | Chalk in water | Milk |

## Techniques for Separating Mixtures

Each separation method exploits a difference in some physical property of the components.

**1. Evaporation** -- Recovers a dissolved solid from a liquid by boiling off the liquid.
- Example: Extracting salt from seawater at salt pans along the Gujarat coast

**2. Sublimation** -- Separates a substance that sublimes from one that does not.
- Example: Recovering ammonium chloride from a mixture with common salt

**3. Filtration** -- Removes an undissolved solid from a liquid using filter paper or cloth.
- Example: Straining tea through a chhanni to remove tea leaves

**4. Centrifugation** -- Spins a mixture at high speed so that denser particles are pushed outward and settle.
- Example: Separating cream from milk at a dairy, separating blood into plasma and cells in a pathology lab

**5. Separating Funnel** -- Separates two liquids that do not mix (immiscible liquids) by draining the denser one from below.
- Example: Separating groundnut oil from water

**6. Chromatography** -- Separates dissolved substances that travel at different speeds through a medium.
- Example: Identifying artificial colours added to sweets (a common food-safety test in India), separating pigments from beetroot extract

**7. Simple Distillation** -- Separates two miscible liquids with a significant gap between their boiling points (more than 25 degrees C difference).
- Example: Separating acetone (boiling point 56 degrees C) from water (boiling point 100 degrees C)

**8. Fractional Distillation** -- Used when miscible liquids have close boiling points (less than 25 degrees C apart). A fractionating column provides extra stages of separation.
- Example: Separating the various fractions of petroleum at a refinery, obtaining pure gases from liquefied air

**9. Magnetic Separation** -- Uses a magnet to pull out magnetic particles from a non-magnetic mixture.
- Example: Removing iron filings from a heap of sand

**10. Winnowing** -- Uses a current of air to blow away lighter particles, leaving heavier ones behind.
- Example: Farmers in rural India tossing harvested grain in the breeze to separate the chaff

## Physical Changes vs Chemical Changes

### Physical Change

A transformation where **no new substance is created**. The chemical identity of the material stays the same; only its physical appearance or state alters.

**Examples:**
- Ghee solidifying in winter
- Water turning to steam
- Dissolving mishri (rock sugar) in water
- Crushing ice with a mortar

### Chemical Change

A transformation where **one or more new substances with different properties are produced**. Also called a **chemical reaction**.

**Examples:**
- Milk curdling to form dahi
- An iron gate developing rust over the monsoon
- Lighting a matchstick
- Digesting the roti you ate for dinner

> **Key idea:** Physical changes are reversible in principle and produce no new substance. Chemical changes produce new substances with properties unlike the originals.

## Pure Substances: Elements and Compounds

Pure substances fall into two groups: **elements** and **compounds**.

### Elements

An **element** is the most basic form of matter -- it **cannot be broken down further** by any chemical process.

Over 100 elements are known today. Around 92 occur naturally; the remainder have been synthesised in laboratories.

**Grouping the elements:**

#### Metals
- Exhibit a characteristic **shine** (lustre)
- Usually **silvery-white** or **golden-yellow**
- Are good **conductors** of heat and electricity
- Can be drawn into wires (**ductile**) and hammered into sheets (**malleable**)
- Produce a ringing tone when struck (**sonorous**)
- Examples: iron, aluminium, copper, gold, silver

**Exception:** Mercury is the sole metal that remains liquid at room temperature.

#### Non-metals
- Appear in a **range of colours** (yellow sulphur, colourless oxygen)
- Are **poor conductors** of heat and electricity (exceptions: graphite conducts electricity)
- Are generally **brittle** -- not malleable or ductile
- Examples: carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, sulphur, phosphorus

**Exception:** Bromine is the sole non-metal that remains liquid at room temperature.

#### Metalloids
- Exhibit properties **partway between** metals and non-metals
- Examples: silicon, germanium, boron

> **Key idea:** Of all the elements, most are solids at room temperature, eleven are gases, and exactly two are liquids -- mercury (a metal) and bromine (a non-metal).

### Compounds

A **compound** is formed when two or more elements **chemically unite in a fixed ratio by mass**.

**Examples:**
- **Water (H2O)** -- hydrogen and oxygen locked in a 1:8 mass ratio
- **Table salt (NaCl)** -- sodium and chlorine combined chemically
- **Carbon dioxide (CO2)** -- carbon and oxygen united in a 3:8 mass ratio

### What Makes Compounds Special?

- A compound's properties are **entirely different** from those of its component elements
- The ratio of elements is **fixed and unvarying**
- The components can only be separated by **chemical reactions or electrolysis**, never by physical methods alone

> **Key idea:** Mix iron filings and sulphur powder and you get a mixture -- the iron is still magnetic and the sulphur is still yellow. But heat them together and you form iron sulphide, a grey compound that is neither magnetic nor yellow. The chemical reaction erases the individual identities.

---

## Mixture vs Compound -- At a Glance

| Feature | Mixture | Compound |
|---|---|---|
| **How formed** | Physical mixing; no reaction needed | Chemical reaction between elements |
| **Composition** | Can vary freely | Always a fixed ratio |
| **Properties** | Retain the properties of each component | Entirely new properties emerge |
| **How separated** | Physical methods (filtration, distillation, etc.) | Chemical or electrochemical methods only |

## Key Terms to Remember

- **Pure substance** -- Consists of a single type of particle (element or compound)
- **Mixture** -- Two or more pure substances combined without chemical bonding
- **Solution** -- Homogeneous mixture with particles smaller than 1 nm
- **Suspension** -- Heterogeneous mixture with visible particles that settle over time
- **Colloid** -- Heterogeneous mixture with particles between 1 nm and 100 nm
- **Tyndall effect** -- Scattering of a light beam by colloidal particles
- **Solubility** -- Maximum mass of solute that dissolves in a given quantity of solvent at a specific temperature
- **Element** -- A substance that cannot be decomposed into anything simpler by chemical means
- **Compound** -- Two or more elements chemically bonded in a fixed proportion
- **Alloy** -- A homogeneous solid mixture of metals (or a metal and a non-metal)
