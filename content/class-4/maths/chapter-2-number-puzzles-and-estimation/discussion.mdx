## Seeing Objects from Different Views

**Child:** Maa, today our teacher said the same object looks different from different sides. But how is that possible?

**Mother:** Let me show you. Look at this steel glass on the table. What shape do you see from the front?

**Child:** It looks like a rectangle!

**Mother:** Now climb on the chair and look straight down into it. What do you see?

**Child:** Oh! It is a circle now!

**Mother:** Same glass, two completely different shapes — just because you changed where you were looking from. That is the magic of views!

**Child:** So when our teacher says "front view," she means what I see standing in front?

**Mother:** Yes. Front view, side view, and top view — three different angles that show three different pictures of the same object. Engineers actually use these three views to draw plans for buildings and machines!

## Views of Common Objects

**Child:** Maa, does a cricket ball also look different from different sides?

**Mother:** That is a great question! Actually, no. A cricket ball is a sphere — it looks like a circle from every single direction. Front, side, top — always a circle!

**Child:** That is the only shape that does that?

**Mother:** Any sphere does — a football, a marble, a lemon. But most other objects change their look. Think about a book. From the front, you see the cover — a big rectangle. From the side, you see just a thin line — the spine.

**Child:** And from the top, I would see another rectangle — the top edge of the pages!

**Mother:** Exactly right! You are getting good at this. Now here is a challenge — what does a sharpened pencil look like from the top, looking straight down at the eraser end?

**Child:** Hmm... a small circle?

**Mother:** Spot on!

## Describing Positions

**Child:** Maa, in class today we had to describe where things are using rows and columns. But I got confused about which is which.

**Mother:** Think of a cinema hall. The seats going from left to right — those are rows. Now imagine standing at the entrance and looking at the seats going from front to back — those are columns.

**Child:** Oh! So rows go sideways and columns go up and down?

**Mother:** Exactly. When someone says "Row 2, Column 3," they mean go to the second row from the bottom, and then count 3 spots from the left.

**Child:** Like finding a seat in a movie theatre!

**Mother:** Perfect comparison! In maths, we use the same idea with grids — a checkerboard of rows and columns. Every square on the grid has a unique address — its row number and column number.

## Using a Grid

**Child:** Maa, we played a treasure hunt game on a grid today! I had to find a hidden star.

**Mother:** How did you find it?

**Child:** The teacher said "Move 3 steps right and 2 steps up from the bottom-left corner."

**Mother:** And you reached the star?

**Child:** Yes! But my friend Rohan took a different path — he went 2 steps up first, then 3 steps right. He reached the same spot!

**Mother:** That is an important lesson — there are many different paths to the same place on a grid. But the total number of steps stays the same if you take the shortest route. Your path had 5 steps, and Rohan's also had 5 steps.

**Child:** Can we move diagonally?

**Mother:** Usually not in grid games — only up, down, left, or right. That is what makes it a fun challenge!

## Reading a Map

**Child:** Maa, our teacher showed us a map of our school today. It looked so different from how the school actually looks!

**Mother:** That is because a map shows things from above — like a bird flying over your school and looking straight down. Everything appears flat.

**Child:** The playground looked like just a big rectangle on the map. But when I stand in it, it feels so huge!

**Mother:** Maps shrink real places to fit on paper. But they show you exactly where everything is — the classrooms, the library, the gate, the canteen. Can you describe the path from our front door to the kitchen?

**Child:** Walk straight from the door, pass the living room on the left, turn right at the dining table, and the kitchen is straight ahead!

**Mother:** See? You just gave directions like a real map reader! Clear directions use words like straight, left, right, and mention landmarks along the way.

**Child:** I want to draw a map of our whole house now!

**Mother:** Go ahead! Start with the front door and add each room one by one. That will be a wonderful top-view drawing of our home.
