## What is Data?

**Child:** Maa, what does data mean? It sounds like a computer word.

**Mother:** It is used a lot with computers, but data is much simpler than that. Whenever you collect answers or numbers about something, that is data!

**Child:** Can you give me an example from home?

**Mother:** Sure! Last month I asked everyone in our family what roti filling they prefer — aloo, paneer, gobhi, or plain. Papa said aloo, you said paneer, Dadi said plain, and I said gobhi. Those four answers together are data!

**Child:** That is it? Just four answers?

**Mother:** Yes! Even four answers count as data. Of course, the more answers you collect, the more useful the data becomes. If you asked your entire class, you would have data from 30 or 40 people. Then you could say things like "most children prefer aloo paratha."

**Child:** So data helps us find out what is most popular!

**Mother:** Exactly. And what is least popular, how many people chose each option, and how different choices compare.

## Asking a Good Question

**Child:** Maa, I want to do a survey in my class. But I am not sure what to ask.

**Mother:** First, think about what you want to find out. Are you curious about food, games, TV shows, books?

**Child:** Games! I want to know what game everyone likes.

**Mother:** Good. Now, a weak question would be "Do you like games?" because everyone will just say yes. A strong question gives specific choices: "Which game do you play the most — cricket, football, kabaddi, or badminton?"

**Child:** Why do I need to give choices?

**Mother:** Because if everyone gives a different answer — "I like tag, I like lock-and-key, I like pitthu" — you will have too many categories to count. With clear choices, each answer falls into a neat group. That makes counting and comparing easy.

**Child:** What if someone's favourite is not on the list?

**Mother:** You can add "Other" as a choice. That way no one is left out, but your main categories stay clean.

## Tally Marks and Tables

**Child:** Maa, today I did my survey! I asked 35 classmates. But now I have a long list of letters: C, F, C, K, B, C, F... It is so confusing!

**Mother:** That is normal. A raw list is hard to read. Let us convert it into tally marks. Read out the responses one by one, and I will make a mark for each.

**Child:** Cricket, Football, Cricket, Kabaddi, Badminton, Cricket, Football...

**Mother:** See, now I have tallies grouped in fives. Cricket has 12 marks, Football has 9, Kabaddi has 6, and Badminton has 8.

**Child:** That is so much clearer than the list!

**Mother:** Now let us put it in a table with two columns: Sport and Number of Children. Tables are the neatest way to show data. From this table, you can instantly see that cricket is the most popular and kabaddi is the least popular.

**Child:** And the total is 12 plus 9 plus 6 plus 8 equals 35. That matches the number of classmates I asked!

**Mother:** A good check! If the total does not match the number of people you surveyed, you know something went wrong.

## Pictographs

**Child:** Maa, what is a pictograph? It sounds like drawing pictures.

**Mother:** That is almost exactly what it is! Instead of just numbers, you use little pictures to show the data. Each picture stands for a certain number.

**Child:** Like what?

**Mother:** Say your school mela sold chaat and samosas. If each little bowl picture represents 10 bowls sold, and Saturday sold 30 bowls of chaat, you would draw 3 bowl pictures for Saturday.

**Child:** And if Sunday sold 50, that would be 5 pictures! I can see the difference just by looking at how many pictures there are.

**Mother:** That is the power of pictographs. You do not even need to read the numbers — the length of the picture row tells you which is more.

**Child:** But what if the number is not a perfect multiple? Like 25 bowls?

**Mother:** Good thinking! If each picture equals 10, then 25 would be shown as 2 full pictures and a half picture. The half picture represents 5.

**Child:** This is like a visual maths chart. I love it!

## Data All Around Us

**Child:** Maa, you said data is everywhere. But where do I see it in real life?

**Mother:** Look at Papa's phone when he checks the weather. The temperature for each day, the chance of rain — all data! Look at the cricket score during an IPL match — runs, wickets, overs, strike rate. All data!

**Child:** Oh! And the report card my teacher gives — my marks in each subject!

**Mother:** Exactly! Your school collects data about every student's marks and attendance. They use it to find out which subjects need more attention, which students are improving, and how the school is doing overall.

**Child:** So even my marks are data!

**Mother:** Everything can be data if you collect it with a purpose. The key is asking the right question, recording answers carefully, and organising them so they tell a story. That is what data handling is all about.
