## Shapes in Buildings

**Child:** Maa, today our teacher showed us pictures of Hawa Mahal in Jaipur! It had so many shapes — windows, arches, everything!

**Mother:** Oh, I love Hawa Mahal! Did you notice the small windows? They are shaped like honeycombs — almost like little hexagons.

**Child:** Yes! And the Charminar has big arches that look like half-circles, and tall towers that look like cylinders!

**Mother:** Exactly! Builders have always used shapes cleverly. Even our house has shapes — the windows are rectangles, the dining table is a circle, and the roof tiles are like small rectangles.

**Child:** I never thought about it that way. Shapes really are everywhere!

## 3D Shapes — Prisms and Pyramids

**Child:** Maa, what is the difference between a prism and a pyramid? I keep getting confused.

**Mother:** Think of it this way. Pick up your lunch box — see how it has the same rectangular shape on the top and the bottom? That makes it a prism. Two matching bases connected by flat sides.

**Child:** Oh! So a Toblerone chocolate box would be a triangular prism because it has triangles on both ends?

**Mother:** Perfect example! Now think of a pyramid. Imagine a tent — it has a flat base on the ground, and the sides slope up to meet at one point on top.

**Child:** Like the pyramids in Egypt!

**Mother:** Exactly. Those have a square base and four triangular sides meeting at the tip. So it is a square pyramid.

**Child:** And all the side faces of a pyramid are always triangles?

**Mother:** Always! That is the key difference. Prisms have rectangular sides, pyramids have triangular sides.

## Faces, Edges, and Corners

**Child:** Maa, our teacher taught us Euler's formula today. F + V - E = 2. It works for every shape!

**Mother:** That is like magic, no? Let us test it. Pick up that dice — it is a cube. Count the faces.

**Child:** Six faces!

**Mother:** Corners?

**Child:** Let me count... eight corners!

**Mother:** And edges?

**Child:** Twelve edges. So 6 + 8 - 12 = 2. It works!

**Mother:** Now try it with your birthday party cap — that is a cone shape. Oh wait, Euler's formula works best with flat faces. Cones and cylinders have curved surfaces, so we need to be careful with those.

**Child:** So it works for cubes, cuboids, pyramids, and prisms — shapes with flat faces only?

**Mother:** That is right, beta. You have understood it perfectly.

## When Lines Meet — Angles

**Child:** Maa, I learned about angles today. But how do I remember which is acute and which is obtuse?

**Mother:** Here is a trick. "Acute" sounds like "a cute little angle" — small and narrow. And "obtuse" sounds big and heavy — wide and open.

**Child:** Ha! That is funny but it works! So the tip of a samosa is an acute angle?

**Mother:** Yes! And when I open the front door wide against the wall, the angle between the door and the wall is obtuse.

**Child:** And the corner of my notebook is a right angle — exactly 90 degrees!

**Mother:** You can even make a right-angle checker at home. Fold a paper in half, then fold it in half again. That folded corner is a perfect right angle. Hold it against any corner to check!

**Child:** I am going to check every corner in the house now!

## Circles — Centre, Radius, and Diameter

**Child:** Maa, my teacher said the diameter is always double the radius. Why?

**Mother:** Let me show you. Take this round roti and fold it exactly in half. See the fold line? That goes from one side, through the centre, to the other side. That is the diameter.

**Child:** And the radius would be from the centre to the edge — just half of that fold line?

**Mother:** Exactly! The diameter passes through the centre and touches both sides. The radius only goes from the centre to one side. So naturally, the diameter is two radii put together.

**Child:** So if my bangle has a radius of 3 cm, its diameter is 6 cm!

**Mother:** Perfect! And here is something fun — all the bangles on your wrist are circles, the rim of your steel glass is a circle, even the base of a diya is a circle. Circles are the most common shape in nature!

**Child:** I want to draw circles with a compass now!

**Mother:** The gap you set on the compass is the radius. The bigger the gap, the bigger your circle!
