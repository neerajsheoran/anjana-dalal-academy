## Spinning Objects

**Child:** Maa, I was spinning a coin on the table and it made a funny wobbling sound before falling. Why does that happen?

**Mother:** Great observation, beta! When you first flick the coin, it spins fast and stays upright because it is balanced. But as it loses energy and slows down, it starts tilting to one side. That tilting is the wobble you see. The coin keeps touching the table at an angle, making that buzzing sound. Finally, it runs out of energy completely and falls flat.

**Child:** Can everything spin like a coin?

**Mother:** Not everything. Objects spin well only when their shape is balanced -- round or symmetrical. A coin, a bangle, a lattu -- these spin beautifully. But try spinning your eraser or an oddly shaped stone. They will just tumble and fall because their weight is not evenly spread.

**Child:** I made a spinner in school today with cardboard and a toothpick! But my friend's spinner was better than mine.

**Mother:** Let me guess -- did you poke the toothpick exactly through the centre of the cardboard?

**Child:** Umm... I think it was a little to the side.

**Mother:** That is the secret! The stick must go through the exact centre. If it is off-centre, the spinner wobbles because one side is heavier than the other. Try again with the hole in the middle -- you will see a huge difference. And here is something fun -- if you spin a square piece of cardboard really fast, it looks like a circle!

**Child:** Really? I want to try that right now!

## Floating and Sinking

**Child:** Maa, today I was playing with things in a bucket of water. My rubber ball floated but my steel spoon sank. Is it because the ball is lighter?

**Mother:** That is part of the answer, beta, but not the whole story. Let me ask you something -- have you ever seen a ship?

**Child:** Yes! Ships are huge and made of steel, right?

**Mother:** Exactly. A ship is made of heavy steel and weighs thousands of tonnes. But it floats! A tiny steel spoon weighs almost nothing compared to a ship, but it sinks. So weight alone cannot explain floating and sinking.

**Child:** Then what decides it?

**Mother:** The **shape** matters a lot. A ship is built with a wide, hollow shape -- like a giant bowl. There is a lot of air trapped inside that hollow space. That air helps the ship stay on top of the water. Your steel spoon is solid and compact with no air inside, so it sinks.

**Child:** So if I change the shape of something, it might float or sink differently?

**Mother:** Let us try it! Take a piece of aluminium foil from the kitchen. First, spread it flat and place it on the water.

**Child:** It is floating!

**Mother:** Now fold it into a small boat shape.

**Child:** Still floating!

**Mother:** Now crush it tightly into a ball and put it in the water.

**Child:** Oh! It sank! But it is the same piece of foil!

**Mother:** Exactly! When it was flat or boat-shaped, it trapped air and covered a big area of water. But as a tight ball, all the air was squeezed out and it became small and dense. That is the magic of shape. And here is a fun fact -- take a lemon and put it in water. It floats. Now peel off the skin and try again.

**Child:** It will sink? Why?

**Mother:** The lemon peel has tiny invisible air pockets that act like a life jacket. Without the peel, those air pockets are gone, and the lemon sinks. Nature is full of amazing surprises!

**Child:** I love these experiments, Maa! Can we try more after dinner?

**Mother:** Of course! Science is all around us -- we just need curious eyes to see it.
