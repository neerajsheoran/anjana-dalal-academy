## The Sun and Shadows

**Child:** Maa, I noticed something strange today. When I walked to school in the morning, my shadow was really long and stretched out in front of me. But during the lunch break, it was tiny -- almost under my feet! Why did it change so much?

**Mother:** What a sharp observation, beta! It all depends on where the Sun is in the sky. In the morning, the Sun is low near the horizon in the east. When light comes from a low angle, it is like shining a torch sideways at something -- the shadow stretches out very long. But at noon, the Sun climbs high up, almost directly above your head. Now the light comes straight down, so your shadow shrinks to a tiny patch right under you.

**Child:** So what would happen in the evening?

**Mother:** In the evening, the Sun goes low again, but this time in the west. So your shadow would become long once more, but it would point in the opposite direction -- towards the east! The shadow always falls on the side away from the Sun.

**Child:** That is so cool! Can I play with shadows at home?

**Mother:** Absolutely! Take a torch and a pencil into a dark room. Hold the pencil close to the torch and look at the wall -- you will see a big shadow. Now slowly move the pencil away from the torch. The shadow gets smaller! And if you move the torch to different positions, the shadow shifts direction. This is exactly how shadow puppetry works. Artists in India have been telling stories using shadow puppets for hundreds of years!

**Child:** Maa, you also told me once about some old clocks that used shadows?

**Mother:** You are thinking of the **sundial**! Long ago, before watches and clocks existed, clever people noticed that shadows move in a predictable way as the Sun crosses the sky. They stuck a pole in the ground and marked where the shadow fell at different times. That became a clock! Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh of Jaipur built the famous Jantar Mantar with a huge sundial called the Samrat Yantra. It is so accurate that it can tell the time just by reading the Sun's shadow.

**Child:** I want to visit Jantar Mantar someday, Maa!

## The Moon and Stars

**Child:** Maa, last night the Moon looked like a big round roti. But a few days ago, it was just a thin curve, like someone had cut most of it away. Does the Moon change its shape?

**Mother:** It sure looks that way! But the Moon is always round, beta. What changes is how much of it we can see lit up by the Sun. Remember, the Moon does not glow on its own -- it shines because it reflects sunlight, like a mirror bouncing a torch beam.

**Child:** Then why do we see different amounts?

**Mother:** Because the Moon moves around the Earth. Sometimes the Sun lights up the entire face of the Moon that we can see -- that is called **Poornima**, the Full Moon, when it looks like a glowing roti! Other times, the Sun lights up only a thin sliver -- that is the crescent Moon. And on **Amavasya**, the New Moon night, the lit side faces completely away from us, so we see no Moon at all.

**Child:** So Diwali happens on Amavasya! No wonder we light so many diyas -- because the sky is at its darkest!

**Mother:** Exactly right! And Rakshabandhan falls on Poornima, when the Moon is at its brightest. Our festivals are deeply connected to the sky. Even Eid begins when people spot the first thin crescent Moon after a New Moon. And Chhath Puja is all about offering prayers to the Sun.

**Child:** What about the stars, Maa? Why do they twinkle?

**Mother:** Stars look like they twinkle because their light travels through layers of moving air in our atmosphere. The air bends the light this way and that, making the star seem to flicker. But actually, stars are enormous balls of fire, just like our Sun! They look tiny because they are unimaginably far away.

**Child:** Can you see any patterns in the stars?

**Mother:** Yes! On a clear night, look for a group of seven bright stars arranged in the shape of a large ladle or serving spoon. That pattern is called **Saptarshi** -- the Seven Sages. Once you spot it, you will recognise it every night!

**Child:** Maa, I also want to ask about Chandrayaan. Did India really land on the Moon?

**Mother:** Yes, and it is one of India's proudest moments! On 23 August 2023, the Vikram Lander from the Chandrayaan-3 mission gently touched down on the Moon's surface. It even carried a little rover that rolled out and studied the Moon's soil. India became only the fourth country to land on the Moon and the very first to land near the Moon's south pole, where no one had gone before!

**Child:** Wow, Indian scientists are amazing! Maybe one day I will help send a rocket to Mars!

**Mother:** With curiosity like yours, beta, anything is possible!

## Sky-Based Festivals and Chandrayaan

**Child:** Maa, my friend told me that in olden times, people did not have calendars. How did they keep track of days and months?

**Mother:** They looked up at the sky! Ancient people watched the Moon very carefully. They noticed that it takes about one month for the Moon to go from Full Moon to New Moon and back to Full Moon again. So they used this cycle to count months. Each new Full Moon or New Moon marked the start of a new month. That is how the earliest calendars were created.

**Child:** So the Moon was like a natural calendar in the sky!

**Mother:** Exactly! And because festivals were tied to these Moon phases, they naturally fall on specific Moon nights even today. Diwali always comes on the darkest night -- Amavasya. On that night, with no moonlight at all, families light hundreds of diyas and candles to chase away the darkness. It is like saying "even when the sky gives us no light, we will create our own!"

**Child:** That is beautiful, Maa. What about Chhath Puja? That is about the Sun, not the Moon, right?

**Mother:** Correct! During Chhath Puja, devotees wake up before dawn and stand in rivers or ponds. They offer prayers and prasad to the rising Sun and then again to the setting Sun. It is their way of thanking the Sun for giving us light, warmth and the energy that grows our food. The Sun is truly the source of all life on Earth.

**Child:** And Karva Chauth? Maa, you fast on that day and wait for the Moon!

**Mother:** Yes! On Karva Chauth, I do not eat or drink anything the whole day. At night, I look through a sieve at the Moon and then at your Papa's face, and only then do I break my fast. The Moon connects us to so many traditions and celebrations.

**Child:** The sky is not just beautiful -- it is like a storybook full of science, festivals and history!

**Mother:** That is the most wonderful way to think about it, beta. Every time you look up at the sky, remember -- you are looking at the same Sun, Moon and stars that inspired our ancestors to create calendars, plan festivals and even dream of reaching the Moon. And thanks to ISRO and Chandrayaan-3, that dream came true!
