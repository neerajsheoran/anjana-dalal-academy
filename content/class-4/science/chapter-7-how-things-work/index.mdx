## Exploring the World Around Us

Look at the objects near you right now -- a pencil, a water bottle, your school bag, maybe a fan spinning overhead. Have you ever stopped to wonder **why** things behave the way they do? Why does a cricket ball fly through the air but a stone drops straight into a pond? Why does a top keep twirling for so long?

In this chapter, we will roll up our sleeves and explore two fascinating ideas -- **spinning** and **floating and sinking**.

---

## Spinning Objects

Place a one-rupee coin on a smooth table and give it a flick with your fingers. Watch it carefully -- it twirls quickly at first, then starts wobbling side to side, and finally collapses flat with a soft ringing sound.

**Does every object spin equally well?** Not at all! Whether something spins well depends on its **shape** and how evenly its weight is spread out.

| Object | Does It Spin? | What Happens? |
|---|---|---|
| **One-rupee coin** | Yes | Twirls smoothly, wobbles as it slows, then falls flat |
| **Glass bangle** | Yes | Spins and produces a clinking sound before stopping |
| **Wooden top (lattu)** | Yes | Spins steadily for a long time on its pointed tip |
| **Rubber eraser** | Barely | Too rectangular and unbalanced to spin properly |
| **Irregular stone** | Barely | Rough, uneven shape prevents smooth spinning |

## Making Your Own Spinner

You can build a spinner using just a **piece of cardboard** and a **sharpened pencil or toothpick**:

1. Cut out a circle from stiff cardboard.
2. Find the exact **centre** and push a toothpick through it.
3. Hold the toothpick between your palms and twist -- watch it whirl!

**What you will discover:**

- The spinner works best when the stick goes through the **exact centre**. If the hole is off-centre, it wobbles and falls quickly.
- Cut a **square** piece of cardboard instead of a circle. Spin it fast -- it looks **perfectly round** while spinning!
- Colour your spinner with different colours -- red, blue, yellow. When it spins fast, the colours **mix together** and you see new shades.

> **Did you know?** The **charkha** (spinning wheel) works on the same idea. A large wheel is turned by hand, which twists raw cotton fibre into thread. **Mahatma Gandhi** encouraged Indians to spin their own cloth on the charkha as a symbol of self-reliance.

![A spinning top (lattu) and a charkha (spinning wheel) as examples of spinning objects](spinning-top-and-charkha.png)

> **Did you know?** The **lattu** (spinning top) is one of India's oldest toys. Thousands of years ago, children played with tops made from baked clay. Over time, wooden tops with metal tips became popular across the country.

## Things That Spin Around Us

Spinning is everywhere once you start noticing:

- **Ceiling fan** -- its blades rotate to push air downward and cool the room
- **Potter's wheel** -- the potter spins a flat disc to shape wet clay into beautiful pots
- **Bicycle wheel** -- spins when you pedal, moving the cycle forward
- **Washing machine drum** -- rotates at high speed to clean your clothes
- **Mixer grinder** -- the blades inside spin fast to grind chutneys and masalas

## Floating and Sinking

Have you ever folded a paper boat and set it sailing in a puddle after the rain? It floats beautifully! But if you throw a marble into the same puddle, it sinks straight to the bottom. Why?

### What Floats and What Sinks?

| Object | Float or Sink? | Why? |
|---|---|---|
| **Dry leaf** | Floats | Very light and spreads flat on the water |
| **Iron nail** | Sinks | Dense, heavy metal packed into a small size |
| **Empty steel katori (bowl)** | Floats | Bowl shape traps air inside and spreads over the water |
| **Pebble** | Sinks | Heavy and compact |
| **Sealed empty plastic bottle** | Floats | Light plastic with air trapped inside |
| **Same bottle filled with water** | Sinks | Now heavy with no extra air inside |

---

## Is It Only About Weight?

You might think the rule is simple -- light things float and heavy things sink. But wait!

Think about this:
- A **giant steel ship** weighing thousands of tonnes floats on the ocean.
- A **tiny iron nail** weighing just a few grams sinks in a glass of water.

So weight alone does not decide whether something floats or sinks. The **shape** of the object plays a very important role too!

---

## The Role of Shape

The **shape** of an object can change whether it floats or sinks, even if the material stays the same.

**Try this at home with aluminium foil:**

| Shape of Foil | What Happens? |
|---|---|
| **Spread out flat like a chapati** | Floats |
| **Folded into a little boat shape** | Floats |
| **Crushed tightly into a ball** | Sinks |

When the foil is flat or boat-shaped, it covers a large area of water and traps **air** underneath or inside. This helps it stay on top. But when squeezed into a tight ball, all the air is pressed out and the foil becomes a small, dense lump -- so it sinks.

> This is exactly how **boats and ships** are designed. They are given a wide, hollow shape that holds a lot of air inside. Even though the metal is heavy, the trapped air keeps the ship afloat!

## Making Boats from Different Materials

Try building your own boats at home using everyday items:

| Material | Type of Boat |
|---|---|
| **Newspaper** | Classic folded paper boat |
| **Ice-cream sticks** | Tie sticks together to make a small raft |
| **Coconut shell** | Scoop out the inside to make a shell boat |
| **Walnut shells** | Use half a walnut as a tiny boat |
| **Clay** | Shape into a hollow boat and let it dry |
| **Thermocol** | Cut and shape into a sturdy float |

When designing your boat, think about:
- **Balance** -- Will it stay upright or tip over sideways?
- **Strength** -- Can it hold together when placed in water?
- **Shape** -- Is the bottom wide and hollow enough to trap air?

| Floats | Sinks |
|---|---|
| Wax candle | Glass marble |
| Cork | Metal coin |
| Dry leaf | Rubber eraser |
| Thermocol | Potato |
| Pumpkin | Steel spoon |

> **Surprising fact:** A lemon with its peel on **floats** in water. But peel off the skin and it **sinks**! The secret? The lemon peel is full of tiny air pockets that act like a life jacket, keeping the lemon afloat.

## Key Points to Remember

- Objects spin best when they are **well-balanced** and the spinning point is at the **centre**.
- A **square spinner** appears circular when it spins at high speed.
- The **charkha** and **lattu** are traditional Indian examples of spinning objects.
- Whether an object floats or sinks depends on both its **weight** and its **shape**.
- By changing the **shape** of a material (like aluminium foil), you can make the same thing float or sink.
- Ships and boats use a **wide, hollow design** that traps air, allowing them to float despite being heavy.
- Experimenting with everyday objects helps us discover **how things work** all around us.
