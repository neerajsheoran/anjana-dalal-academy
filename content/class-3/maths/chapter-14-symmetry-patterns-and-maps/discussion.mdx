## What is Symmetry?

**Child:** Maa, teacher said a butterfly is symmetrical. What does that mean?

**Mother:** Look at a butterfly carefully. Its left wing looks exactly like its right wing, just flipped. If you drew a line down the middle of the butterfly, both sides would match perfectly. That is **symmetry**.

**Child:** Like when I fold a paper in half and both sides are the same?

**Mother:** Exactly! When you fold along the **line of symmetry** and both halves sit perfectly on top of each other, the shape is symmetrical.

**Child:** Is my face symmetrical, Maa?

**Mother:** Almost! Your left eye matches your right eye, your left ear matches your right ear. Faces are not perfectly symmetrical, but they are very close. That is why a painter can draw half your face and use a mirror to show the other half!

**Child:** What about the letter A? Is it symmetrical?

**Mother:** Good thinking! Yes, capital A is symmetrical. Draw a line down the middle -- the left half mirrors the right half. But the letter B is different -- it is symmetrical with a horizontal line, not a vertical one.

## Symmetry in Rangolis and Bead Chains

**Child:** Maa, during Diwali we made a rangoli. Was it symmetrical?

**Mother:** The one we made last year was! Remember how we drew one half first and then copied the same design on the other side? That made it symmetrical. Rangoli designs from all over India -- Kolam from Tamil Nadu, Pookalam from Kerala -- are often symmetrical.

**Child:** At school we also made bead chains. Teacher said some are symmetrical and some are not. How do I check?

**Mother:** Easy! Look at the chain from the middle bead. Does the pattern on the left side match the pattern on the right? If yes, it is symmetrical.

**Child:** So if my chain goes Red, Blue, Blue, Red, Red, Blue, Blue, Red -- that is symmetrical?

**Mother:** Let us check! The left half is Red, Blue, Blue, Red. The right half is Red, Blue, Blue, Red. They are the same! So yes, your chain is symmetrical. Well done!

## Tiling, Maps, and Directions

**Child:** Maa, why are floors always covered with square or rectangular tiles? Why not round tiles?

**Mother:** Try this experiment. Take a few round bangles and place them next to each other on a table. Can you cover the whole table with them?

**Child:** No! There are gaps between them because they are round!

**Mother:** Exactly! Round shapes always leave **gaps**. But squares and rectangles fit together perfectly with **no gaps and no overlaps**. That is why floors, walls, and pathways all use square or rectangular tiles.

**Child:** What about triangles?

**Mother:** Triangles can work too! Two triangles placed together make a rectangle. So you can tile a floor with triangles as well.

**Child:** Maa, at the fair last time, we used a map to find the food court. How does a map work?

**Mother:** A map shows a place from above, like a bird looking down. Everything is drawn smaller but in the right position. Roads become lines, buildings become boxes. And you follow **directions** -- straight, right, left, back -- to navigate.

**Child:** Like a treasure map in pirate stories!

**Mother:** Ha ha, yes! But instead of treasure, you find samosas and chaat at the food court!
