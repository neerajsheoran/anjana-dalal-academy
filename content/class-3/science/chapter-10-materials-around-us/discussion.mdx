## Noticing Things Around Us

**Child:** Maa, today our teacher asked us to look around the classroom and name the materials. I found wood, metal, glass, plastic, and cloth -- all in one room!

**Mother:** That is wonderful, beta! Everything around us is made of some material. Look around our kitchen -- what materials do you see?

**Child:** The tawa is metal, the belan is wood, the dabba is plastic, the glass is... glass!

**Mother:** Very good! And the towel hanging on the hook?

**Child:** That is cloth! Maa, is everything in the whole world made of just a few materials?

**Mother:** Most things are, yes! Wood, metal, glass, plastic, cloth, clay, and rubber -- these are the main ones. Sometimes objects use more than one material too. Look at your pencil.

**Child:** Oh! The body is wood, the lead inside is graphite, and the eraser at the top is rubber. One pencil, three materials!

## What Are Objects Made Of?

**Child:** Maa, where does glass come from? It looks so smooth and see-through. It cannot grow on a tree!

**Mother:** You will be surprised, beta. Glass is made from **sand**!

**Child:** Sand?! The same sand from the beach?

**Mother:** Yes! When sand is heated to a very, very high temperature -- over 1700 degrees -- it melts and becomes a thick liquid. This liquid is shaped into windows, bottles, and spectacles, and when it cools down, it becomes the hard, transparent glass we know.

**Child:** That is amazing! So our window was once sand from a beach?

**Mother:** In a way, yes! And wood comes from trees, metal comes from special rocks called ore deep inside the earth, and cloth comes from cotton plants, sheep wool, or even silkworms.

**Child:** Silkworms make silk? I thought silk was some special cloth from a shop!

**Mother:** Ha ha, no! Tiny silkworms spin silk threads to make their cocoon. People carefully unwind these threads and weave them into beautiful silk sarees and fabrics.

## Can You See Through It?

**Child:** Maa, why can I see the garden through the window glass but not through the wall?

**Mother:** Because glass is **transparent** -- it lets light pass through completely, so you can see clearly. The wall is **opaque** -- it blocks all light, so you cannot see through it at all.

**Child:** What about the bathroom glass? I can see some light through it but everything looks blurry.

**Mother:** That is called **translucent**! It lets some light through, but not enough to see things clearly. Butter paper and thin oiled paper are also translucent.

**Child:** So there are three types -- transparent, translucent, and opaque?

**Mother:** Exactly right, beta! Try classifying objects at home. Is your lunch box transparent or opaque?

**Child:** Opaque! I cannot see my paratha without opening it.

**Mother:** And your water bottle?

**Child:** Transparent! I can see exactly how much water is left!

## How Materials Feel and Behave

**Child:** Maa, why is the marble floor so smooth but the doormat is rough?

**Mother:** Because they are made of different materials with different **properties**. Marble is polished stone -- smooth and hard. The doormat is made of coir -- rough and scratchy. These properties make them useful for different things.

**Child:** I would not want a doormat made of marble -- that would be slippery!

**Mother:** Exactly! And you would not want a floor made of coir -- it would be uncomfortable to walk on. We use the **right material for the right purpose**. Think about why we wear cotton clothes in summer.

**Child:** Because cotton is soft and lets air pass through. Wearing a rubber shirt would be terrible -- so hot and sticky!

**Mother:** Ha ha, yes! And imagine sitting on a chair made of cloth -- you would sink right through. Chairs need a hard, rigid material like wood or metal.

**Child:** So every material has a job it is good at!

**Mother:** Perfectly said, beta.

## Solids, Liquids, and Gases

**Child:** Maa, our teacher taught us about solids, liquids, and gases today. She said everything is one of these three. But I am confused about some things.

**Mother:** Tell me what is confusing you.

**Child:** Is sand a liquid? It flows when I pour it from one hand to another!

**Mother:** Good question! Sand **looks** like it flows, but pick up one tiny grain. Does it change shape?

**Child:** No, each grain keeps its shape.

**Mother:** So each grain is a tiny **solid**. Sand flows because the grains slide over each other, not because they change shape like water does. Water actually changes its shape to match the container.

**Child:** Oh! And what about butter? It is soft and squishy. Is it a liquid?

**Mother:** Put a piece of butter on a plate. Does it flatten out and cover the plate like water would?

**Child:** No, it sits there in its shape.

**Mother:** Then it is a solid -- just a very soft one! Liquids flow and take the shape of their container. Solids keep their own shape, even if they are soft.

**Child:** Now I get it! And gases are like air -- they spread everywhere?

**Mother:** Yes! Open a packet of chips in one corner. Soon the smell reaches every part of the room. That is because the gas (the smell) spreads out in all directions.

## Things from Nature and Things We Make

**Child:** Maa, is a wooden table natural or man-made?

**Mother:** Think about it, beta. Did the table grow in a forest?

**Child:** No! Someone cut a tree and made the table.

**Mother:** Right. The **tree** is natural -- it grew in nature by itself. But the **table** is man-made -- a carpenter shaped the wood into a table. Every man-made thing starts from something natural.

**Child:** So a cotton shirt was once a cotton plant?

**Mother:** Yes! And a glass bottle was once sand, and a steel spoon was once a rock called iron ore deep inside the earth. Humans take natural things and shape them into useful objects.

**Child:** That means natural things can be alive or not alive. A tree is alive, but a rock is not.

**Mother:** Exactly! Trees, birds, and fish are living natural things. Rocks, water, and air are non-living natural things. You have understood it perfectly, beta!
